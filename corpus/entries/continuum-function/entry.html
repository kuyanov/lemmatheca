Two ways to climb through infinite cardinal sizes

We assume AC and identify cardinals with initial ordinals. All powers in this entry are cardinal powers, counting functions. Extend the aleph sequence to all ordinal indices by

\[ \aleph_0=\omega,\qquad\aleph_{\alpha+1}=\aleph_\alpha^+,\qquad\aleph_\lambda=\sup_{\alpha<\lambda}\aleph_\alpha\quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit}). \]

The beth sequence instead takes power sets at successor steps:

\[ \beth_0=\aleph_0,\qquad\beth_{\alpha+1}=2^{\beth_\alpha},\qquad\beth_\lambda=\sup_{\alpha<\lambda}\beth_\alpha\quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit}). \]

The successor cardinal exists, power-set sizes exist, and suprema of sets of cardinals are cardinals. Each rule therefore specifies a unique cardinal from earlier values. Set-valued transfinite recursion defines the two class sequences; one can extend each rule to arbitrary histories by returning zero when a value is not an infinite cardinal. Induction ensures that this fallback never occurs. No set of all cardinals is used.

Both sequences are strictly increasing and preserve nonzero limit suprema. Transfinite induction gives \(\aleph_\alpha\le\beth_\alpha\): at successors, Cantor's theorem puts \(2^{\beth_\alpha}\) above \(\beth_\alpha\ge\aleph_\alpha\), hence at least at \(\aleph_\alpha^+\); limits follow by taking suprema. In particular, \(\beth_1=2^{\aleph_0}=\mathfrak c\).

Every infinite cardinal occurs in the aleph sequence. To see this for a given \(\kappa\), consider the set of infinite initial ordinals at most \(\kappa\), ordered by ordinal comparison. It is a well-order beginning at \(\omega\). Its consecutive elements are cardinal successors, and any member with no immediate predecessor is the supremum of its predecessors, since otherwise their cardinal supremum would be a missing intermediate member. Its enumeration therefore follows exactly the aleph recursion and reaches \(\kappa\).

GCH asks every power set to have the next cardinal size

The continuum function sends each infinite cardinal \(\kappa\) to \(2^\kappa\). The generalized continuum hypothesis, abbreviated GCH, asserts

\[ 2^\kappa=\kappa^+\qquad\text{for every infinite cardinal }\kappa. \]

Its instance at \(\aleph_0\) is the continuum hypothesis. GCH is an additional assumption in the calculations below.

GCH holds exactly when \(\beth_\alpha=\aleph_\alpha\) at every ordinal index. Under GCH their recursion rules agree, proving equality by induction. Conversely, equality at \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha+1\) gives \(2^{\aleph_\alpha}=\aleph_{\alpha+1}\), and every infinite cardinal is an aleph.

A sufficiently large exponent determines the power

For infinite \(\lambda\) and \(2\le\kappa\le2^\lambda\), \(\kappa^\lambda=2^\lambda\).

Proof

Injections of the value sets give \(2^\lambda\le\kappa^\lambda\le(2^\lambda)^\lambda\). By the power laws and the infinite square theorem, the upper bound equals \(2^{\lambda\cdot\lambda}=2^\lambda\). Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein gives equality. □

In particular this applies whenever \(2\le\kappa\le\lambda\), since \(\lambda<2^\lambda\).

A successor base obeys Hausdorff's formula

For infinite cardinals \(\kappa,\lambda\), without assuming GCH,

\[ (\kappa^+)^\lambda=\kappa^\lambda\cdot\kappa^+. \]

Thus \(\aleph_{\alpha+1}^{\aleph_\beta}=\aleph_\alpha^{\aleph_\beta}\cdot\aleph_{\alpha+1}\) for ordinal indices \(\alpha,\beta\). Multiplication here is cardinal multiplication.

Proof

If \(\lambda\ge\kappa^+\), makes both powers equal to \(2^\lambda\). Multiplying this infinite cardinal by \(\kappa^+\le2^\lambda\) leaves it unchanged.

If \(\lambda<\kappa^+\), regularity of the successor cardinal bounds the range of every \(f:\lambda\to\kappa^+\) inside some \(\gamma<\kappa^+\). Each such \(\gamma\) has size at most \(\kappa\), so there are at most \(\kappa^\lambda\) functions into it. Counting the union over \(\kappa^+\) possible bounds gives \((\kappa^+)^\lambda\le\kappa^+\cdot\kappa^\lambda\), using indexed bounds. In the other direction, functions into \(\kappa\) embed among these functions, and constant functions supply at least \(\kappa^+\) points. Their maximum equals their product by infinite cardinal multiplication, giving the reverse inequality. □

Infinite exponents are part of the statement. At exponent zero, the power is one, while the displayed product would be \(\kappa^+\).

Four cases reduce general infinite powers

For infinite cardinals \(\kappa,\lambda\), the Hausdorff–Tarski reduction gives the following exhaustive cases, without assuming GCH:

  1. If \(\kappa\le\lambda\), then \(\kappa^\lambda=2^\lambda\).
  2. If \(\kappa>\lambda\) and some cardinal \(\mu<\kappa\) satisfies \(\mu^\lambda\ge\kappa\), then \(\kappa^\lambda=\mu^\lambda\) for any such \(\mu\).
  3. If \(\mu^\lambda<\kappa\) for every cardinal \(\mu<\kappa\), and \(\lambda<\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\), then \(\kappa^\lambda=\kappa\).
  4. If \(\mu^\lambda<\kappa\) for every cardinal \(\mu<\kappa\), and \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\le\lambda\), then \(\kappa^\lambda=\kappa^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}\).

Proof

Case 1 is . In case 2, monotonicity and the power laws give

\[ \mu^\lambda\le\kappa^\lambda\le(\mu^\lambda)^\lambda=\mu^{\lambda\cdot\lambda}=\mu^\lambda. \]

In the remaining cases, \(2^\lambda<\kappa\), so \(\lambda<\kappa\). In case 3, cofinality bounds place every function \(\lambda\to\kappa\) in some \(\gamma<\kappa\). There are \(|\gamma|^\lambda<\kappa\) functions for each bound. The union over \(\kappa\) bounds has size at most \(\kappa\cdot\kappa=\kappa\), by indexed bounds. Constant functions give equality.

For case 4 put \(\theta=\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\), and take positive ordinals \(\gamma_i<\kappa\), for \(i<\theta\), cofinal in \(\kappa\). Encode \(f:\lambda\to\kappa\) by the functions

\[ f_i(x)=\begin{cases}f(x),&f(x)<\gamma_i,\\0,&f(x)\ge\gamma_i.\end{cases} \]

Each \(f_i:\lambda\to\gamma_i\) is well-defined because \(0<\gamma_i\). The code is injective: if two functions differ at \(x\), some \(\gamma_i\) exceeds both values and preserves that difference. Each coordinate has \(|\gamma_i|^\lambda<\kappa\) possibilities. Hence

\[ \kappa^\lambda\le\prod_{i<\theta}|\gamma_i|^\lambda\le\kappa^\theta\le\kappa^\lambda, \]

where the last inequality uses \(\theta\le\lambda\). □

The last case has \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\le\lambda<\kappa\), so its base is singular. Repeated smaller-base reductions cannot descend forever: cardinals are ordinals and are well-ordered. Thus the unresolved values reduce to power-set sizes and cofinal powers of singular cardinals. The theorem reduces the problem; it does not assign these values.

GCH determines powers using cofinality

Assume GCH. For infinite cardinals \(\kappa,\lambda\),

\[ \kappa^\lambda=\begin{cases} \kappa,&\lambda<\operatorname{cf}(\kappa),\\ \kappa^+,&\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\le\lambda<\kappa,\\ \lambda^+,&\kappa\le\lambda. \end{cases} \]

Proof

If \(\kappa\le\lambda\), gives \(\kappa^\lambda=2^\lambda=\lambda^+\). Now assume \(\lambda<\kappa\). Every function \(\lambda\to\kappa\) has a graph in \(\mathcal P(\lambda\times\kappa)\), so the infinite product theorem and GCH give \(\kappa^\lambda\le2^\kappa=\kappa^+\).

If also \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\le\lambda\), König's consequence gives \(\kappa<\kappa^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}\le\kappa^\lambda\). The upper bound \(\kappa^+\) therefore forces equality with \(\kappa^+\).

If instead \(\lambda<\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\), each function has bounded range in \(\kappa\). For each bound \(\gamma<\kappa\), put \(\theta=\max(\lambda,|\gamma|)\), an infinite cardinal below \(\kappa\). Graph coding bounds the number of functions \(\lambda\to\gamma\) by \(2^\theta=\theta^+\le\kappa\). Taking the union over \(\kappa\) bounds gives at most \(\kappa\cdot\kappa=\kappa\) functions. Constant functions give the opposite bound. □

For finite nonzero exponents, an infinite base remains unchanged by the finite-power theorem. Exponent zero and bases zero or one follow the empty-function conventions.

SCH specifies the smallest possible cofinal power of a singular cardinal

An infinite cardinal \(\kappa\) is a strong limit if \(2^\mu<\kappa\) for every cardinal \(\mu<\kappa\). The singular cardinals hypothesis, abbreviated SCH, asserts that for every singular cardinal \(\kappa\),

\[ 2^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}<\kappa\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\kappa^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}=\kappa^+. \]

König's theorem already forces this power above \(\kappa\); SCH requires it to be the very next cardinal when its displayed hypothesis holds. GCH implies SCH by . Here SCH is stated as a principle, not proved from AC.

For a singular strong limit \(\kappa\), write \(\mu=\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\) and choose a cofinal sequence \((\kappa_i)_{i<\mu}\) of ordinals below \(\kappa\). A subset of \(\kappa\) is determined by its intersections with these initial segments, giving

\[ 2^\kappa\le\prod_{i<\mu}2^{|\kappa_i|}\le\kappa^\mu\le(2^\kappa)^\mu=2^\kappa. \]

The first inequality uses that the cofinal initial segments cover \(\kappa\); the second uses the strong-limit condition; the last equality uses the power laws and \(\mu\le\kappa\). Thus \(2^\kappa=\kappa^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}\), and SCH entails \(2^\kappa=\kappa^+\) at such a cardinal. This is a consequence under SCH, not a proof of SCH.

Known bounds constrain the continuum function without determining it

In ZFC the continuum function is nondecreasing, satisfies \(2^\kappa>\kappa\), and satisfies \(\operatorname{cf}(2^\kappa)>\kappa\).

Proof

For \(\kappa\le\lambda\), extending a binary function by zero injects the functions on \(\kappa\) into those on \(\lambda\), giving \(2^\kappa\le2^\lambda\). Strict growth is Cantor's theorem, and the cofinality bound is the consequence of König's inequality. □

These are necessary conditions. Nothing proved here says that every assignment satisfying them can occur. Such realization and independence assertions require models of set theory and forcing; they cannot be inferred from the inequalities alone. At singular strong limits the additional relation to cofinal powers in also applies.
