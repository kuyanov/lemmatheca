An induction axiom applies to a formula and all its parameters

The induction schema of Peano arithmetic has one axiom for each arithmetic formula \(A(x,\bar u)\). Its parameters \(\bar u\) remain free until we close the axiom universally. Consequently the axiom applies in every PA-model with any fixed values of those parameters. The induction variable ranges over all of the model, including its nonstandard elements.

\[A(0,\bar u)\land\forall x\,(A(x,\bar u)\to A(Sx,\bar u))\;\longrightarrow\;\forall x\,A(x,\bar u).\]

This does not give induction for every external subset of the model. For example, the set of standard elements of a nonstandard model contains zero and is closed under successor, but it is a proper external subset. If one applied induction to that subset, one would wrongly conclude that the model had no nonstandard elements. Each induction below therefore specifies an arithmetic assertion, with a fixed finite tuple of parameters.

We will sometimes prove an arithmetic sentence by working in an arbitrary model of PA. After proving that the sentence holds in every such model, first-order completeness supplies a PA derivation in the shared proof calculus. This is a semantic route to a derivation, rather than an algorithm that prints that derivation. It uses neither truth only in the standard natural numbers nor induction for arbitrary subsets. When we write “PA proves”, this passage back to an actual derivation is included.

We use the arithmetic abbreviations \(u\le v\) for \(\exists w\,(w+u=v)\) and \(u\lt v\) for \(Su\le v\), introduced with bounded formulas. A function notation introduced by a proved unique graph is an abbreviation: its occurrence can always be replaced by a value variable and that graph assertion.

Internal induction also proves least-element principles

PA proves induction for every arithmetic formula with parameters. Consequently, if an arithmetic formula \(A(x,\bar u)\) has a witness, it has a least witness:

\[\mathrm{PA}\vdash\forall\bar u\,\bigl(\exists x\,A(x,\bar u)\to\exists x\,(A(x,\bar u)\land\forall z\lt x\,\neg A(z,\bar u))\bigr).\]

Proof

Induct on \(n\) in the formula saying that either every \(x\le n\) fails \(A\), or there is a least witness at most \(n\). At zero, test \(A(0,\bar u)\). In the step, retain an already found least witness; otherwise test \(A(Sn,\bar u)\). If some witness exists, apply the resulting assertion at that witness. The parameters remain fixed in this induction. □

PA also proves the discrete linear-order and semiring laws used below. Associativity, commutativity and distributivity follow by induction from the recursive addition and multiplication axioms. Additive cancellation needs induction too: induct on the common summand in \(a+b=a+c\Rightarrow b=c\). At \(a=0\), use the zero-on-the-left law; at \(Sa\), rewrite both sides as successors, apply successor injectivity, and use the induction hypothesis. Injectivity alone would not supply cancellation.

Induction on \(v\), using the predecessor axiom, proves \(u\le v\) or \(v\lt u\). Cancellation and the successor axioms then give antisymmetry. All these are inductions on arithmetic formulas, with their other variables as parameters.

A least counterexample justifies recursion on smaller codes

For each fixed arithmetic formula \(P(x,\bar u)\), PA proves strong induction: if \(P(x,\bar u)\) follows from its truth at every \(y\lt x\), then it holds for every \(x\).

Proof

Fix the parameters and suppose a counterexample exists. Apply the least-witness principle to \(\neg P\). Its least witness \(c\) has \(P(y,\bar u)\) for every \(y\lt c\). The assumed induction step gives \(P(c,\bar u)\), contradicting the choice of \(c\). This proof is first-order: the formula \(\neg P\) names the counterexamples. □

This is the form of induction needed when a syntax routine calls itself on the children of an encoded expression. The input decreases numerically; it need not be the immediate predecessor. A bounded loop, a maximum or a graph-defined function inside \(P\) must first be written using its arithmetic graph, so that \(P\) really is an arithmetic formula.

Division with remainder is an arithmetic induction

PA proves that, for every \(m\ne0\) and every \(c\), there are unique \(q,r\) with \(c=mq+r\) and \(r\lt m\).

Proof

Induct on \(c\). The representation of zero uses \(q=r=0\). From \(c=mq+r\), retain \(q\) and increase \(r\) when \(Sr\lt m\); when \(Sr=m\), use \(Sq\) and zero. These cases exhaust \(r\lt m\) by the discrete order laws.

For uniqueness, if \(q\lt q'\), then \(mq+r\lt m(q+1)\le mq'\le mq'+r'\), contradicting equality of the two representations. The reversed inequality is impossible in the same way, so \(q=q'\). Additive cancellation then gives equality of the remainders. □

Bézout coefficients and two coprime congruences

Write \(a\mid b\) for \(\exists q\,(aq=b)\). Positive \(m,n\) are coprime when every common divisor is \(1\). PA proves the signed-coefficient Bézout identity

\[\exists u\exists v\exists u'\exists v'\;(mu+nv=1+mu'+nv').\]

It follows internally that coprime \(r,n\) satisfy \(r\mid nt\Rightarrow r\mid t\), and that positive coprime moduli admit simultaneous prescribed remainders \(a\lt m,b\lt n\).

Proof

The arithmetic language has no subtraction on arbitrary numbers. Describe a positive difference by \(z\ne0\) and \(mu+nv=z+(mu'+nv')\). Such a difference exists: take \(z=m\). The least-witness theorem supplies a least positive difference \(z\). Divide \(m\) by \(z\). If its remainder is positive, substituting the displayed expression for the multiple of \(z\) and moving terms across the equality writes that remainder as a smaller positive difference of combinations of \(m,n\), a contradiction. The same reasoning applies to \(n\). Thus \(z\) divides both, and coprimality gives \(z=1\).

For cancellation, multiply the Bézout equality for \(r,n\) by \(t\). Since \(r\mid nt\), all terms except \(t\) are multiples of \(r\). The division theorem, or cancellation of a quotient difference, then gives \(r\mid t\). Multiplying two Bézout identities similarly shows that coprimality with each of two factors implies coprimality with their product.

For simultaneous residues, the Bézout coefficients provide a signed solution to the two congruences by the two-modulus calculation. Add a sufficiently large multiple of \(mn\) to obtain a nonnegative solution, and divide to choose its remainder representative. Every difference used in this argument is expressed by an equality of nonnegative sums. Its least element and quotient therefore come from arithmetic formulas, so the same argument works at internal moduli. □

The remainder decoder has a unique value at every internal input

Retain the existing bounded graph

\[B(c,d,i,z)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;z\lt (i+1)d+1\ \land\ \exists q\le c\;(c=((i+1)d+1)q+z).\]

PA proves \(\forall c\,\forall d\,\forall i\,\exists!z\,B(c,d,i,z)\). We may therefore write \(\beta(c,d,i)\) as a graph abbreviation, not as a new symbol of \(L_A\).

Proof

The modulus \((i+1)d+1\) is positive even when \(d=0\). Apply the division lemma. Its quotient is at most \(c\), since a positive multiple of the quotient plus a nonnegative remainder is \(c\). The division uniqueness proof gives the required uniqueness. □

One large common multiple supplies many coprime moduli

PA proves one uniform sentence:

\[\forall N\exists D\;(D\ne0\land\forall j\le N\,(j\ne0\to j\mid D)).\]

For such a positive common multiple \(d'\) through \(n+1\), the moduli \(m_i=1+(i+1)d'\), for \(i\le n\), are pairwise coprime.

Proof

For the first assertion, induct on \(N\): use \(1\) at zero, and replace a suitable \(D\) by \(D(N+1)\) in the step. Multiplication preserves the preceding divisibilities. This proves the single sentence at all internal \(N\); no factorial function has been assumed.

For the second assertion, take \(i\lt j\le n\) and a common divisor \(r\) of \(m_i,m_j\). A common divisor of \(r,d'\) divides \(m_i\) and \((i+1)d'\), hence divides \(1\); thus \(r,d'\) are coprime. The equations for the two moduli show \(r\mid(j-i)d'\). Coprime cancellation gives \(r\mid j-i\). The positive difference \(j-i\le n+1\) divides \(d'\), so \(r\mid d'\). As \(r\mid m_i\) too, \(r\mid1\), giving \(r=1\). The differences are abbreviations for the unique additive gaps supplied by the arithmetic order. □

Arithmetic can append one more value to a coded history

PA proves the following sentence, with every function occurrence understood through its graph:

\[\forall c\,\forall d\,\forall n\,\forall a\,\exists c'\exists d'\;\bigl(\beta(c',d',n)=a\land\forall i\lt n\;\beta(c',d',i)=\beta(c,d,i)\bigr).\]

This is an assertion about arbitrary internal codes and lengths. It does not quantify over an external list of length \(n\).

Proof

Use the common-multiple lemma at \(N=n+1\). Multiply its positive \(D\) by \(c+a+2\), obtaining a common multiple \(d'\gt c,a\). The same lemma makes the moduli \(m_i=1+(i+1)d'\), for \(i\le n\), pairwise coprime.

Prescribe the remainder \(\beta(c,d,i)\) when \(i\lt n\), and \(a\) when \(i=n\). Each is less than \(d'\), hence less than its new modulus: a remainder of \(c\) is at most \(c\). PA can solve all these congruences without assuming that an arbitrary internal list has already been coded. Induct on \(k\le n+1\) in the following arithmetic assertion: there exist a simultaneous solution \(s\) for the indices below \(k\), and a positive modulus \(p\), such that every earlier \(m_i\) divides \(p\), and \(p\) is coprime to every remaining \(m_j\), \(k\le j\le n\).

At \(k=0\), take \(s=0,p=1\). For the step, solve the two congruences modulo \(p\) and \(m_k\) using the two-modulus theorem, prescribing the old residue \(s\bmod p\) and the new required residue. Replace \(p\) by \(pm_k\). Earlier residues are preserved because their moduli divide \(p\); coprimality with all remaining moduli follows from pairwise coprimality and the product rule. At \(k=n+1\) let \(c'=s\). The congruences and remainder uniqueness give precisely the displayed assertion. Both inductions have arithmetic formulas with the original parameters fixed. □

A bounded family of witnesses can be recorded together

For every fixed arithmetic formula \(A(i,u,\bar x)\), PA proves

\[\forall i\lt n\,\exists u\,A(i,u,\bar x)\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists c\exists d\,\forall i\lt n\,A(i,\beta(c,d,i),\bar x).\]

The displayed implication is universally closed over \(n,\bar x\). It is a schema, one theorem for each fixed formula \(A\).

Proof

Induct on \(k\le n\) in the assertion that codes exist for witnesses at all indices below \(k\). At zero any codes work. At the step choose a witness \(u\) for \(A(k,u,\bar x)\), and apply the history-extension theorem to append it. Earlier values are unchanged. The assertion at \(k=n\) is the conclusion. For a fixed tuple of witnesses, repeat this construction once for each coordinate. □

PA also proves a common bound for a bounded family: append the witnesses and use the fact that every decoded value is at most the first code parameter. This is why a bounded universal quantifier over existential assertions can be converted to one existential choice of codes followed by bounded tests.

Why is coding each ordinary finite list insufficient?

Suppose that for each ordinary \(n\) one can encode every ordinary list of length \(n\). Does that statement alone prove, in an arbitrary PA-model, that an internally coded history of length \(n\) can be extended?

Show answerHide answer

No. It speaks only about standard lengths and external finite lists. An internal length may be nonstandard. The extension theorem instead begins with numbers \(c,d,n,a\) inside the model and constructs numbers \(c',d'\) there. Its congruence invariant is an arithmetic formula, so PA induction applies at every internal stage. No external list with one entry for each internal index is assumed. The empty preserved segment at \(n=0\) is included: only the newly prescribed entry is required.
