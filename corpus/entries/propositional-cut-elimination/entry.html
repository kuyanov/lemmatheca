Use a proved formula as an intermediate lemma

Use the signed NNF formulas, duality and finite-set contexts of the one-sided calculus. Write \(\mathsf C\vdash\Gamma\) for a finite tree using its five rules. Thus \(\Gamma,A\) means \(\Gamma\cup\{A\}\), and repetition in a context has no effect.

The cut rule is the additional inference

\[ \frac{\vdash\Gamma,A\qquad\vdash\Delta,A^\perp}{\vdash\Gamma\cup\Delta}\;\mathrm{cut}(A). \]

Its displayed formula \(A\) is the cut formula. A derivation with cut is a finite tree generated by the five original rules and this additional rule; write \(\mathsf C_{\mathrm{cut}}\vdash\Gamma\) when one exists. A cut-free derivation uses only the original five rules. These are syntactic definitions: a derivation consists of rule applications, rather than a semantic assertion about valuations.

Cut composes two arguments through opposite occurrences of the same intermediate formula. The formula may be absent from the concluding context. The problem is to replace that composition by a finite tree using only the original rules.

Separate formula complexity from proof size

The formula size \(|A|\) counts all nodes of its expression tree. Signed atoms and constants have size one, and

\[ |B\wedge C|=|B\vee C|=|B|+|C|+1. \]

The proof size of a finite derivation counts its rule applications, including leaves. A leaf has size one; a unary inference adds one to its premise size, and a binary inference adds one to the sum of its premise sizes. Every proper premise tree is therefore strictly smaller than the tree containing it.

We use two separate finite recursions. Preparatory transformations recurse on proper premise trees. The main cut construction recurses on the formula size, permitting any cut-free input proofs for a smaller formula. It therefore remains applicable even if a preparatory transformation produces a larger proof.

Erase the principal formula, including an old copy

For a finite context \(\Xi\), the erasure \(\Xi\setminus\{D\}\) removes \(D\) if it is present. A useful proof transformation must work even when the context used in an inference already contained its principal formula. Merely reading off the premise of the last rule would then leave that old copy behind.

In the transformations below, a nonprincipal last inference is repeated after recursively transforming its premise trees. If erasure removed one of that inference's displayed children, weakening puts the child back before the inference is repeated. The contexts required for the repeated inference are enlarged to a common context by the same weakening operation. All additions are explicit finite unions.

Falsity can be removed from a cut-free sequent

Every cut-free derivation of \(\Xi\) can be transformed into a cut-free derivation of \(\Xi\setminus\{\bot\}\). In particular, a derivation of \(\Gamma,\bot\) supplies one of \(\Gamma\).

Induct on the given finite tree. At an atomic-identity leaf, neither of its two signed atoms is \(\bot\), so both survive erasure. At a truth leaf, \(\top\) survives. For a conjunction or disjunction inference, transform its premises by induction and repeat its last rule. Its principal compound and its displayed children are different from \(\bot\) except that a child may itself be \(\bot\); in that case weaken the transformed premise by that child before repeating the rule. The repeated conclusion is precisely the erased original context.

If the last rule introduces \(\bot\), apply the induction hypothesis directly to its premise. This removes any old occurrence of \(\bot\) there too, and its conclusion is the required erased context. These recursive calls use only proper premise trees. Finally \((\Gamma\cup\{\bot\})\setminus\{\bot\}\subseteq\Gamma\), so weakening gives the stated consequence.

Replace a conjunction by either component

For every cut-free derivation of \(\Xi\) and formulas \(B,C\), there are cut-free derivations of both

\[ (\Xi\setminus\{B\wedge C\}),B\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\Xi\setminus\{B\wedge C\}),C. \]

Consequently a derivation of \(\Gamma,B\wedge C\) supplies derivations of \(\Gamma,B\) and \(\Gamma,C\).

We construct the first derivation by induction on the supplied proof, with \(B,C\) fixed. An atomic-identity or truth leaf survives erasing the compound \(D=B\wedge C\); then weaken by \(B\). If the last principal formula differs from \(D\), recursively transform every premise and repeat the same last inference. Use the erasure convention to reinsert a displayed child equal to \(D\), if necessary, and to put the new alternative \(B\) in every common context. Since the last principal is not erased, the resulting conclusion is exactly \((\Xi\setminus\{D\}),B\). This covers disjunction and bottom rules as well as a different conjunction.

Suppose instead that the last inference introduces \(D\), with common context \(\Theta\). Its left premise proves \(\Theta,B\). Apply the induction hypothesis to that proper premise tree, obtaining \(((\Theta\cup\{B\})\setminus\{D\}),B\). Since the proper subformula \(B\) is different from \(D\), this context is \((\Theta\setminus\{D\}),B\), exactly the required erasure of the conclusion. This extra recursive call is what removes an old copy of \(D\) already in \(\Theta\).

The second construction uses the right premise and inserts \(C\) instead. Both recursions terminate on proper premise trees. In the displayed consequence, erasing the inserted conjunction leaves a subset of \(\Gamma\); weaken the constructed proofs to the stated contexts.

Replace a disjunction by both alternatives

A cut-free derivation of \(\Xi\) can be transformed, for any \(B,C\), into one of

\[ (\Xi\setminus\{B\vee C\}),B,C. \]

Thus a derivation of \(\Gamma,B\vee C\) supplies one of \(\Gamma,B,C\).

Induct on the proof with \(D=B\vee C\) fixed. Atomic-identity and truth leaves survive erasing \(D\), and weakening adds \(B,C\). If the last principal is different from \(D\), transform its premises recursively, reinsert any erased displayed child, and repeat its rule with \(B,C\) included in the context. The retained last principal gives the required conclusion.

If the last rule introduces \(D\), its premise proves \(\Theta,B,C\), where the conclusion is \(\Theta,D\). Apply the induction hypothesis to that premise tree. Both \(B,C\) are proper subformulas of \(D\), hence different from it. Its transformed conclusion is therefore \((\Theta\setminus\{D\}),B,C\), removing an old occurrence in \(\Theta\) as well. Again every recursive call is on a proper premise tree; weakening gives the final consequence.

Replace one positive literal by a proof of its opposite

Fix an atom \(p\), a finite context \(\Delta\), and a cut-free derivation \(Q\) of \(\Delta,\bar p\). For every cut-free derivation \(P\) of \(\Xi\), there is a cut-free derivation of

\[ (\Xi\setminus\{p\})\cup\Delta. \]

In particular, cut on the positive literal \(p\) is admissible.

Recurse on \(P\), keeping \(Q\) fixed. If its last rule is atomic identity on an atom \(q\ne p\), the two opposite literals on \(q\) survive erasing the positive literal \(p\); use the same leaf and weaken by \(\Delta\). If it is identity on \(p\), the negative literal \(\bar p\) survives in \(\Xi\setminus\{p\}\). Thus the supplied tree \(Q\) can be weakened directly into the target context. This is the only case that substitutes a copy of \(Q\) for an identity leaf. A truth leaf remains a truth leaf.

A logical or bottom inference cannot have the positive literal \(p\) as principal. Transform its proper premise trees recursively, add back a displayed child if it was \(p\), enlarge the common context by \(\Delta\), and repeat its last rule. This gives exactly the target context because its principal formula was not erased. These are all cases, so the recursive construction terminates. For an input proving \(\Gamma,p\), its erased context is contained in \(\Gamma\); weaken the output to \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\).

Compose two cut-free arguments without cut

For every NNF formula \(A\) and finite contexts \(\Gamma,\Delta\), cut-free derivations of \(\Gamma,A\) and \(\Delta,A^\perp\) can be transformed into a cut-free derivation of \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\).

Induct on \(|A|\), allowing arbitrary contexts and arbitrary cut-free premise trees at every smaller size. If \(A=\bot\), erase \(\bot\) from the first derivation and weaken to \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\). If \(A=\top\), its dual is \(\bot\); erase that from the second derivation instead. If \(A=p\), apply . If \(A=\bar p\), swap the two premise trees and apply that same positive-literal construction; the resulting context \(\Delta\cup\Gamma\) is the required one.

Let \(A=B\wedge C\). transforms the first derivation into derivations of \(\Gamma,B\) and \(\Gamma,C\). Since \(A^\perp=B^\perp\vee C^\perp\), transforms the second into one of \(\Delta,B^\perp,C^\perp\). Cut the first component \(B\) using the induction hypothesis, obtaining \(\Gamma\cup\Delta,C^\perp\). Then cut \(C\) against the other component proof, obtaining \(\Gamma\cup\Gamma\cup\Delta=\Gamma\cup\Delta\). Both cut formulas are proper subformulas of \(A\).

Let \(A=B\vee C\). Disjunction inversion gives \(\Gamma,B,C\), while conjunction inversion on \(A^\perp=B^\perp\wedge C^\perp\) gives \(\Delta,B^\perp\) and \(\Delta,C^\perp\). First cut \(B\), with \(C\) retained as a context alternative, giving \(\Gamma\cup\Delta,C\). Then cut \(C\), obtaining \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\cup\Delta=\Gamma\cup\Delta\). The two recursive calls again have smaller cut formulas.

These six cases define the transformation for every formula. The erasing inversions handle any principal formula already present in a context. No bound on the height of an intermediate proof is needed: the main induction hypothesis permits all proofs for its strictly smaller formula.

What replaces a principal conjunction cut?

Suppose \(P_B,P_C\) prove \(\Gamma,B\) and \(\Gamma,C\), and \(Q\) proves \(\Delta,B^\perp,C^\perp\). A conjunction rule and a disjunction rule turn these into the two premises of a cut on \(B\wedge C\). How does the construction replace this cut?

Show answerHide answer

Compose \(P_B\) with \(Q\) on \(B\), keeping \(C^\perp\) in the context. Compose its result with \(P_C\) on \(C\). Each composition uses the already constructed cut-free transformation for a smaller formula:

\[ (\Gamma,B),(\Delta,B^\perp,C^\perp)\ \longmapsto\ (\Gamma\cup\Delta,C^\perp), \]
\[ (\Gamma,C),(\Gamma\cup\Delta,C^\perp)\ \longmapsto\ \Gamma\cup\Delta. \]

The repeated copy of \(\Gamma\) disappears because contexts are sets. For arbitrary premise trees, inversion first exposes these component arguments. Those inversions can rebuild or duplicate parts of a proof, so the recursive guarantee is the decrease from \(B\wedge C\) to \(B,C\), not a promised decrease of every intermediate tree's height or size.

Remove every intermediate cut from a finite proof

For every finite context \(\Gamma\), \(\mathsf C_{\mathrm{cut}}\vdash\Gamma\) if and only if \(\mathsf C\vdash\Gamma\). More precisely, every supplied finite derivation with cut has a cut-free derivation of exactly the same concluding context.

Transform the supplied tree recursively. An atomic-identity or truth leaf remains the same leaf. At any of the three original rules with premises, transform those proper premise trees and repeat that original rule. At a cut inference, first transform its two proper premise trees into cut-free trees. Apply to those transformed trees and its original cut formula. The result is a cut-free tree of the original cut conclusion.

The outer recursion terminates because the original proof tree is finite: recursive calls visit its proper premise trees. Each call to cut admissibility terminates by the formula-size construction already proved. This defines the claimed transformation without choosing a valuation or testing semantic validity. Conversely every cut-free tree is also a tree in the enlarged rule system, giving the reverse implication.

Track the formulas inside an expression

A formula \(B\) is a subformula of \(A\) if \(B=A\), or if \(A\) is a conjunction or disjunction and \(B\) is a subformula of one of its two children. This recursive definition includes the whole formula. A subformula of a subformula of \(A\) is a subformula of \(A\).

For transitivity, induct on the route from the middle formula to \(A\). A route of length zero leaves the original subformula relation unchanged. If the middle formula lies inside one child of \(A\), the induction hypothesis puts the first formula inside that same child, so the defining child clause puts it inside \(A\).

A cut-free proof uses only subformulas of its conclusion

Every formula in every sequent of a cut-free derivation of \(\Gamma\) is a subformula of some member of \(\Gamma\). Therefore every derivable sequent, even when first supplied with cuts, has a derivation with this property.

At each original rule, every member of a premise context is a subformula of a member of its concluding context. Retained context members are subformulas of themselves. In a conjunction rule its displayed child in either premise is a subformula of the introduced conjunction. In a disjunction rule both displayed children are subformulas of the introduced disjunction. In the bottom rule every premise member is retained. These observations also apply when an inserted formula was already in the context.

Follow a formula from any node down the finite route to the root, composing these subformula relations by . This gives a member of the final context containing it as a subformula. Finally apply to obtain a cut-free tree when the originally supplied tree uses cuts. This is an existence assertion about a transformed proof, not a restriction on every proof with cut.

A cut-free tree also supports interpolant extraction: for a covering split of its final alternatives, a recursion returns two factor proofs and a formula using only atom names common to the two sides. Composing that recursion with the specified cut-removal transformation handles a supplied proof with cuts.

The empty and falsity sequents have no proof, even with cut

Neither \(\mathsf C_{\mathrm{cut}}\vdash\varnothing\) nor \(\mathsf C_{\mathrm{cut}}\vdash\{\bot\}\) holds. This conclusion follows from the proof transformations without a semantic completeness theorem.

A cut-free proof cannot conclude the empty context. Each of its two leaf rules requires a member of its context. Each of its other rules has a conclusion containing its principal formula, so none can be the last rule of a proof of the empty context.

A cut-free proof of \(\{\bot\}\) would, by , give a cut-free proof of the empty context, which is impossible. A proof with cut of either sequent would become such a cut-free proof by . Thus both enlarged-system derivability claims are false as well.

Must an arbitrary proof with cut already be analytic?

For an atom \(p\), can a derivation concluding \(\{\top\}\) nevertheless use \(p\) and \(\bar p\), which are not subformulas of \(\top\)?

Show answerHide answer

Yes. The two contexts \(\{\top,p\}\) and \(\{\top,\bar p\}\) each have a truth leaf. Cut them on \(p\); their remaining contexts unite to \(\{\top\}\). Both signed atoms occur in this three-node derivation, but the only subformula of \(\top\) is \(\top\) itself.

Cut elimination replaces this particular proof by a cut-free proof of \(\{\top\}\), for example its single truth leaf. The subformula theorem describes that cut-free proof. It does not require every way of proving the same sequent to avoid unrelated intermediate formulas.
