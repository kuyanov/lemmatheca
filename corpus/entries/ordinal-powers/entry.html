Powers continue multiplication through ordinal stages

The product \(\alpha\cdot\beta\) consists of \(\beta\) successive blocks of type \(\alpha\). Powers repeat this operation. For a positive ordinal \(\alpha\), set-valued transfinite recursion defines

\[ \alpha^0=1,\qquad \alpha^{\beta+1}=\alpha^\beta\cdot\alpha,\qquad \alpha^\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}\alpha^\beta \quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit}). \]

At a limit, the powers at earlier stages determine the answer. Set \(0^0=1\) and \(0^\beta=0\) for \(\beta>0\); the displayed limit rule is restricted to positive bases. Induction gives \(1^\beta=1\) for every \(\beta\). Finite powers of finite ordinals agree with the usual natural-number powers.

To apply the recursion theorem, extend these rules to every set-valued history: return zero if a history has a nonordinal value, and otherwise use the displayed case for its position. At a limit, Replacement collects the earlier ordinal values and their supremum is an ordinal. Induction shows that the resulting history always has ordinal values, so the fallback is never used. The construction needs no set containing all ordinals.

All operations in this entry are ordinal operations. A power describes an order, whereas cardinal exponentiation counts functions. Neither interpretation should be substituted for the other.

A base greater than one gives strictly increasing powers

If \(\alpha>1\), then \(\alpha^\beta>0\) and \(\beta<\gamma\) implies \(\alpha^\beta<\alpha^\gamma\). At a nonzero limit exponent, the power is a limit ordinal.

Proof

Induction gives positivity: the initial value is one, a product of positive ordinals is positive, and a limit supremum contains the initial value. For each positive \(p\), strict comparison of block counts gives \(p=p\cdot1<p\cdot\alpha\). Thus each successor power exceeds its predecessor. Induction on \(\gamma\), using this fact at successors and the supremum rule at limits, gives strict increase. At a limit exponent these strictly increasing earlier values have no greatest member, so their supremum is a limit ordinal. □

Infinite powers can still describe countable orders

For each finite \(m\ge2\), \(m^\omega=\omega\). By contrast, \(\omega^2\) consists of countably many infinite blocks, and \(\omega^\omega=\sup_{n<\omega}\omega^n\) is larger than each \(\omega^n\).

Proof

The finite natural-number powers \(m^n\) are unbounded among the natural numbers, so their ordinal supremum is \(\omega\). The successor rule gives \(\omega^2=\omega\cdot\omega\); the limit rule and give the assertions about \(\omega^\omega\).

Each \(\omega^n\) is countable, by induction using countable products of orders. Their union \(\omega^\omega\) is countable by countability of countable unions, which uses choice. Thus these strictly different order types all have the same infinite cardinality. □

What does two to the omega mean?

Why does ordinal \(2^\omega=\omega\) not contradict the uncountability of all binary sequences?

Show answerHide answer

The ordinal power is the supremum of the finite orders \(2^n\). It has countably many positions. The cardinal power \(2^{\aleph_0}\) counts all functions from \(\mathbb N\) to \(\{0,1\}\), including infinitely supported sequences. That collection is uncountable. The base and exponent do not specify the meaning of the operation by themselves; the ordinal or cardinal convention matters.

The powers of omega eventually exceed any fixed ordinal

For every ordinal \(\gamma\), \(\gamma\le\omega^\gamma\). Consequently, for any \(\alpha\), the exponents satisfying \(\omega^\gamma\le\alpha\) form a set contained in \(\alpha+1\).

Proof

Use transfinite induction. At zero, \(0\le1\). If \(\gamma\le\omega^\gamma\), strict increase gives \(\gamma+1\le\omega^\gamma+1\le\omega^{\gamma+1}\). At a limit \(\lambda\), take suprema in \(\gamma\le\omega^\gamma\le\omega^\lambda\) for \(\gamma<\lambda\). If \(\omega^\gamma\le\alpha\), this inequality forces \(\gamma\le\alpha\). □

The inequality can be equality. We do not assume that the exponent of a leading power is always smaller than the ordinal it describes.

Every nonzero ordinal has a largest leading power

For every \(\alpha>0\), there is a unique \(\beta\) with \(\omega^\beta\le\alpha<\omega^{\beta+1}\).

Proof

Let \(S=\{\gamma:\omega^\gamma\le\alpha\}\). This is a set by , and it contains zero. Put \(\beta=\sup S\). If \(\beta=0\), it belongs to \(S\). If \(\beta\) is a successor and did not belong to \(S\), its predecessor would bound \(S\), contradicting the supremum. If \(\beta\) is a nonzero limit, each \(\delta<\beta\) lies below some member of \(S\), so \(\omega^\delta\le\alpha\); continuity gives \(\omega^\beta\le\alpha\). Thus \(\beta\) is the greatest member of \(S\). Its successor does not belong to \(S\), giving the upper inequality. Strict increase of powers makes \(\beta\) unique. □

The leading power has a finite coefficient and a smaller remainder

For \(\alpha>0\), take \(\beta\) from . There are unique \(n\in\mathbb N\), \(n>0\), and \(r<\omega^\beta\) such that

\[\alpha=\omega^\beta\cdot n+r.\]

Proof

The successor and product limit rules give \(\omega^{\beta+1}=\omega^\beta\cdot\omega=\sup_{k<\omega}\omega^\beta\cdot k\). Since \(\alpha\) is smaller than this supremum, there is a largest finite \(n\) with \(\omega^\beta\cdot n\le\alpha\); it is positive. Removing this initial segment gives a unique \(r\) with the displayed equality. If \(r\ge\omega^\beta\), addition comparison would give \(\alpha\ge\omega^\beta\cdot(n+1)\), contradicting maximality. Conversely, every \(r<\omega^\beta\) places the sum in the interval from \(\omega^\beta\cdot n\) to \(\omega^\beta\cdot(n+1)\), excluding its upper endpoint. These disjoint intervals determine \(n\); cancellation of a common left summand determines \(r\). □

Terms below a power of omega remain below it when added

If \(\gamma>0\) and \(x,y<\omega^\gamma\), then \(x+y<\omega^\gamma\). In particular, a finite sum of terms \(\omega^{\delta_i}\cdot n_i\), with \(\delta_i<\gamma\) and finite \(n_i\), is below \(\omega^\gamma\).

Proof

Induct on positive \(\gamma\). If \(\gamma=\delta+1\), choose positive finite \(k,l\) such that \(x<\omega^\delta\cdot k\) and \(y<\omega^\delta\cdot l\), using the supremum over finite block counts. Monotonicity and left distributivity give \(x+y\le\omega^\delta\cdot(k+l)<\omega^\gamma\). If \(\gamma\) is a nonzero limit, continuity supplies a common positive \(\delta<\gamma\) with both \(x,y<\omega^\delta\); apply the induction hypothesis. Finally, \(\omega^{\delta_i}\cdot n_i<\omega^{\delta_i+1}\le\omega^\gamma\). Repeated use of the first assertion proves the finite-sum assertion, with the empty sum equal to zero. □

Every nonzero ordinal has a unique Cantor normal form

Every \(\alpha>0\) has exactly one expression

\[ \alpha=\omega^{\beta_0}\cdot n_0+\omega^{\beta_1}\cdot n_1+\cdots+\omega^{\beta_{k-1}}\cdot n_{k-1}, \qquad \beta_0>\beta_1>\cdots>\beta_{k-1}, \]

where \(k\) is a positive finite integer and every coefficient \(n_i\) is a positive finite integer. This is the Cantor normal form. Zero has the empty expression.

Proof

Repeatedly apply to the nonzero remainder. The next leading exponent is strictly smaller than the preceding exponent, because the remainder is below its leading power. The procedure must stop after finitely many steps: an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of ordinals would have a least member, and its next term would be smaller. The final zero remainder gives the required finite expression.

To check uniqueness for any expression of the required shape, its tail after the first term is below \(\omega^{\beta_0}\) by ; if \(\beta_0=0\), there can be no later term and the tail is zero. Hence the whole expression lies between \(\omega^{\beta_0}\cdot n_0\) and \(\omega^{\beta_0}\cdot(n_0+1)\), excluding the upper endpoint. This identifies its leading exponent and coefficient as those of the construction. The unique initial-segment remainder identifies its tail. Repeat this argument until both expressions end; a remaining nonempty tail would be positive. □

Positive coefficients are essential: allowing zero would let us insert irrelevant terms and destroy uniqueness.

Normal forms compare at their first difference

Compare the exponent–coefficient pairs from left to right. At the first difference, the greater exponent gives the greater ordinal; for equal exponents, the greater coefficient gives the greater ordinal. If one list is a proper prefix of the other, the longer list gives the greater ordinal.

Proof

Cancel the common initial terms using strictness in the second summand. A remaining expression with leading exponent \(\beta\) is at least \(\omega^\beta\) and below \(\omega^{\beta+1}\). If \(\beta<\gamma\), this upper bound is at most \(\omega^\gamma\), the lower bound of the other expression. With the same exponent, the disjoint coefficient intervals from determine the comparison. A nonempty remaining tail is positive, which settles the prefix case. □

How much information does a normal form retain?

Write \(\omega^2+\omega+\omega+5\) in normal form and compare it with \(\omega^2\cdot2\). What does the theorem say about ordinals below \(\omega^\omega\)?

Show answerHide answer

The normal form is \(\omega^2+\omega\cdot2+5\). It is smaller than \(\omega^2\cdot2\): the leading exponents agree, but the first coefficient is one rather than two. Below \(\omega^\omega\), the leading exponent, and therefore every exponent, is a natural number. These ordinals are exactly the finite expressions \(\omega^m\cdot a_m+\cdots+\omega\cdot a_1+a_0\) with natural coefficients, omitting zero terms. Indeed, each such expression is below \(\omega^{m+1}<\omega^\omega\), and the normal-form theorem gives the converse. The zero polynomial represents zero.

A larger power absorbs a smaller beginning

If \(\gamma>0\) and \(x<\omega^\gamma\), then \(x+\omega^\gamma=\omega^\gamma\). For example, \((\omega^2+\omega\cdot3+7)+\omega^2=\omega^2\cdot2\).

Proof

The ordinal \(\omega^\gamma\) is a limit. The limit rule for addition gives \(x+\omega^\gamma=\sup_{y<\omega^\gamma}(x+y)\). Every term is below \(\omega^\gamma\) by , while \(y\le x+y\) gives the reverse bound after taking suprema. For the example, associativity separates the first \(\omega^2\) from its smaller tail; that tail is absorbed by the appended \(\omega^2\). □
