Proofs can use temporary assumptions

If both \(A\) and \(B\) hold, then \(A\) holds. A formal proof can express this reasoning as a small tree. Each inference has its premises above a horizontal line and its conclusion below it.

The label beside an inference names its rule. \(I\) means introduction: the rule builds a formula with the indicated connective. \(E\) means elimination: the rule uses a formula with that connective to infer a conclusion. For example, \(\land E_1\) selects the left conjunct, while \(\to I\) introduces an implication. Subscripts distinguish versions of a rule; \(\land E_2\) selects the right conjunct. A numeral after a comma, as in \(\to I,1\), identifies the temporary assumptions discharged at that step. The superscript in \([A]^1\) marks those assumptions; it is a label, not an exponent.

Here is a proof using conjunction elimination and implication introduction:

\[\frac{\dfrac{[A\land B]^1}{A}\;\land E_1}{(A\land B)\to A}\;\to I,1.\]

The bracketed formula is a temporary assumption. The conjunction-elimination step extracts \(A\); implication introduction then discharges the assumption marked \(1\). The final conclusion therefore has no open assumptions. Discharge means that the conclusion no longer depends on the marked assumption, because that dependence has been incorporated into an implication.

We use the finite propositional formulas built from atoms, \(\bot\), negation, conjunction, disjunction, and implication. A derivation is a finite tree built from assumptions and the rules in . A finite set \(\Gamma\) is a context of allowed open assumptions. Write \(\Gamma\vdash A\) when a derivation concludes \(A\) and every assumption leaf is either discharged or belongs to \(\Gamma\). Write \(\vdash A\) for \(\varnothing\vdash A\). The proof need not use every assumption in the context.

The rules of classical natural deduction

Here is the full rule list. Letters \(A,B,C\) stand for arbitrary formulas. An assumption leaf \(A\) is permitted when \(A\) belongs to the current context. In the displays, brackets mark temporary assumptions; the matching numeral names the inference that discharges them. Vertical dots \(\vdots\) stand for an intervening derivation. The subscripts in \(\lor I_1\) and \(\lor I_2\) indicate whether the premise supplies the left or right disjunct. \(\mathrm{CC}\) abbreviates classical contradiction.

\[\frac{A\qquad B}{A\land B}\;\land I.\]
\[\frac{A\land B}{A}\;\land E_1\qquad \frac{A\land B}{B}\;\land E_2.\]
\[\frac{A}{A\lor B}\;\lor I_1\qquad \frac{B}{A\lor B}\;\lor I_2.\]
\[\frac{A\lor B\qquad \begin{array}{c}[A]^1\\ \vdots\\ C\end{array}\qquad \begin{array}{c}[B]^1\\ \vdots\\ C\end{array}}{C}\;\lor E,1.\]
\[\frac{\begin{array}{c}[A]^1\\ \vdots\\ B\end{array}}{A\to B}\;\to I,1\qquad \frac{A\to B\qquad A}{B}\;\to E.\]
\[\frac{\begin{array}{c}[A]^1\\ \vdots\\ \bot\end{array}}{\neg A}\;\neg I,1\qquad \frac{A\qquad\neg A}{\bot}\;\neg E.\]
\[\frac{\bot}{A}\;\bot E\qquad \frac{\begin{array}{c}[\neg A]^1\\ \vdots\\ \bot\end{array}}{A}\;\mathrm{CC},1.\]

The first rule for \(\bot\), called explosion, derives any formula from a contradiction. The last, classical contradiction, derives \(A\) when assuming \(\neg A\) leads to a contradiction. It is part of our chosen calculus; the completeness theorem below concerns this classical rule list.

Discharge may remove any selected occurrences of the indicated assumption, including none. Thus a proof of \(B\) that never uses \(A\) still permits \(A\to B\). With contexts, implication introduction has the precise form “from \(\Gamma\cup\{A\}\vdash B\), infer \(\Gamma\vdash A\to B\).” Negation introduction and classical contradiction have the corresponding context forms. Disjunction elimination uses a proof of \(A\lor B\) from \(\Gamma\), a proof of \(C\) from \(\Gamma\cup\{A\}\), and a proof of \(C\) from \(\Gamma\cup\{B\}\). An equal formula already allowed by \(\Gamma\) remains allowed after discharge. Repeated occurrences are individual leaves even though the context is a set.

Allowing more assumptions preserves a proof

If \(\Gamma\subseteq\Delta\) and \(\Gamma\vdash A\), then \(\Delta\vdash A\). This property is called weakening.

Proof

Use induction on the derivation. An open assumption in \(\Gamma\) also belongs to \(\Delta\). Each rule remains the same after enlarging its ambient context. In a branch with temporary assumption \(B\), use \(\Gamma\cup\{B\}\subseteq\Delta\cup\{B\}\); its discharge at the last step is unchanged. This handles implication introduction, negation introduction, classical contradiction, and the two branches of disjunction elimination. The other rules simply combine the weakened premise derivations. □

When different premise branches have different contexts, weakening puts them in the union of those contexts. This justifies using one common ambient context in the rule descriptions.

Classical contradiction supplies the two cases

For every formula \(A\), \(\vdash A\lor\neg A\). Consequently, if \(\Gamma\cup\{A\}\vdash C\) and \(\Gamma\cup\{\neg A\}\vdash C\), then \(\Gamma\vdash C\).

Proof

Temporarily assume \(\neg(A\lor\neg A)\). Within that branch, assume \(A\). Disjunction introduction gives \(A\lor\neg A\), contradicting the outer assumption. Negation introduction discharges \(A\), giving \(\neg A\). Disjunction introduction now gives \(A\lor\neg A\) again, so the outer assumption produces a contradiction. Classical contradiction discharges it and concludes \(A\lor\neg A\). For the consequence, weaken this assumption-free proof to \(\Gamma\) and apply disjunction elimination to the two given branches. □

Every derivation preserves truth

For every finite context \(\Gamma\) and formula \(A\), if \(\Gamma\vdash A\), then \(\Gamma\models A\): every valuation making all formulas of \(\Gamma\) true also makes \(A\) true.

Proof

Induct on the finite derivation. An assumption is true because it belongs to the context. Conjunction introduction combines two true formulas, and its eliminations select a true conjunct. Either disjunction introduction preserves truth. For disjunction elimination, a true disjunction has a true disjunct; apply the induction hypothesis to the branch with that disjunct temporarily added.

For implication elimination, a true implication with a true antecedent has a true consequent. For implication introduction, consider a valuation satisfying the ambient context. If \(A\) is false, \(A\to B\) is true. If \(A\) is true, the valuation also satisfies the branch context \(\Gamma\cup\{A\}\), so the induction hypothesis makes \(B\) true.

Negation elimination cannot have both its premises true. For negation introduction, if \(A\) were true along with the ambient context, the induction hypothesis for the branch would make \(\bot\) true, which is impossible. Thus \(A\) is false and \(\neg A\) true. Explosion has no valuation satisfying its derived premise \(\bot\), so its conclusion is a consequence of the context. Finally, in classical contradiction, if the conclusion \(A\) were false, \(\neg A\) would be true; the induction hypothesis for that branch would again make \(\bot\) true. These are all the rules. □

Can the consequent recover the antecedent?

For distinct atoms \(p,q\), can \(p\) be derived from \(p\to q\) and \(q\)?

No. Set \(p\) false and \(q\) true. Both premises are true while \(p\) is false. therefore rules out any derivation. Implication elimination requires the antecedent as its second premise; it does not reverse the implication.

Each truth-table row has a derivation

Write \(v(A)\) for the recursively computed truth value of a formula \(A\). Let \(S\) be a finite set of atoms and \(v\) a valuation. Its signed context is \[\begin{aligned}L(S,v)&=\{p:p\in S,\ v(p)=\mathrm{true}\}\\ &\quad\cup\{\neg p:p\in S,\ v(p)=\mathrm{false}\}.\end{aligned}\] If every atom of \(A\) belongs to \(S\), then \(L(S,v)\vdash A\) when \(v(A)\) is true, and \(L(S,v)\vdash\neg A\) when \(v(A)\) is false.

Proof

Induct on the construction of \(A\), using the same signed context throughout. For an atom, the required literal is an assumption. The formula \(\bot\) is always false; assume \(\bot\) and immediately discharge it by negation introduction to obtain \(\neg\bot\).

For \(\neg B\), if \(B\) is false, the induction hypothesis already derives \(\neg B\). If \(B\) is true, it derives \(B\); assuming \(\neg B\) gives a contradiction, so negation introduction derives \(\neg\neg B\), the negative of the formula under consideration.

For \(B\land C\), if both conjuncts are true, combine their derivations. Otherwise at least one is false. Assume the conjunction, extract that conjunct, and contradict its negation supplied by induction; discharge the conjunction to derive \(\neg(B\land C)\).

For \(B\lor C\), a true disjunct supplies the disjunction. If both are false, induction supplies \(\neg B\) and \(\neg C\). Assume \(B\lor C\), then use disjunction elimination: the \(B\)-branch contradicts \(\neg B\), and the \(C\)-branch contradicts \(\neg C\). Both conclude \(\bot\), so negation introduction gives \(\neg(B\lor C)\).

For \(B\to C\), if \(C\) is true, derive it and introduce the implication, allowing the temporary \(B\) to be unused. If \(B\) is false, induction supplies \(\neg B\); assuming \(B\) gives a contradiction, explosion gives \(C\), and implication introduction discharges \(B\). These cover every true implication. In the false case, \(B\) is true and \(C\) false. Assume \(B\to C\), use the derived \(B\) to obtain \(C\), and contradict the derived \(\neg C\). Negation introduction then gives \(\neg(B\to C)\). Any derivation used inside an enlarged branch is justified by weakening. □

The conjunction selecting one assignment is developed separately in truth-row formulas.

A finite collection of atom choices can be discharged

Let \(S\) be a finite set of atoms. If \(\Gamma\cup L(S,v)\vdash C\) for every valuation \(v\), then \(\Gamma\vdash C\).

Proof

Induct on the size of \(S\). If \(S\) is empty, its signed context is empty, so any valuation gives the required derivation from \(\Gamma\). There is always a valuation, for example the one making every atom false, even when there are no atoms.

Otherwise write \(S=T\cup\{p\}\) with \(p\notin T\). Fix any valuation \(v\). Change only \(p\), first to true and then to false. The signs on \(T\) remain those of \(v\), and the hypothesis gives derivations of \(C\) from \(\Gamma\cup L(T,v)\cup\{p\}\) and from \(\Gamma\cup L(T,v)\cup\{\neg p\}\). Apply to discharge this last choice. We have obtained \(\Gamma\cup L(T,v)\vdash C\) for every \(v\); the induction hypothesis removes the remaining choices. □

Every finite-premise consequence has a derivation

For every finite context \(\Gamma\) and formula \(C\), \(\Gamma\models C\) if and only if \(\Gamma\vdash C\). In particular, a formula is a tautology if and only if it has a derivation with no open assumptions.

Proof

Soundness gives one direction. For the other, assume \(\Gamma\models C\). Let \(S\) contain all atoms occurring in \(C\) or in a member of \(\Gamma\); this set is finite because the context and every formula are finite. Fix a valuation \(v\).

If all premises in \(\Gamma\) are true under \(v\), then \(C\) is true, and gives \(L(S,v)\vdash C\). Weaken to \(\Gamma\cup L(S,v)\). If some premise \(B\in\Gamma\) is false, the same lemma gives \(L(S,v)\vdash\neg B\). In the enlarged context \(B\) is also an assumption, so negation elimination gives \(\bot\), and explosion gives \(C\). Thus every valuation supplies \(\Gamma\cup L(S,v)\vdash C\). removes the signed assumptions and yields \(\Gamma\vdash C\). The empty-context case is the tautology assertion. □

The argument uses a finite truth table. It establishes completeness for finite premises; no assertion about infinite-premise compactness or first-order completeness is needed.

Consistency and satisfiability meet after completeness

A finite context \(\Gamma\) is consistent if \(\Gamma\nvdash\bot\). It is satisfiable if some valuation makes every member true. These definitions describe different objects: consistency concerns the absence of a derivation, while satisfiability concerns the existence of a valuation.

For finite contexts they are equivalent. A satisfying valuation rules out a derivation of \(\bot\) by soundness. Conversely, if no valuation satisfies \(\Gamma\), then every valuation satisfying it would make \(\bot\) true, vacuously; thus \(\Gamma\models\bot\). Completeness gives \(\Gamma\vdash\bot\), so \(\Gamma\) is inconsistent. Taking the contrapositive proves the other direction.

What happens at the two extremes?

Can the empty context derive \(\bot\)? What can be derived from \(A\) and \(\neg A\)?

Show answerHide answer

The empty context cannot derive \(\bot\): any valuation satisfies all its premises and makes \(\bot\) false, contradicting soundness. In contrast, \(A,\neg A\vdash C\) for every formula \(C\): negation elimination derives \(\bot\), and explosion derives \(C\). An inconsistent context therefore has every formula as a consequence, both syntactically and semantically.
