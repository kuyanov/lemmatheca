How large can a model be?

All structures here are nonempty, and all languages and domains are sets. We use the axiom of choice in the surrounding mathematics. Write \(|L|\) for the cardinality of the language: the size of the set of nonlogical symbols of \(L\), counting function and relation symbols of all finite arities. Its language-size bound is

\[\lambda_L=\max(\aleph_0,|L|).\]

There are at most \(\lambda_L\) formulas: formulas are finite strings over an alphabet of size at most \(\lambda_L\). The downward Löwenheim–Skolem bound therefore supplies small elementary substructures containing prescribed parameters. We now ask for large ones. An elementary embedding preserves the truth of every formula with parameters. Its image is an elementary substructure, so after replacing the target by an isomorphic copy we can treat the map as an inclusion.

The compactness arguments below use semantic compactness for arbitrary set-sized languages. The enlarged language may be uncountable even when \(L\) is countable.

Forcing many distinct elements with constants

Let \(M\) be an infinite \(L\)-structure, and let \(I\) be any set. Add a name \(c_a\) for each \(a\in M\), and fresh constants \(d_i\), \(i\in I\), disjoint from those names and from \(L\). In this language form

\[\Sigma=\operatorname{ElDiag}(M)\;\cup\;\{d_i\ne d_j:i,j\in I,\ i\ne j\}.\]

Here the elementary diagram records every sentence true in the expansion of \(M\) naming each of its elements. Each finite subset of \(\Sigma\) has a model.

Only finitely many constants \(d_i\) occur in a given finite subset. Interpret them as distinct elements of \(M\), possible because \(M\) is infinite. Interpret every \(c_a\) as \(a\), and assign arbitrary values to the remaining \(d_i\). The full elementary diagram is then true, as are the finitely many requested inequalities. This proves finite satisfiability. The \(d_i\) need not avoid all the old elements: distinctness among them is what forces the lower size bound.

Every infinite structure has arbitrarily large elementary extensions

For every infinite \(L\)-structure \(M\) and every cardinal \(\kappa\), there is an elementary embedding \(e:M\to N\) into a structure with \(|N|\ge\kappa\). Equivalently, after taking an isomorphic copy, there is an elementary extension \(M\preccurlyeq N\) of at least that size.

Choose a set \(I\) of cardinality \(\kappa\). By and compactness, \(\Sigma\) has a model \(N'\). Let \(N\) be its reduct to \(L\). Since \(N'\) models the elementary diagram, the diagram lemma makes the specific map \(e(a)=c_a^{N'}\) an elementary embedding. The map \(i\mapsto d_i^{N'}\) is injective, so \(|N|\ge\kappa\). The copy construction turns \(e\) into a literal elementary inclusion if desired.

In particular, take \(\kappa=|M|^+\) to obtain a proper extension. Infinitude is essential; a finite structure has no proper elementary extension.

Choosing the exact size of an elementary extension

Let \(M\) be an infinite \(L\)-structure. If

\[\kappa\ge\max(\aleph_0,|L|,|M|),\]

then there is an elementary embedding \(M\to N\) with \(|N|=\kappa\). Thus \(M\) has, up to the same copy convention, an elementary extension of exactly that size.

First use to find \(e:M\to P\) with \(|P|\ge\kappa\). Choose \(Y\subseteq P\) of cardinality \(\kappa\). The set \(X=e[M]\cup Y\) has cardinality \(\kappa\), by addition of infinite cardinals. Apply the downward bound inside \(P\) to get \(N\preccurlyeq P\) containing \(X\) with \(|N|\le\kappa\). Because \(Y\subseteq N\), equality holds.

The map \(e\) now takes values in \(N\), and remains elementary: for every formula \(\varphi\) and tuple \(\bar a\) from \(M\),

\[M\models\varphi[\bar a]\iff P\models\varphi[e(\bar a)] \iff N\models\varphi[e(\bar a)].\]

Both equivalences use the specified parameters, so their composite certifies the same map into \(N\).

An infinite model gives every sufficiently large infinite size

If an \(L\)-theory \(T\) has an infinite model, then for every cardinal \(\kappa\ge\lambda_L\), it has a model of cardinality exactly \(\kappa\). Moreover, any infinite \(L\)-structure \(M\) has an elementarily equivalent structure of each such size.

Fix an infinite model \(M\) of \(T\). If \(\kappa\ge|M|\), use . If \(\kappa\le|M|\), the exact-size downward theorem, with no prescribed parameters, gives \(N\preccurlyeq M\) of size \(\kappa\). In either case an elementary embedding relates the structures, and therefore they agree on sentences. Thus \(N\equiv M\) and \(N\models T\). Applying this to \(\operatorname{Th}(M)\), or just retaining the sentence agreement, proves the final assertion.

For a countable language the threshold is \(\aleph_0\). The theorem concerns all infinite cardinalities; it makes no promise about finite models.

Can a smaller model still contain the original structure?

If \(L\) is countable and \(M\) is uncountable, the theorem gives a countable \(N\equiv M\). Can we require \(M\preccurlyeq N\)? Does the theorem require every infinite model of \(T\) to have size at least \(|L|\)?

A countable \(N\) cannot contain uncountable \(M\), even by an embedding, since every embedding is injective. One can instead take \(N\preccurlyeq M\). The \(|M|\) bound in belongs to extensions of a prescribed structure, whereas asks for another model of its theory.

The language bound is a sufficient threshold, not a lower bound on every model. An arbitrarily large language can interpret all its constants by one element. The same assignment on a countably infinite domain gives an infinite model too. Conversely, for any infinite cardinal \(\mu\), a language with constants \(c_i\), \(i<\mu\), and axioms \(c_i\ne c_j\) for distinct indices has no model of size less than \(\mu\); interpreting the constants by distinct elements in \(\mu\) gives a model of size \(\mu\). Thus the dependence on language size cannot in general be removed.

Different cardinalities do not give different sentences

Structures of different cardinalities are not isomorphic, and a complete theory with an infinite model has models of different sizes. Elementary extensions preserve the truth of every sentence in the original language.

An isomorphism is a bijection, so structures of different cardinalities cannot be isomorphic. But the structures in can all be chosen elementarily equivalent to the same \(M\). Thus even a complete theory with an infinite model has nonisomorphic models of different sizes. This does not contradict countable categoricity results, which compare only countable models.

In particular, if \(M\preccurlyeq N\), no \(L\)-sentence becomes true or false on passing from \(M\) to \(N\). Producing these two models therefore supplies no sentence on which they disagree. The preservation converses describe which formulas retain truth under ordinary substructure inclusions, where elementarity is not assumed.

What a model proves about nonderivability

For this block and the remaining blocks, let \(L\) be countable, and let \(T\) be a theory of \(L\)-sentences. Write \(T\vdash\theta\) for derivability in the first-order proof calculus. Then

\[T\nvdash\theta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\cup\{\neg\theta\}\text{ has a model}.\]

A model of \(T\cup\{\neg\theta\}\) is a counterexample to \(T\models\theta\), so soundness forbids a proof of \(\theta\). Conversely, if \(T\nvdash\theta\), completeness implies \(T\not\models\theta\). There is therefore a model of \(T\) in which \(\theta\) is false, exactly a model of \(T\cup\{\neg\theta\}\). The countable-language hypothesis matches the proof-calculus completeness theorem being used.

Independence requires witnesses on both sides

A sentence \(\theta\) is independent of \(T\) if neither \(T\vdash\theta\) nor \(T\vdash\neg\theta\). By and double negation, this is equivalent to both theories

\[T\cup\{\theta\},\qquad T\cup\{\neg\theta\}\]

having models. The models may have the same cardinality or different cardinalities; the requirement is their opposite answers to \(\theta\).

For example, let \(T\) be the empty theory in the equality-only language and let \(\theta=\forall x\forall y\,(x=y)\). A one-element structure satisfies \(\theta\), and a two-element structure satisfies \(\neg\theta\). These witnesses show independence. This is stronger evidence than merely having two nonisomorphic models: two infinite equality-only structures of different sizes agree on every sentence, by completeness of the theory of infinite sets.

Conditional model constructions give relative consistency

Write \(\operatorname{Con}(T)\) for the external assertion that \(T\) has no proof of contradiction. Suppose \(T\) and \(S\) are theories in countable languages, possibly different ones, and we have proved: from any model of \(T\), there exists a model of \(S\). Then, in the surrounding mathematics,

\[\operatorname{Con}(T)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Con}(S).\]

Assume \(T\) is consistent. The model existence theorem supplies a model of \(T\). Apply the given construction to obtain a model of \(S\). Soundness makes \(S\) consistent.

The hypothesis is the mathematical work in an application; this lemma does not supply the required construction. Here \(\operatorname{Con}(T)\) is an assertion about finite proofs in the metatheory. We have not encoded it as an arithmetic sentence or proved that this argument is formalizable in either object theory. Nor does a model obtained by completeness come with any additional well-foundedness or transitivity property.
