Clauses as finite sets of alternatives

Suppose at least one of two switches must be on, but each is also required to be off. The three requirements are \(p\lor q\), \(\neg p\), and \(\neg q\). A truth table finds the contradiction. Resolution instead records a short chain: the first two requirements force \(q\), which conflicts with the third.

We use the literals and conjunctive normal forms already introduced. A literal is \(p\) or \(\neg p\), for an atom \(p\in P\). Here a clause \(C\) is a finite set of literals, read as their disjunction. A valuation satisfies \(C\), written \(v\models C\), when at least one member is true. A finite clause set \(\Gamma\) is read as the conjunction of its clauses: \(v\models\Gamma\) means \(v\models C\) for every \(C\in\Gamma\).

Reordering literals and deleting repetitions does not affect a disjunction's truth value. Thus replacing the earlier finite lists by finite sets preserves satisfaction, and the same applies to the outer conjunction. These are representations of the same propositional formulas, with the same valuations.

The empty clause \(\varnothing\) is false under every valuation. The empty clause set is true under every valuation. In particular, the clause sets \(\varnothing\) and \(\{\varnothing\}\) have opposite satisfiability behavior. A clause set is satisfiable if some valuation satisfies it, and unsatisfiable otherwise.

Resolve on one atom at a time

If \(p\in C\) and \(\neg p\in D\), their resolvent on \(p\) is

\[ R_p(C,D):=(C\setminus\{p\})\cup(D\setminus\{\neg p\}). \]

The resolution rule permits us to infer this clause from \(C\) and \(D\). The selected atom \(p\) is called the pivot. We remove only the selected occurrence from each parent; other literals, including a remaining opposite sign of the pivot, stay in the resolvent. Sets already identify repeated literals.

For example, resolving \(\{p,\neg q,\neg r\}\) with \(\{\neg p,\neg q\}\) on \(p\) gives \(\{\neg q,\neg r\}\). Resolving the two unit clauses \(\{p\}\) and \(\{\neg p\}\) gives the empty clause. A clause containing both \(p\) and \(\neg p\) is called tautological; every valuation satisfies it.

Finite derivations and refutations

A resolution derivation from \(\Gamma\) is a finite tree of clauses. Each leaf is a member of \(\Gamma\); each internal node is the resolvent of its two children on a specified pivot. Write \(\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{res}}C\) if such a tree has root \(C\). A derivation with root the empty clause is a refutation.

For the switch requirements, the leaves \(\{p,q\}\) and \(\{\neg p\}\) give \(\{q\}\); resolving this with the leaf \(\{\neg q\}\) gives \(\varnothing\). All three leaves belong to the given clause set, so this is a refutation.

A derived clause can replace a leaf in another derivation. More precisely, if every member of \(\Delta\) has a derivation from \(\Gamma\), then every clause derivable from \(\Delta\) is derivable from \(\Gamma\). Induct on the second tree: replace a premise leaf by its supplied derivation, and retain each resolution node. This is a construction using the stated rule, rather than an additional inference rule.

Resolution preserves every satisfying valuation

If \(\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{res}}C\), every valuation satisfying \(\Gamma\) satisfies \(C\). Consequently a clause set with a resolution refutation is unsatisfiable.

Proof

First check one inference. Suppose \(v\models C,D\), with pivot \(p\in C\) and \(\neg p\in D\). If \(v(p)=0\), a true literal of \(C\) is different from \(p\), so it remains in \(R_p(C,D)\). If \(v(p)=1\), a true literal of \(D\) is different from \(\neg p\), and again remains. Thus \(v\models R_p(C,D)\).

Induct on a derivation tree. Its leaves are satisfied by the hypothesis, and the preceding argument passes satisfaction from each pair of children to their parent. A refutation would make \(v\models\varnothing\), which is impossible. □

In particular, adding a resolvent to a clause set preserves exactly its satisfying valuations. The reverse implication holds because the original clauses are still present. Replacing both parents by their resolvent is a different operation and need not preserve the information they carried.

Why not remove two complementary pairs?

Take \(C=\{p,q\}\) and \(D=\{\neg p,\neg q\}\), where \(p\ne q\). What are the two possible resolvents? Why would deleting both complementary pairs at once be unsound?

Resolving on \(p\) gives \(\{q,\neg q\}\); resolving on \(q\), with the parents oriented appropriately, gives \(\{p,\neg p\}\). Both are tautological. Deleting both pairs would instead produce \(\varnothing\). But the assignment \(v(p)=1\), \(v(q)=0\) satisfies both parents, so the empty clause is not their consequence. This is why one inference selects one pivot.

Forget one atom while retaining its constraints

Fix an atom \(p\). Discard every clause containing both \(p\) and \(\neg p\), since those clauses are always true. Partition the remaining clauses into \(\Gamma_0\), containing neither sign of \(p\); \(\Gamma_+\), containing \(p\) but not \(\neg p\); and \(\Gamma_-\), containing \(\neg p\) but not \(p\). Define

\[ E_p(\Gamma):=\Gamma_0\cup\{R_p(C,D):C\in\Gamma_+,\ D\in\Gamma_-\}. \]

This finite clause set contains no occurrence of \(p\). Every member is either an original clause or the result of one resolution step, so it is derivable from \(\Gamma\). No other atom is introduced. We discard clauses tautological on the selected pivot only; tautologies on other atoms may remain harmlessly.

Every model after elimination extends to a model before it

If \(v\models E_p(\Gamma)\), there is a valuation \(w\models\Gamma\) that agrees with \(v\) on every atom other than \(p\). Conversely, a valuation satisfying \(\Gamma\) satisfies \(E_p(\Gamma)\). Thus \(\Gamma\) and \(E_p(\Gamma)\) are equisatisfiable.

Proof

For \(C\in\Gamma_+\), write \(C^-=C\setminus\{p\}\); for \(D\in\Gamma_-\), write \(D^-=D\setminus\{\neg p\}\). These remainders contain neither sign of \(p\), so changing \(v(p)\) does not change their truth values.

If every positive remainder \(C^-\) is satisfied by \(v\), set \(w(p)=0\) and leave the other values unchanged. Positive clauses remain true because their remainders are true; negative clauses are true because \(\neg p\) is true.

Otherwise choose \(C\in\Gamma_+\) whose remainder is false, and set \(w(p)=1\). Every negative remainder \(D^-\) must be true under \(v\): its union with this fixed false \(C^-\) is a resolvent in \(E_p(\Gamma)\). Hence all negative clauses remain true, and all positive clauses are true because \(p\) is true.

In both cases the neutral clauses retain their values, and discarded clauses are tautological. This constructs \(w\models\Gamma\). Conversely, neutral clauses are premises and resolvents follow by the one-step soundness argument in . □

Recover a forgotten switch

Eliminate \(p\) from \(\Gamma=\{\{p,q\},\{\neg p,r\},\{\neg q\}\}\), where \(p,q,r\) are distinct. Find a model of the eliminated clauses, then extend it to the original clauses.

The neutral clause is \(\{\neg q\}\), and the only positive-negative pair gives \(\{q,r\}\). Thus \(E_p(\Gamma)=\{\{\neg q\},\{q,r\}\}\). Set \(q=0\), \(r=1\). The positive remainder \(\{q\}\) is false, so the extension proof chooses \(p=1\). These values satisfy all three original clauses. The eliminated clauses describe precisely the restrictions on \(q,r\) under which some value for \(p\) works.

Every finite unsatisfiable clause set has a refutation

For every finite clause set \(\Gamma\),

\[ \Gamma\text{ is unsatisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{res}}\varnothing. \]

Finite closure gives a decision procedure

Starting with a finite clause set, repeatedly add any missing resolvent. Stop when the empty clause is found or when no further resolvent can be added. This process terminates. It finds the empty clause exactly when the original clause set is unsatisfiable; otherwise the final clause set is satisfiable.

Proof

Let \(S\) be the finite set of atoms occurring initially. Resolution never introduces an atom outside \(S\). There are only finitely many literals on \(S\), and hence finitely many subsets of those literals. Each addition is a new clause from this finite collection, so only finitely many additions are possible. More precisely, if \(\mathcal C(S)\) is the set of all clauses on these literals, at most \(|\mathcal C(S)|-|\Gamma|\) additions are possible. A saturation step adds exactly one missing resolvent. No step is available precisely when the current set is closed under resolution, so a closed endpoint is reached within this bound.

Each step preserves exactly the satisfying valuations, by ; induction along a finite run gives the same conclusion for its endpoint. Every added clause is also derivable, so finding the empty clause certifies unsatisfiability. Suppose instead that the process reaches a clause set \(\Delta\) closed under resolution, without the empty clause. Induction on derivation trees shows that every clause derivable from \(\Delta\) belongs to \(\Delta\). If \(\Delta\) were unsatisfiable, would put the empty clause in it, a contradiction. A model of \(\Delta\) is also a model of the initial set. □

The argument guarantees termination and a correct answer. It makes no claim that the search will be short. Atom elimination gives another terminating procedure. List all occurring atoms and apply the one-atom elimination operation successively. At each stage the chosen atom disappears and satisfiability is preserved. After the last stage, no atom remains, so the only possible clause is the empty clause. If it occurs, the original set is unsatisfiable. Otherwise the final set has no clauses and every valuation satisfies it. Repeatedly apply in reverse order to recover a model of the original clauses; values on atoms outside the eliminated list stay unchanged.

Prove a formula by refuting its negation

Let \(A\) be any propositional formula, and let \(\Gamma\) be a finite clause representation of a formula equivalent to \(\neg A\). Then \(A\) is valid exactly when \(\Gamma\) has a resolution refutation. Such a \(\Gamma\) always exists, by the normal-form theorem.

Proof

Validity means that \(A\) is true under every valuation, equivalently that \(\neg A\) has no satisfying valuation. Conversion to an equivalent CNF and removal of repeated literals and clauses preserve satisfaction. Apply to that finite clause set. □

For example, the negation of \(((p\lor q)\land\neg p)\to q\) is equivalent to \((p\lor q)\land\neg p\land\neg q\). Its clauses are the switch requirements in , where we gave an explicit refutation. Thus this implication is valid. More generally, to test whether finitely many premises entail \(B\), apply the same construction to the conjunction of the premises with \(\neg B\).

Resolution is a separate proof calculus from natural deduction. Its completeness concerns refutations of clauses. It does not say that every clause that follows semantically from some premises must itself be obtainable by the resolution rule.
