Put negation at the atoms

Fix an atom set \(P\). A signed atom is a pair \((p,+)\) or \((p,-)\), written \(p\) or \(\bar p\). A formula in negation normal form, abbreviated an NNF formula, is a finite expression generated by

\[ A ::= p\mid\bar p\mid\bot\mid\top\mid(A\wedge A)\mid(A\vee A). \]

The two signed atoms are distinct syntactic objects, even though the bar anticipates their opposite meanings. Negation never applies to a compound expression in this grammar. This differs from the stronger CNF and DNF requirements: conjunctions and disjunctions may be nested in any order. An NNF formula's atom support, \(\operatorname{at}(A)\), is the finite set of atom names appearing in it, ignoring signs. Constants have empty support, and a binary connective takes the union of its children's supports.

Negation becomes a structural dual

The dual \(A^\perp\) of an NNF formula is defined recursively by

\[ p^\perp=\bar p,\quad\bar p^\perp=p,\quad\bot^\perp=\top,\quad\top^\perp=\bot. \]
\[ (A\wedge B)^\perp=A^\perp\vee B^\perp,\qquad(A\vee B)^\perp=A^\perp\wedge B^\perp. \]

Every formula satisfies \((A^\perp)^\perp=A\) and \(\operatorname{at}(A^\perp)=\operatorname{at}(A)\).

Induct on the finite expression. Each signed atom or constant returns to itself after two swaps. At a binary connective the first dual swaps the connective and dualizes its children, while the second swaps back; apply the induction hypotheses to the children. Swaps of signs and connectives introduce or remove no atom names, which gives the support identity by the same induction.

Interpret the signed language

For a valuation \(v:P\to\{0,1\}\), the NNF evaluation \(\lVert A\rVert_v\) assigns \(v(p)\) to \(p\), \(1-v(p)\) to \(\bar p\), zero to \(\bot\), and one to \(\top\). Conjunction and disjunction use their ordinary Boolean truth tables.

The reading map \(r\) turns an NNF expression into an ordinary propositional formula: read \(p\) as \(p\), \(\bar p\) as \(\neg p\), \(\bot\) as \(\bot\), and \(\top\) as \(\neg\bot\); retain both binary connectives. For all \(A,v\),

\[ \llbracket r(A)\rrbracket_v=\lVert A\rVert_v,\qquad\lVert A^\perp\rVert_v=1-\lVert A\rVert_v. \]

Also \(\operatorname{at}(r(A))=\operatorname{at}(A)\).

The reading identity and its support assertion follow directly by induction from the clauses just specified. For duality, signs and constants give complementary values. At a conjunction, the dual is a disjunction of the two complementary values, which is false exactly when both original values are true. At a disjunction, the dual is a conjunction of complementary values, which is true exactly when both original values are false. These checks prove the displayed duality identity in the two remaining cases.

Translate a formula together with its negation

The positive translation \(N^+(F)\) and negative translation into NNF \(N^-(F)\) of an ordinary propositional formula are defined together. Here the word negative concerns Boolean negation, rather than an interpretation into constructive logic. Their defining equations are

\[ N^+(p)=p,\quad N^-(p)=\bar p,\quad N^+(\bot)=\bot,\quad N^-(\bot)=\top. \]
\[ N^+(\neg F)=N^-(F),\qquad N^-(\neg F)=N^+(F). \]
\[ N^+(F\wedge G)=N^+(F)\wedge N^+(G),\qquad N^-(F\wedge G)=N^-(F)\vee N^-(G). \]
\[ N^+(F\vee G)=N^+(F)\vee N^+(G),\qquad N^-(F\vee G)=N^-(F)\wedge N^-(G). \]
\[ N^+(F\to G)=N^-(F)\vee N^+(G),\qquad N^-(F\to G)=N^+(F)\wedge N^-(G). \]

These equations are a terminating construction: every recursive call is on an immediate subformula. They do not distribute conjunction over disjunction or disjunction over conjunction.

The Gödel–Gentzen negative translation is a different construction on the shared propositional language: it interprets classical deductions intuitionistically and double-negates atoms and disjunctions, rather than producing the signed NNF syntax used here.

The translations preserve their specified meanings

For every ordinary formula \(F\), NNF formula \(A\), and valuation \(v\),

\[ \lVert N^+(F)\rVert_v=\llbracket F\rrbracket_v,\qquad\lVert N^-(F)\rVert_v=1-\llbracket F\rrbracket_v. \]
\[ N^-(F)=(N^+(F))^\perp,\qquad\operatorname{at}(N^+(F))=\operatorname{at}(F)=\operatorname{at}(N^-(F)). \]

The reading map is a right inverse: \(N^+(r(A))=A\). Consequently every ordinary formula has an equivalent NNF reading with exactly the same atom support.

Prove the assertions about \(F\) together by structural induction. The signed-atom and constant clauses have the specified values and supports. At a negation the positive and negative roles exchange; the induction hypotheses give both values, and duality's involution gives the syntactic identity. Conjunction and disjunction use the two De Morgan truth tables checked in . For implication, its Boolean value is the disjunction of the negated antecedent and the consequent. Its complement is the conjunction of the antecedent and the negated consequent, exactly the two clauses for \(N^\pm\). Each binary clause joins the same two supports, and the defining dual swaps its two clauses as required.

Induct separately on \(A\) for the right-inverse assertion. A negative atom reads as \(\neg p\), whose positive translation is \(\bar p\); \(\top\) reads as \(\neg\bot\), whose translation is \(\top\). The other four constructors are preserved directly. Finally read back \(N^+(F)\): its value is that of \(F\) by the evaluation and translation identities, and its support is unchanged by both maps.

A sequent presents alternatives

A one-sided sequent \(\vdash\Gamma\) consists of a finite set \(\Gamma\) of NNF formulas. It is valid if, for every valuation, at least one member has value one. Write \(\Gamma,A\) for \(\Gamma\cup\{A\}\), and similarly for several formulas. The empty sequent is invalid: it offers no true member, under any valuation. The singleton sequent \(\vdash\{\top\}\) is valid, and \(\vdash\{\bot\}\) is invalid.

Contexts are sets throughout this calculus. Their order has no mathematical meaning, and repeating a formula does not change a context. Thus exchange and contraction, viewed as reordering or removing repetitions, are identities of contexts. This convention does not assert a translation theorem for another proof calculus with lists or multisets.

Generate finite proofs with five rules

A sequent derivation is a finite tree built from the following rules. Its conclusion is the sequent at the root. The leaves are precisely the two rules with no premises; \(p\) ranges over atoms and \(A,B\) over NNF formulas.

\[ \frac{}{\vdash\Gamma}\ (p,\bar p\in\Gamma),\qquad\frac{}{\vdash\Gamma}\ (\top\in\Gamma). \]
\[ \frac{\vdash\Gamma,A\qquad\vdash\Gamma,B}{\vdash\Gamma,A\wedge B},\qquad\frac{\vdash\Gamma,A,B}{\vdash\Gamma,A\vee B},\qquad\frac{\vdash\Gamma}{\vdash\Gamma,\bot}. \]

Write \(\mathsf C\vdash\Gamma\) when such a tree exists. The first leaf rule is atomic identity; it requires the same atom with opposite signs. The second introduces truth. In each binary-connective rule, the displayed compound formula in the conclusion is its principal formula. A context may already contain a displayed formula: insertion still means ordinary set union. In the conjunction rule both premises have exactly the same remaining context.

No rule here introduces an arbitrary intermediate lemma. In particular, no rule infers \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\) from \(\Gamma,A\) and \(\Delta,A^\perp\). Each use of a rule must have finite premise trees; an infinite branch with no leaf is not a derivation.

The cut-admissibility transformation constructs such a composition using only these five rules. More generally, cut elimination removes every cut from a finite proof without changing its concluding context.

Unused alternatives may be added

Weakening is admissible: if \(\mathsf C\vdash\Gamma\) and \(\Gamma\subseteq\Delta\), then \(\mathsf C\vdash\Delta\). More precisely, for every finite \(\Sigma\), a derivation of \(\Gamma\) can be changed into one of \(\Gamma\cup\Sigma\) by adding \(\Sigma\) to the context at every node.

Induct on the supplied tree, with \(\Sigma\) fixed. An atomic-identity leaf still contains its two opposite literals after adding \(\Sigma\); a truth leaf still contains \(\top\). At a conjunction inference apply the induction hypotheses to both premises and repeat the same conjunction rule with common context \(\Gamma\cup\Sigma\). Set-union associativity and commutativity identify its premises and conclusion with the required enlarged sequents. At a disjunction or bottom inference, enlarge the premise by induction and repeat its last rule. This constructs a finite tree of the same shape. For an arbitrary inclusion take \(\Sigma=\Delta\), since \(\Gamma\cup\Delta=\Delta\).

Atomic identity suffices for every formula

For every NNF formula \(A\), there is a derivation of \(\{A,A^\perp\}\). Hence there is also a derivation of \(\Gamma,A,A^\perp\) for every finite \(\Gamma\).

Induct on \(A\). For either signed atom this is an atomic-identity leaf. For \(\bot\) or \(\top\), the sequent contains \(\top\), so is a truth leaf.

Suppose \(A=B\wedge D\). By induction and weakening, derive \(\{B,B^\perp,D^\perp\}\) and \(\{D,B^\perp,D^\perp\}\). Applying the disjunction rule to the two dual components in each sequent gives \(\{B,B^\perp\vee D^\perp\}\) and \(\{D,B^\perp\vee D^\perp\}\). The conjunction rule with common context \(\{B^\perp\vee D^\perp\}\) produces \(\{B\wedge D,B^\perp\vee D^\perp\}\), as desired.

If \(A=B\vee D\), weaken the two induction derivations to \(\{B,D,B^\perp\}\) and \(\{B,D,D^\perp\}\). The conjunction rule with context \(\{B,D\}\) gives \(\{B,D,B^\perp\wedge D^\perp\}\), and the disjunction rule gives \(\{B\vee D,B^\perp\wedge D^\perp\}\). Weakening supplies the final assertion in all cases. Thus compound identities are constructed with the five primitive rules.

Every derivation presents a valid sequent

If \(\mathsf C\vdash\Gamma\), then for every valuation \(v\) some \(A\in\Gamma\) satisfies \(\lVert A\rVert_v=1\). In particular, the empty sequent and the singleton \(\{\bot\}\) have no derivation.

Induct on the finite proof tree. At an atomic-identity leaf, exactly one of \(p,\bar p\) is true. At a truth leaf, \(\top\) is true. For the conjunction rule, if a member of the common context \(\Gamma\) is true, the conclusion is already satisfied. Otherwise the two premise conclusions force \(A\) and \(B\) to be true, so their conjunction is true. For the disjunction rule, the true premise member is either in \(\Gamma\) or one of \(A,B\); in the latter case \(A\vee B\) is true. The bottom rule preserves the already true member of its premise. This proves soundness for every rule.

No valuation satisfies the empty disjunction or the singleton falsity sequent. A derivation of either would contradict the assertion just proved, establishing both nonderivability claims.

For numbered atoms, the analytic completeness theorem gives the converse. Its specified search procedure takes an ordered finite presentation of a sequent and returns a primitive derivation or a countervaluation for that exact underlying set.

Move hypotheses to the other side by duality

For finite sets \(\Lambda,\Delta\) of ordinary formulas, define their encoded two-sided sequent by

\[ E(\Lambda;\Delta)=\{N^-(F):F\in\Lambda\}\cup\{N^+(G):G\in\Delta\}. \]

This sequent is valid exactly when every valuation satisfying all members of \(\Lambda\) satisfies at least one member of \(\Delta\). Therefore a derivation of \(E(\Lambda;\{G\})\) establishes \(\Lambda\models G\), and a derivation of \(\{N^+(G)\}\) establishes that \(G\) is a tautology.

Their disjunction thus says exactly that truth of every hypothesis forces truth of some conclusion. This equivalence includes empty sets: with no hypotheses the antecedent is true, while with no conclusions the consequent is false. The two derivability consequences follow from .
