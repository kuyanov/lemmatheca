Divide an infinite set into two infinite pieces

Work in ZFC. A set \(S\subseteq\omega\) splits an infinite set \(X\subseteq\omega\) if both \(X\cap S\) and \(X\setminus S\) are infinite. The even numbers split \(\omega\), but they do not split the set of even numbers. A splitter must leave infinitely many points on each side; merely meeting both sides is insufficient.

Failure to split means almost choosing one side

For infinite \(X\), a set \(S\) fails to split \(X\) exactly when \[X\subseteq^*S\quad\text{or}\quad X\subseteq^*(\omega\setminus S).\] Here almost containment permits finitely many exceptions. Indeed, the two alternatives say respectively that \(X\setminus S\) or \(X\cap S\) is finite. Consequently \(S\) splits \(X\) if and only if its complement does.

Split every infinite set, or resist every proposed splitter

Write \([\omega]^\omega\) for the infinite subsets of \(\omega\). A splitting family is a family \(\mathcal S\subseteq[\omega]^\omega\) such that every infinite \(X\subseteq\omega\) is split by some \(S\in\mathcal S\). A reaping family, also called an unsplittable family, is a family \(\mathcal R\subseteq[\omega]^\omega\) for which no single \(S\subseteq\omega\) splits every member. Thus \[\forall S\subseteq\omega\ \exists X\in\mathcal R: X\subseteq^*S\ \text{or}\ X\subseteq^*(\omega\setminus S).\] Define the splitting number \(\mathfrak s\) and the reaping number \(\mathfrak r\) as the least cardinalities of splitting and reaping families respectively.

The two kinds of family exist

The whole family \([\omega]^\omega\) is splitting: enumerate any infinite \(X\) without repetition and take its even-indexed points. It is also reaping: for any proposed \(S\), at least one of \(S\) and its complement is infinite, and that side is a member which \(S\) does not split. Since these witnesses exist, well-ordering cardinals gives attained minima \(\mathfrak s,\mathfrak r\). Both are at most \(\mathfrak c=2^{\aleph_0}\), the cardinality of \(\mathcal P(\omega)\).

Countably many sets can be split together and can be escaped

Every countable family of infinite subsets of \(\omega\) has a common splitter. Every countable family of subsets of \(\omega\) leaves some infinite set unsplit by all its members.

For simultaneous splitting, first handle a nonempty family as a sequence \(X_0,X_1,\ldots\), allowing repetitions. At stage \(n\), choose for every \(i\le n\) two points \(x_{n,i},y_{n,i}\in X_i\), with all chosen points distinct from one another and from all points used at earlier stages. Only finitely many points are forbidden at each choice. Put \(S=\{x_{n,i}:i\le n\}\). Every \(X_i\) contains infinitely many of these points and infinitely many of the reserved \(y_{n,i}\), which never enter \(S\). The empty family is split vacuously by any set.

For escape, take a nonprincipal ultrafilter \(U\) on \(\omega\). For each member \(S\), choose the one side of \(S,\omega\setminus S\) belonging to \(U\). These countably many chosen sides have infinite finite intersections, since \(U\) contains no finite set. Their infinite pseudointersection \(P\) is almost contained in a chosen side for every \(S\), so none splits \(P\). This also handles the empty family.

A bound on next points makes an alternating splitter

For infinite \(X\), let \(q_X(n)\) be its least point strictly greater than \(n\). Given \(g:\omega\to\omega\), define endpoints \[a_0=0,\qquad a_{k+1}=\max\{a_k+1,g(a_k)+1\},\] and let \(S_g\) be the union of the even-indexed intervals \([a_{2k},a_{2k+1})\). The endpoints increase strictly, so these nonempty consecutive intervals partition \(\omega\), and both \(S_g\) and its complement are infinite.

If \(q_X\le^*g\), then for every sufficiently large \(k\), \[a_k<q_X(a_k)\le g(a_k)<a_{k+1}.\] Hence \(X\) meets every sufficiently late interval. Infinitely many even intervals and infinitely many odd intervals contain points of \(X\), proving that \(S_g\) splits \(X\). The comparison is eventual because \(a_k\ge k\).

A dominating family supplies enough splitters

\(\mathfrak s\le\mathfrak d\). Choose a dominating family \(D\) of size \(\mathfrak d\). For every infinite \(X\), some \(g\in D\) eventually dominates \(q_X\). The preceding construction makes \(\{S_g:g\in D\}\) a splitting family of size at most \(|D|\), yielding the inequality.

A small family has one splitter

Every family \(\mathcal R\subseteq[\omega]^\omega\) of size less than \(\mathfrak b\) has a common splitter. Indeed, \(\{q_X:X\in\mathcal R\}\) also has size less than \(\mathfrak b\), so the definition of the bounding number supplies an eventual bound \(g\). The set \(S_g\) splits every member of \(\mathcal R\). Consequently \(\mathfrak b\le\mathfrak r\).

A small family of proposed splitters has an unsplit set

\(\mathfrak p\le\mathfrak s\). Let \(\mathcal S\subseteq[\omega]^\omega\) have size less than the pseudointersection number. Fix a nonprincipal ultrafilter \(U\), and select for each \(S\in\mathcal S\) its side belonging to \(U\). The selected family has size at most \(|\mathcal S|\) and all finite intersections are infinite. By the minimality of \(\mathfrak p\), it has an infinite pseudointersection \(P\). Each proposed splitter almost contains \(P\) or almost misses it; thus \(\mathcal S\) is not splitting.

An ultrafilter base resists every splitter

\(\mathfrak r\le\mathfrak u\). Any base \(\mathcal B\) for a nonprincipal ultrafilter \(U\) is reaping. Given \(S\subseteq\omega\), choose its side belonging to \(U\); some \(B\in\mathcal B\) is contained in that side. Every base member is infinite, and \(S\) cannot split this \(B\). A base of minimum size therefore witnesses the stated bound.

The splitting and reaping numbers sit in two chains

\[\aleph_1\le\mathfrak p\le\mathfrak s\le\mathfrak d\le\mathfrak c, \qquad\aleph_1\le\mathfrak b\le\mathfrak r\le\mathfrak u\le\mathfrak c.\]

The four new comparisons combine with the bounds for \(\mathfrak p,\mathfrak u\) and the bounds for \(\mathfrak b,\mathfrak d\). In particular both new minima are uncountable. Their definitions exchange which object is chosen first: a splitting family supplies many choices for one arbitrary infinite set, while a reaping family prevents one arbitrary choice from working for all its members.

Why is \(\{X\}\) never reaping when \(X\) is infinite?

Enumerate \(X\) without repetition and take its even-indexed points as \(S\). Its odd-indexed points remain outside \(S\), so both pieces are infinite. This defeats the singleton family's requirement.

What do these bounds imply under CH?

Assume \(\mathfrak c=\aleph_1\). What are \(\mathfrak s\) and \(\mathfrak r\)?

Show answerHide answer

Both equal \(\aleph_1\), since each lies between \(\aleph_1\) and \(\mathfrak c\). The bounds by themselves make no claim about possible unequal values when CH fails.
