Compare proofs in the same propositional language

Use the shared formulas over any atom set \(P\), with \(\bot,\neg,\land,\lor,\to\). Write \(\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm I}A\) for intuitionistic derivability, the existence of a finite proof in intuitionistic natural deduction. For a finite context, write \(\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm C}A\) for classical derivability in the existing classical calculus. Its extra rule derives \(A\) from a contradiction under temporary assumption \(\neg A\).

A negative translation will change the formulas, including the open assumptions, so that every classical proof has an intuitionistic counterpart. This does not make the extra rule intuitionistically valid on arbitrary unchanged formulas. Both proof relations here are syntactic: their assertions concern finite trees built from the specified rules.

Translate atoms and disjunctions under double negation

The Gödel–Gentzen negative translation used here sends a formula \(A\) to \(A^{\mathsf N}\), recursively as follows:

\[p^{\mathsf N}=\neg\neg p,\qquad \bot^{\mathsf N}=\bot,\qquad (\neg A)^{\mathsf N}=\neg A^{\mathsf N}.\]
\[(A\land B)^{\mathsf N}=A^{\mathsf N}\land B^{\mathsf N},\qquad (A\to B)^{\mathsf N}=A^{\mathsf N}\to B^{\mathsf N}.\]
\[(A\lor B)^{\mathsf N}=\neg\neg(A^{\mathsf N}\lor B^{\mathsf N}).\]

These are formulas in the original syntax, including its primitive negation. In particular, translating an implication does not insert a double negation around the entire implication. This translation interprets classical proofs constructively; it has a different purpose from the positive and negative Boolean translations into NNF.

Translate every allowed assumption as well

For a context \(\Gamma\), define its translated context by

\[\Gamma^{\mathsf N}:=\{B^{\mathsf N}:B\in\Gamma\}.\]

It is finite when \(\Gamma\) is finite, and translation commutes with inserting a temporary assumption:

\[(\Gamma\cup\{B\})^{\mathsf N}=\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\cup\{B^{\mathsf N}\}.\]

This equality follows directly from membership in an image set. It is essential when an implication, negation or disjunction rule discharges an assumption. Translation of a proof is not permission to leave its open premises untranslated.

Single out conclusions whose double negation can be removed

A formula \(C\) is stable if

\[\vdash_{\mathrm I}\neg\neg C\to C.\]

A closed proof of this implication works in every ambient context by weakening and implication elimination. Conversely, a deduction of \(C\) from the single assumption \(\neg\neg C\) gives the closed implication by discharge. Stability is a property of a particular formula, rather than the unrestricted double-negation-elimination schema.

Negation, conjunction and stable consequents preserve stability

Falsum and every negation are stable. A conjunction of stable formulas is stable. An implication is stable whenever its consequent is stable; no stability assumption on its antecedent is needed.

For falsum, \(\neg\bot\) has a closed proof: assume \(\bot\) and conclude that same contradiction. Thus \(\neg\neg\bot\) contradicts this theorem and gives \(\bot\). For a negation \(\neg D\), the needed implication is \(\neg\neg\neg D\to\neg D\), already proved by the triple-negation law.

Suppose \(C,D\) are stable and assume \(\neg\neg(C\land D)\). If \(\neg C\) held, any assumption \(C\land D\) would give \(C\) and hence a contradiction. This would prove \(\neg(C\land D)\), contradicting the original assumption. So \(\neg\neg C\), and stability gives \(C\). The same argument gives \(D\); conjunction introduction finishes.

Suppose \(D\) is stable and assume \(\neg\neg(C\to D)\). To prove the implication, temporarily assume \(C\). If \(\neg D\) held, a further assumption \(C\to D\) would give \(D\) and a contradiction. Thus \(\neg(C\to D)\), contradicting the outer assumption. We have obtained \(\neg\neg D\), so stability gives \(D\). Discharge \(C\), and then the original assumption.

Every translated formula is stable

For every propositional formula \(A\),

\[\vdash_{\mathrm I}\neg\neg A^{\mathsf N}\to A^{\mathsf N}.\]

Induct on \(A\), using . An atom translates to a negation, namely \(\neg(\neg p)\), so is stable. Falsum is stable. A primitive negation translates to a negation, and a disjunction translates to a double negation, so these cases also follow directly. For conjunction use the two induction hypotheses. For implication use the induction hypothesis for its consequent. These are all constructors of the shared syntax.

In particular, \(A^{\mathsf N}\) and \(\neg\neg A^{\mathsf N}\) imply one another intuitionistically: the other direction is double-negation introduction.

Classical truth and the occurring atom names are unchanged

For every Boolean valuation \(v\) and formula \(A\), translation preserves its value and its atom support:

\[v(A^{\mathsf N})=v(A),\qquad \operatorname{At}(A^{\mathsf N})=\operatorname{At}(A).\]

Consequently \(A\) and \(A^{\mathsf N}\) imply one another in the classical calculus.

Induct on the formula. Double negation leaves a Boolean value unchanged and introduces no atom. Falsum has no atoms and keeps its false value. The negation, conjunction and implication cases use the induction hypotheses in their defining clauses. For disjunction apply those hypotheses to its two children, then use Boolean double-negation elimination for the outside pair of negations. The support of each binary formula is the union of the two child supports.

Both \(A\to A^{\mathsf N}\) and \(A^{\mathsf N}\to A\) are therefore tautologies. Classical propositional completeness gives their closed classical proofs. This argument establishes classical equivalence; it does not turn these two implications into intuitionistic theorems.

Eliminate a double-negated disjunction into a stable conclusion

If \(C\) is stable and intuitionistic proofs give

\[\Delta\vdash_{\mathrm I}\neg\neg(X\lor Y),\qquad \Delta,X\vdash_{\mathrm I}C,\qquad \Delta,Y\vdash_{\mathrm I}C,\]

then \(\Delta\vdash_{\mathrm I}C\). Also a proof of \(X\), or a proof of \(Y\), gives a proof of \(\neg\neg(X\lor Y)\) in the same context.

For elimination, work temporarily under \(\neg C\). The two supplied branches, weakened to allow this extra assumption, turn an assumption \(X\) into a contradiction and an assumption \(Y\) into a contradiction. Thus they give \(\neg X\) and \(\neg Y\). If \(X\lor Y\) were assumed, disjunction elimination would yield a contradiction in either branch. This proves \(\neg(X\lor Y)\), contrary to the given double negation. Discharge \(\neg C\) to obtain \(\neg\neg C\), and apply stability. Every use of an extra ambient assumption is justified by weakening and proof replacement.

For introduction, introduce the appropriate disjunct to obtain \(X\lor Y\), and then use double-negation introduction. These constructions use only primitive intuitionistic rules.

Translate every finite classical proof

For every finite context \(\Gamma\) and formula \(A\),

\[\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm C}A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\vdash_{\mathrm I}A^{\mathsf N}.\]

The construction translates the given proof rule by rule, including all discharged assumptions.

Induct on the classical derivation. An assumption translates to an assumption in the image context. Conjunction introduction and either projection are reapplied to the translated premises. Implication introduction and elimination are likewise reapplied, using the translated-context insertion equality in the introduction case. Negation introduction and elimination use their original primitive rules because \((\neg B)^{\mathsf N}=\neg B^{\mathsf N}\) and \(\bot^{\mathsf N}=\bot\). Falsum elimination gives the translated conclusion from the translated contradiction.

A disjunction-introduction step uses the introduction part of . At disjunction elimination the induction hypotheses give \(\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\vdash_{\mathrm I}\neg\neg(B^{\mathsf N}\lor D^{\mathsf N})\) and branches deriving \(A^{\mathsf N}\) from each translated disjunct. The elimination part of that lemma applies because \(A^{\mathsf N}\) is stable.

Finally consider classical contradiction. Its branch derives \(\bot\) from \(\Gamma,\neg A\). By induction the translated branch derives \(\bot\) from \(\Gamma^{\mathsf N},\neg A^{\mathsf N}\). Negation introduction therefore gives \(\neg\neg A^{\mathsf N}\). The stability theorem gives \(A^{\mathsf N}\). This replaces the classical step by actual intuitionistic inferences and completes every rule case. No semantic completeness theorem is used to manufacture this translation.

The first-order extension treats both protected quantifier rules and logical equality, while retaining translated assumptions and actual finite proof trees.

The translated intuitionistic consequence is exactly classical consequence

For finite \(\Gamma\), the converse also holds:

\[\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm C}A\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\vdash_{\mathrm I}A^{\mathsf N}.\]

The forward direction is . Conversely, every intuitionistic rule is a classical rule, so read the given finite intuitionistic proof as a classical proof from \(\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\). This is the inclusion direction of the earlier classical-calculus bridge. For every \(B\in\Gamma\), its assumption and the closed classical implication \(B\to B^{\mathsf N}\) prove \(B^{\mathsf N}\) from \(\Gamma\). Replace each translated open assumption by this proof. Such replacement is admissible classically by the same proof-tree induction as intuitionistic replacement: under a temporary assumption keep that assumption as a leaf and weaken the replacements for ambient leaves. Classical contradiction is reapplied after transforming its branch. This gives \(\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm C}A^{\mathsf N}\). The closed implication \(A^{\mathsf N}\to A\) now gives \(A\).

In particular, classical theorems correspond exactly to intuitionistic theorems among translated formulas. The conclusion is about \(A^{\mathsf N}\), not arbitrary \(A\) unchanged.

For numbered-atom formulas, the negative translation is intuitionistically equivalent to double negation. The finite-premise Glivenko theorem therefore gives a corresponding formulation with pointwise double-negated assumptions.

Substitution commutes up to intuitionistic equivalence

Write \(C\simeq_{\mathrm I}D\) when both \(C\to D\) and \(D\to C\) have closed intuitionistic proofs. For a formula substitution \(\sigma\), put \(\sigma^{\mathsf N}(p):=(\sigma(p))^{\mathsf N}\). Then

\[(A[\sigma])^{\mathsf N}\simeq_{\mathrm I}(A^{\mathsf N})[\sigma^{\mathsf N}].\]

For an atom renaming \(f:P\to Q\), there is the stronger literal identity

\[(A[f])^{\mathsf N}=(A^{\mathsf N})[f],\]

where \(A[f]\) replaces each atom \(p\) by the atom \(f(p)\).

First note that intuitionistic equivalence is preserved by every connective. To transfer a negation, use the reverse implication between the inputs to turn any counter-assumption into the original contradiction. To transfer conjunction or disjunction, use the corresponding introduction and elimination rules on the two supplied equivalences. For an implication, convert its new antecedent into the old antecedent, apply the old implication, and convert its consequent into the new consequent. Reversing these constructions gives the other direction. In particular, equivalence is preserved under two negations.

Now induct on \(A\). In the atomic case the two displayed sides are \((\sigma(p))^{\mathsf N}\) and \(\neg\neg(\sigma(p))^{\mathsf N}\). They are equivalent by translated stability and double-negation introduction. Falsum is unchanged. The negation, conjunction and implication cases use connective congruence and the induction hypotheses; disjunction uses congruence followed by its two outside negations. This proves the equivalence. For a renaming, the atomic case is instead the literal equality \(\neg\neg f(p)=\neg\neg f(p)\), and every recursive clause preserves equality, proving the stronger statement.

Finite support extends the correspondence to arbitrary premise sets

For an arbitrary set \(\Gamma\), use the established classical convention: \(\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm C}A\) means that some finite \(D\subseteq\Gamma\) derives \(A\) in the finite-context calculus. Then

\[\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm C}A\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\vdash_{\mathrm I}A^{\mathsf N}.\]

A finite classical support \(D\subseteq\Gamma\) translates by the finite-context theorem, and weakening along \(D^{\mathsf N}\subseteq\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\) proves the forward direction. For the reverse, intuitionistic finite support gives a finite set \(E\subseteq\Gamma^{\mathsf N}\) supporting the translated proof. For each member of \(E\), choose one preimage in \(\Gamma\); let \(D\) be the finite set of those choices. Then \(D\subseteq\Gamma\) and \(E\subseteq D^{\mathsf N}\). Weaken to \(D^{\mathsf N}\), apply finite-context correspondence, and obtain \(D\vdash_{\mathrm C}A\). If \(E\) is empty, take \(D=\varnothing\). No injectivity of translation is needed.

See what becomes of a classical excluded-middle proof

For every formula \(A\),

\[(A\lor\neg A)^{\mathsf N}=\neg\neg(A^{\mathsf N}\lor\neg A^{\mathsf N}),\qquad \vdash_{\mathrm I}(A\lor\neg A)^{\mathsf N}.\]

The equality is the definition of translation, including its primitive-negation clause. Apply double-negated excluded middle to the formula \(A^{\mathsf N}\). More explicitly, assume \(\neg(A^{\mathsf N}\lor\neg A^{\mathsf N})\). An assumption \(A^{\mathsf N}\) contradicts it by left disjunction introduction, so \(\neg A^{\mathsf N}\) follows. Right disjunction introduction then contradicts the same assumption. Discharging it gives the displayed theorem.

This is the constructive content of translating the classical excluded-middle proof. For an atom \(p\), it says \(\vdash_{\mathrm I}\neg\neg(\neg\neg p\lor\neg\neg\neg p)\).

Can one remove the translation from a translated theorem?

If \(\vdash_{\mathrm I}A^{\mathsf N}\), must \(\vdash_{\mathrm I}A\)?

No. Take \(A=p_0\lor\neg p_0\). Its translation has the closed intuitionistic proof just given. The original formula has no intuitionistic proof: in the two-world chain countermodel, the root forces neither the atom nor its negation. Soundness therefore rules out a proof of their disjunction.

Translated stability only removes a double negation surrounding \(A^{\mathsf N}\). It does not identify \(A^{\mathsf N}\) with \(A\) intuitionistically. Classical equivalence is insufficient for that conclusion.

Does the construction need any atom at all?

What happens to the translation and proof theorem when the atom set is empty? Can the translated theorem then supply a proof of falsum?

Show answerHide answer

No atom is needed by the definition or any induction above. Formulas can still be built from \(\bot\) and connectives. For example, \((\bot\to\bot)^{\mathsf N}=\bot\to\bot\), which has the implication-identity proof, while \(\bot^{\mathsf N}=\bot\).

Neither calculus proves falsum without assumptions, by their Boolean soundness and its false Boolean value. Thus the translation of a classical theorem cannot turn into a closed proof of falsum. The exact consequence theorem continues to hold for the empty atom set; it makes no nonemptiness assumption.
