Compare functions after discarding an initial segment

Write \(\omega=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\), and let \(\omega^\omega\) be the set of all functions from \(\omega\) to \(\omega\). For \(f,g\in\omega^\omega\), eventual domination means

\[ f\le^*g\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists N\in\omega\;\forall n\ge N\;(f(n)\le g(n)). \]

The threshold \(N\) may depend on \(f\) and \(g\). This is a preorder: it is reflexive, and two successive comparisons can be combined by taking the larger threshold. Antisymmetry fails, since changing finitely many values preserves eventual domination in both directions.

We work in ZFC, using the axiom of choice, and identify cardinals with their initial ordinals. The comparisons themselves do not require choice; choice supplies the well-orderings and selections used below.

One bound and a family of bounds solve different problems

A family \(B\subseteq\omega^\omega\) is bounded if one function \(g\in\omega^\omega\) eventually dominates all its members. It is unbounded if no such function exists. A family \(D\subseteq\omega^\omega\) is dominating if every function is eventually dominated by some member of \(D\). Thus

\[ \begin{aligned} B\text{ is bounded}&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists g\in\omega^\omega\;\forall f\in B\;(f\le^*g),\\ B\text{ is unbounded}&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall g\in\omega^\omega\;\exists f\in B\;(f\not\le^*g),\\ D\text{ is dominating}&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall f\in\omega^\omega\;\exists g\in D\;(f\le^*g). \end{aligned} \]

For unboundedness, \(f\not\le^*g\) means that \(f(n)>g(n)\) at arbitrarily large inputs. For domination, the chosen member of \(D\) may depend on \(f\). The empty family is bounded, by any function, and is not dominating.

Every dominating family is unbounded. Indeed, if \(g\) bounded a dominating family \(D\), the function \(g^+(n)=g(n)+1\) would satisfy \(g^+\le^*h\) for some \(h\in D\). But then \(g^+\le^*h\le^*g\), contrary to \(g(n)+1>g(n)\) at every input.

Does using strict eventual inequality change these properties?

Write \(f<^*g\) when \(f(n)<g(n)\) for all sufficiently large \(n\). Would using \(<^*\) instead of \(\le^*\) change which families are bounded, unbounded, or dominating? Is \(f<^*g\) equivalent to \(f\le^*g\) together with \(g\not\le^*f\)?

Show answerHide answer

The three family properties do not change. A nonstrict bound \(g\) becomes a strict bound by replacing it with \(g^+(n)=g(n)+1\), and a strict bound is already a nonstrict bound. Consequently, unboundedness agrees too. If \(D\) is dominating for \(\le^*\), apply its definition to \(f^+(n)=f(n)+1\): some \(g\in D\) satisfies \(f^+\le^*g\), hence \(f<^*g\). The converse is immediate. The same family \(D\) works.

The two relations are nevertheless different. Let \(f(n)=0\), and let \(g(n)=1\) at even inputs and \(g(n)=0\) at odd inputs. Then \(f\le^*g\), while \(g\not\le^*f\) because the even inputs are unbounded. But \(f<^*g\) fails because equality occurs at every odd input. Strict eventual inequality asks for strict inequality throughout a tail, not merely infinitely often.

Measure the first sizes at which the two requirements can be met

The bounding number \(\mathfrak b\) and the dominating number \(\mathfrak d\) are

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathfrak b&=\min\{|B|:B\subseteq\omega^\omega\text{ is unbounded}\},\\ \mathfrak d&=\min\{|D|:D\subseteq\omega^\omega\text{ is dominating}\}. \end{aligned} \]

Both minima exist. The whole set \(\omega^\omega\) is dominating by reflexivity and is unbounded because, for any proposed bound \(g\), the function \(g^+\) defeats it. The candidate sizes therefore form nonempty sets of cardinals, which have least members.

In particular, every family of size less than \(\mathfrak b\) is bounded, and every family of size less than \(\mathfrak d\) fails to dominate. There are unbounded and dominating families of the respective minimum sizes. By , using strict eventual inequality gives the same two cardinals.

The set of natural-number functions has the size of the continuum

Put \(\mathfrak c=2^{\aleph_0}\), the cardinality of the real line. Then \(|\omega^\omega|=\mathfrak c\).

Proof

Every binary-valued function is natural-valued, so \(2^{\aleph_0}\le\aleph_0^{\aleph_0}\). Conversely, \(\aleph_0\le2^{\aleph_0}\), and the power laws give

\[ \aleph_0^{\aleph_0}\le (2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0} =2^{\aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0} =2^{\aleph_0}. \]

The final equality uses \(\aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0=\aleph_0\). The two bounds prove the claim. □

The bounding and dominating numbers lie between countability and the continuum

In ZFC,

\[ \aleph_1\le\mathfrak b\le\mathfrak d\le\mathfrak c. \]

Proof

Every countable family has a strict eventual bound, so no unbounded family is countable. Hence \(\mathfrak b\ge\aleph_1\), the least uncountable cardinal. Every dominating family is unbounded, as proved in , giving \(\mathfrak b\le\mathfrak d\). Finally, \(\omega^\omega\) itself is dominating and has size \(\mathfrak c\) by , so \(\mathfrak d\le\mathfrak c\). □

Fewer than the bounding number of bounded families still have a bound

If \((B_i)_{i\in I}\) is a family of bounded subsets of \(\omega^\omega\) and \(|I|<\mathfrak b\), then \(\bigcup_{i\in I}B_i\) is bounded.

Proof

For each \(i\), choose a bound \(g_i\) for \(B_i\). The family \(\{g_i:i\in I\}\) has size at most \(|I|<\mathfrak b\), so it has a bound \(g\). If \(f\in B_i\), transitivity gives \(f\le^*g_i\le^*g\). Thus \(g\) bounds the union. If \(I\) is empty, its union is empty and any function bounds it. □

There is no size restriction on each bounded family \(B_i\). What matters is the number of bounds that must be combined. Their eventual thresholds may depend on \(i\) and on \(f\).

Cofinality divides a large family into smaller initial pieces

If \(\kappa\) is an infinite cardinal and \(|X|=\kappa\), then \(X\) is the union of \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\) many subsets, each of size less than \(\kappa\).

Proof

List \(X=\{x_\alpha:\alpha<\kappa\}\) without repetitions, and put \(\mu=\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\), the least size of a cofinal subset. Choose an increasing cofinal sequence \((\gamma_i)_{i<\mu}\) in \(\kappa\). Define

\[ X_i=\{x_\alpha:\alpha<\gamma_i\}. \]

Since \(\gamma_i<\kappa\) and \(\kappa\) is initial, \(|X_i|=|\gamma_i|<\kappa\). Every \(x_\alpha\) appears in some \(X_i\): the limit property of infinite cardinals gives \(\alpha+1<\kappa\), and cofinality supplies \(\gamma_i\ge\alpha+1\). Therefore \(X=\bigcup_{i<\mu}X_i\). □

The pieces need not be disjoint. This is a cover indexed by cofinality, not a claim that their sizes have one common bound below \(\kappa\).

The bounding number is regular

In ZFC, \(\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak b)=\mathfrak b\).

Proof

Choose an unbounded family \(B\) of size \(\mathfrak b\). If \(\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak b)<\mathfrak b\), covers \(B\) by fewer than \(\mathfrak b\) pieces, each of size less than \(\mathfrak b\). Every piece is bounded by the definition of \(\mathfrak b\). Their union would be bounded by , contrary to the choice of \(B\). Since cofinality is always at most cardinality, equality follows. □

This says that \(\mathfrak b\) is a regular cardinal. Smaller bounded families cannot be combined in fewer than \(\mathfrak b\) stages to produce a minimum-sized unbounded family.

The dominating number cannot have cofinality below the bounding number

In ZFC, \(\mathfrak b\le\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak d)\). Combining the results gives

\[ \aleph_1\le\mathfrak b=\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak b) \le\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak d)\le\mathfrak d\le\mathfrak c. \]

Proof

Choose a dominating family \(D\) of size \(\mathfrak d\). Suppose \(\mu=\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak d)<\mathfrak b\). By , write \(D=\bigcup_{i<\mu}D_i\), with \(|D_i|<\mathfrak d\) for every \(i\). Each \(D_i\) fails to dominate, so choose \(f_i\in\omega^\omega\) such that

\[ \forall h\in D_i\quad f_i\not\le^*h. \]

The family \(\{f_i:i<\mu\}\) has size at most \(\mu<\mathfrak b\), so some \(g\) bounds it. Since \(D\) is dominating, choose \(h\in D\) with \(g\le^*h\). This \(h\) belongs to some \(D_i\), and then \(f_i\le^*g\le^*h\), contradicting the displayed requirement. Thus \(\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak d)\ge\mathfrak b\). The remaining comparisons follow from , , and \(\operatorname{cf}(\mathfrak d)\le\mathfrak d\). □

The conclusion bounds the cofinality of \(\mathfrak d\); it does not say that \(\mathfrak d\) is regular.

Can an eventually bounded family be arbitrarily large at each input?

For \(i,k\in\omega\), let \(s_{i,k}(i)=k\) and \(s_{i,k}(n)=0\) for \(n\ne i\). Consider \(S=\{s_{i,k}:i,k\in\omega\}\). Is \(S\) bounded for \(\le^*\)? Can it have a pointwise bound, satisfying \(s(n)\le g(n)\) for every \(s\in S\) and every \(n\)? Is \(S\) dominating?

Show answerHide answer

The zero function eventually bounds every spike: \(s_{i,k}(n)=0\) whenever \(n\ge i+1\). But a pointwise bound is impossible. For any proposed \(g\) and any input \(i\), the member \(s_{i,g(i)+1}\) exceeds \(g\) at \(i\). Eventual boundedness allows a separate finite exceptional set for each member.

The family is not dominating: the constant function \(1\) is not eventually dominated by any spike. This also follows from the fact that a dominating family must be unbounded.

What does the continuum hypothesis force these numbers to be?

Assume the continuum hypothesis, \(\mathfrak c=\aleph_1\). Determine \(\mathfrak b\) and \(\mathfrak d\). Without this additional assumption, does the inequality \(\mathfrak b\le\mathfrak d\) alone imply equality?

Show answerHide answer

Under CH, places both cardinals between \(\aleph_1\) and \(\mathfrak c=\aleph_1\), so \(\mathfrak b=\mathfrak d=\aleph_1\). An inequality alone does not assert equality. The arguments in this entry give bounds and a cofinality restriction; they do not determine which unequal values can occur.
