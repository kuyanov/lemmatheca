Use native formulas and actual single-conclusion trees

Fix a set of atom names with decidable equality. Work with the cut-free intuitionistic certificates on native formulas built from atoms, \(\bot,\neg,\land,\lor,\to\). Write \(\mathcal P(\Gamma,C)\) for the actual finite trees with antecedent \(\Gamma\) and sole conclusion \(C\). Antecedents are finite sets; \(\Gamma,A\) means \(\Gamma\cup\{A\}\). Left rules retain their principal assumption in every premise.

Let \(\operatorname{at}(A)\) be the finite set of atom names occurring in \(A\), and put \(\operatorname{At}(\Gamma)=\bigcup_{A\in\Gamma}\operatorname{at}(A)\). Each binary connective takes the union of its children's supports, while \(\operatorname{at}(\neg A)=\operatorname{at}(A)\) and \(\operatorname{at}(\bot)=\varnothing\). We abbreviate the native formula \(\bot\to\bot\) by \(\top\); it also has empty support. Its proof introduces the implication from the identity of \(\bot\).

Cover the antecedent by two possibly overlapping sides

For a supplied tree \(T\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,C)\), a covering split is a pair of finite antecedents \((L,R)\) satisfying \(\Gamma\subseteq L\cup R\). An exact split has \(\Gamma=L\cup R\). As in the earlier covering convention, overlap and extra assumptions are allowed.

At a left rule, test whether its principal formula belongs to \(L\). If it does, assign it to that side, even if it also belongs to \(R\); otherwise coverage puts it in \(R\). Temporary components are inserted into the selected side without deleting their parent. This preserves every retained assumption and works when a component was already present.

Return a formula, two factor trees, and their atom bound

An interpolation packet for \((L,R,C)\) consists of a native formula \(I\), two actual cut-free trees, and the support condition

\[L\Rightarrow I,\qquad R,I\Rightarrow C,\]
\[\operatorname{at}(I)\subseteq \operatorname{At}(L)\cap\bigl(\operatorname{At}(R)\cup\operatorname{at}(C)\bigr).\]

The concluding formula belongs on the right of this vocabulary comparison: the middle formula conveys what the left assumptions contribute to the remaining assumptions and conclusion. The recursion below specifies all three pieces of data. The factor trees use precisely the established intuitionistic rules, with one conclusion at every node and native negation retained.

Project, apply, and paste already supplied factors

We may enlarge a tree's antecedent by actual weakening and compose two supplied trees by the proved cut-admissibility operation. Both operations return cut-free trees.

To obtain \(J\) from an assumed \(J\land K\), apply conjunction-left to the identity of \(J\) in the context enlarged by \(J,K\). The other projection is identical with the identity of \(K\). Given a supplied tree of \(\Theta,J\Rightarrow D\), paste the first projection into it to get \(\Theta,J\land K\Rightarrow D\), weakening either input as required. This is the existing cut operation applied to actual inputs.

To apply an assumed \(J\to K\), supply a tree of \(J\) and use implication-left with a continuation under \(K\). To use an assumed \(\neg J\), supply a tree of \(J\) and use negation-left. Introducing \(J\lor K\) from either supplied disjunct uses the corresponding right rule. We will name these constructions when assembling factors; none adds a new primitive inference.

Start the extraction at identity and falsum

At an atomic identity leaf concluding \(p\), choose \(I=p\) if \(p\in L\), and choose \(I=\top\) otherwise. At a falsum-left leaf concluding any \(C\), choose \(I=\bot\) if \(\bot\in L\), and choose \(I=\top\) otherwise.

In the first identity case, identity gives \(L\Rightarrow p\) and \(R,p\Rightarrow p\). The atom \(p\) occurs in \(L\) and in the conclusion, so its support is permitted. In the other case, coverage gives \(p\in R\). Prove \(L\Rightarrow\top\) by implication-right and falsum identity; the right factor is the atomic identity already available in \(R,\top\).

If falsum is on the left, falsum identity proves \(L\Rightarrow\bot\), and falsum-left proves \(R,\bot\Rightarrow C\). Otherwise it belongs to \(R\): use the same proof of \(\top\) on the left and falsum-left on the right. Both constant choices have empty support. The first membership test also resolves overlapping sides deterministically.

Restore a retained conjunction on its selected side

Suppose the last rule is conjunction-left, with \(D=A\land B\in\Gamma\) and premise \(\Gamma,A,B\Rightarrow C\). If \(D\in L\), recurse with sides \((L,A,B;R)\); otherwise recurse with \((L;R,A,B)\). Keep the returned interpolant \(I\).

In the left case the first factor is \(L,A,B\Rightarrow I\). Conjunction-left, using \(D\in L\), gives \(L\Rightarrow I\). The second factor already is \(R,I\Rightarrow C\). In the right case, reapply conjunction-left to the second factor \(R,A,B,I\Rightarrow C\), using \(D\in R,I\); the first factor is unchanged.

The atoms of \(A,B\) already occur in their parent on the selected side. Thus adding them changes neither that side's vocabulary nor the required common-atom bound. No occurrence of the principal assumption has been erased from a premise.

Combine two right conclusions by a conjunction

For conjunction-right, extract from the premise trees \(\Gamma\Rightarrow A\) and \(\Gamma\Rightarrow B\) with the same sides \((L,R)\), obtaining \(J,K\). Set \(I=J\land K\).

The first factors \(L\Rightarrow J\) and \(L\Rightarrow K\) combine by conjunction-right. For the second factor, project \(J\) from \(I\) and paste into the first premise's factor \(R,J\Rightarrow A\). This gives \(R,I\Rightarrow A\). Project and paste \(K\) to obtain \(R,I\Rightarrow B\), then conclude \(R,I\Rightarrow A\land B\).

Both supports lie in \(\operatorname{At}(L)\). Their other bounds lie respectively in \(\operatorname{At}(R)\cup\operatorname{at}(A)\) and \(\operatorname{At}(R)\cup\operatorname{at}(B)\). Their union therefore lies in the required right vocabulary for \(A\land B\). For either disjunction-right rule, keep its sole premise interpolant and apply the same disjunction-right rule to its second factor. The conclusion's support contains that of its chosen disjunct.

Use a disjunction when the disjunction assumption is on the left

Let \(D=A\lor B\in L\) be the principal assumption of a disjunction-left step. Recurse on its two premises with sides \((L,A;R)\) and \((L,B;R)\), obtaining \(J,K\). Set \(I=J\lor K\).

The first child gives \(L,A\Rightarrow J\); introduce the left disjunct to get \(L,A\Rightarrow I\). The second gives \(L,B\Rightarrow K\); introduce the right disjunct to get \(L,B\Rightarrow I\). Disjunction-left with the retained \(D\in L\) concludes \(L\Rightarrow I\).

The other child factors are \(R,J\Rightarrow C\) and \(R,K\Rightarrow C\). Weaken both to retain \(I\), and use disjunction-left on this added assumption \(I=J\lor K\) to obtain \(R,I\Rightarrow C\). The atoms of each temporary assumption \(A\) or \(B\) already occur in \(L\) through \(D\), so each child interpolant has the final common-atom bound. Their disjunction takes the union of those supports.

Use a conjunction when that assumption is on the right

If the same principal \(D=A\lor B\) is not in \(L\), it belongs to \(R\). Recurse with sides \((L;R,A)\) and \((L;R,B)\), obtaining \(J,K\), and set \(I=J\land K\).

Conjunction-right joins the two first factors into \(L\Rightarrow I\). For the right factor, disjunction-left on \(D\in R,I\) requires proofs of \(R,I,A\Rightarrow C\) and \(R,I,B\Rightarrow C\). In the first branch project \(J\) from \(I\), then paste into the child factor \(R,A,J\Rightarrow C\). In the second branch project \(K\), then paste into \(R,B,K\Rightarrow C\). Reapply disjunction-left.

Each child support lies in \(\operatorname{At}(L)\), and adding \(A\) or \(B\) to \(R\) adds no atom because \(D\in R\). Thus both child supports, and their union, satisfy the required right bound. This proves both factors for the specified conjunction, including coincident child formulas.

Reverse the first split when the implication is on the left

Suppose implication-left uses \(D=A\to B\in L\), with premise trees \(\Gamma\Rightarrow A\) and \(\Gamma,B\Rightarrow C\). On the first premise recurse with the reversed sides \((R;L)\). On the continuation use \((L,B;R)\). Write their outputs as \(J,K\). Their factors are

\[R\Rightarrow J,\qquad L,J\Rightarrow A,\]
\[L,B\Rightarrow K,\qquad R,K\Rightarrow C.\]

Set \(I=J\to K\).

For \(L\Rightarrow J\to K\), introduce \(J\). The first premise factor supplies \(A\) under \(L,J\); the continuation factor, weakened by \(J\), supplies \(K\) under \(L,J,B\). Apply implication-left using the retained \(D\in L,J\), then discharge \(J\). This constructs the first factor.

For \(R,I\Rightarrow C\), use implication-left on \(I=J\to K\). Its antecedent proof is \(R\Rightarrow J\), weakened by \(I\); its continuation is \(R,K\Rightarrow C\), also weakened by \(I\). Thus the second factor follows by one genuine implication-left step.

The reversed child gives \(\operatorname{at}(J)\subseteq\operatorname{At}(R)\cap(\operatorname{At}(L)\cup\operatorname{at}(A))\). Since \(D\in L\), both \(\operatorname{at}(A)\) and \(\operatorname{at}(B)\) lie in \(\operatorname{At}(L)\). Hence \(J\)'s atoms lie on both final sides. The continuation's bound for \(K\) already is the final bound after absorbing \(B\) into \(L\)'s vocabulary. Their union is exactly the support of \(J\to K\). Reversing the split supplies the direction \(L,J\Rightarrow A\) needed to introduce this implication.

Keep the first split when the implication is on the right

If the principal \(D=A\to B\) is not in \(L\), it belongs to \(R\). Recurse on the antecedent premise with \((L;R)\), giving \(J\), and on the continuation with \((L;R,B)\), giving \(K\). Set \(I=J\land K\).

Combine \(L\Rightarrow J\) and \(L\Rightarrow K\) by conjunction-right. For the second factor work in \(R,I\). Project \(J\) from \(I\) and paste it into the first child factor \(R,J\Rightarrow A\), obtaining the antecedent of \(D\). For the continuation under \(B\), project \(K\) from \(I\) and paste it into \(R,B,K\Rightarrow C\). These give the two premises of implication-left using \(D\in R,I\), hence \(R,I\Rightarrow C\).

The bound for \(J\) is \(\operatorname{At}(L)\cap(\operatorname{At}(R)\cup\operatorname{at}(A))\). Here \(A\)'s atoms already occur in \(R\) through \(D\). The same absorption removes the temporary \(B\) from the continuation's vocabulary bound for \(K\). Therefore their conjunction has exactly the required common-vocabulary inclusion.

Follow the polarity of a native negation assumption

At negation-left, the premise proves \(\Gamma\Rightarrow A\), the principal \(D=\neg A\) belongs to \(\Gamma\), and the conclusion is any \(C\). If \(D\in L\), recurse with reversed sides \((R;L)\), obtaining \(J\), and set \(I=\neg J\).

The child factors are \(R\Rightarrow J\) and \(L,J\Rightarrow A\). For the first output, introduce \(J\), apply negation-left using \(\neg A\in L,J\) to obtain falsum, and conclude \(L\Rightarrow\neg J\) by negation-right. For the second output, weaken \(R\Rightarrow J\) by \(\neg J\), then apply negation-left to derive \(R,\neg J\Rightarrow C\). Because \(A\)'s atoms already lie in \(L\), the reversed child bounds \(J\) by \(\operatorname{At}(R)\cap\operatorname{At}(L)\); negation preserves that support.

If \(D\notin L\), recurse normally with \((L;R)\) and keep \(I=J\). Its factors are \(L\Rightarrow J\) and \(R,J\Rightarrow A\). Negation-left with \(D\in R,J\) turns the latter into \(R,J\Rightarrow C\). The extra \(\operatorname{at}(A)\) in the child bound is already contained in \(\operatorname{At}(R)\). These constructions use the native negation rules on the displayed parse trees.

Put each discharged assumption on the right

At implication-right concluding \(A\to B\), recurse on \(\Gamma,A\Rightarrow B\) with sides \((L;R,A)\) and keep its interpolant \(I\). At negation-right concluding \(\neg A\), use the same sides for its premise \(\Gamma,A\Rightarrow\bot\), again keeping \(I\).

The first factor stays \(L\Rightarrow I\). The second factor in the implication case is \(R,A,I\Rightarrow B\). Reorder the finite antecedent and introduce the implication, discharging \(A\), to obtain \(R,I\Rightarrow A\to B\). For negation, the second factor proves \(R,A,I\Rightarrow\bot\); negation-right yields \(R,I\Rightarrow\neg A\).

For implication the child's right vocabulary is \(\operatorname{At}(R)\cup\operatorname{at}(A)\cup\operatorname{at}(B)\), which is the vocabulary of the final right assumptions and conclusion. For negation it is \(\operatorname{At}(R)\cup\operatorname{at}(A)\), the same final vocabulary. The left support bound is unchanged.

Specify the recursion on the supplied proof tree

Define \(\mathsf M(T;L,R)\) by the preceding clauses, with the conclusion of \(T\) understood. At a leaf use . At conjunction-left use . At conjunction-right or either disjunction-right use . At disjunction-left choose or by the principal's membership in \(L\). At implication-left choose or by the same test. Use at negation-left, and at either remaining right rule.

Each recursive call visits a strict premise subtree and quantifies afresh over its covering sides. In the two reversing cases, the child conclusion is the antecedent \(A\), so the child packet's support bound also uses that actual new conclusion. Every other change to the split is an explicit insertion of premise assumptions. The recursion returns the displayed formula together with the two constructed trees; all pasting invokes the specified cut-free composition operation.

Factor every covered intuitionistic certificate

For every supplied \(T\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,C)\) and covering split \(\Gamma\subseteq L\cup R\), the specified operation \(\mathsf M(T;L,R)\) returns an interpolation packet. In particular its two trees and formula satisfy

\[L\Rightarrow I,\qquad R,I\Rightarrow C,\]
\[\operatorname{at}(I)\subseteq \operatorname{At}(L)\cap\bigl(\operatorname{At}(R)\cup\operatorname{at}(C)\bigr).\]

Induct on the actual finite input tree, proving the assertion for every covering pair at each node. Coverage places a left principal on at least one side. The deterministic test selects a valid case, and the enlarged or reversed sides cover every premise. The induction hypotheses supply the child packets with their correct conclusions. Each reconstruction proved above returns both required factor trees and preserves the support bound.

The listed cases exhaust all eleven constructors of the cut-free calculus. Strict subtree descent proves termination; the size of a tree returned by admissible composition is irrelevant, because the extractor never recurses on that returned tree. Thus the theorem concerns the actual data produced by this recursion on the supplied input.

Remove intermediate cuts before extracting

For an actual certificate with cut \(T\) of \(\Gamma\Rightarrow C\), define its packet by \(\mathsf M(\operatorname{Elim}(T);L,R)\), using the earlier cut-removal transformation.

The transformation returns an actual cut-free tree with exactly the same antecedent and conclusion. Therefore the original covering condition still applies, and returns both factors with the stated vocabulary. The middle formulas used by the original cuts may contain other atoms; extraction is run after those cuts have been removed.

Obtain a common-atom factor for intuitionistic deduction

If \(\{A\}\vdash_{\mathrm I} B\), there exists a native formula \(I\) such that

\[\{A\}\vdash_{\mathrm I} I,\qquad\{I\}\vdash_{\mathrm I} B,\]
\[\operatorname{at}(I)\subseteq\operatorname{at}(A)\cap\operatorname{at}(B).\]

The established native-deduction correspondence supplies the existence of a cut-free tree for \(\{A\}\Rightarrow B\). For each such tree, extract with \(L=\{A\}\) and \(R=\varnothing\). Its actual factor trees are \(\{A\}\Rightarrow I\) and \(\{I\}\Rightarrow B\). The same correspondence reads both as intuitionistic deductions, and the packet's bound simplifies to the displayed intersection.

This establishes the existential assertion from native derivability. The more specific operation in takes an already supplied cut-free tree and returns its formula and factor trees. Both results use the shared native syntax directly; there is no additional translation of the interpolant's atom names or negation.

Follow the reversed split in a concrete implication use

For distinct atoms \(p,q\), let \(T\) prove \(\{p\to q,p\}\Rightarrow q\) by implication-left. Its antecedent premise is the atomic identity of \(p\); its continuation is the atomic identity of \(q\) in the context additionally containing \(q\).

Take \(L=\{p\to q\}\), \(R=\{p\}\). The implication is selected into \(L\), so the first child uses the reversed sides \((R;L)\). Its atomic leaf returns exactly \(J=p\). In the continuation, the sides are \((L,q;R)\), so its leaf returns \(K=q\). The specified output is exactly \(I=p\to q\). The left factor is the expanded identity of that implication, and the right factor applies it to the assumed \(p\).

For the same input tree with \(L=\{p\}\), \(R=\{p\to q\}\), the implication is selected into \(R\). The first leaf returns \(J=p\); the continuation's \(q\) lies only on its right side, so that leaf returns \(K=\top\). This time the extractor returns exactly \(p\land\top\). Its left factor introduces the conjunction, while the right factor projects \(p\) and uses the retained implication. Its support is \(\{p\}\), precisely the common vocabulary permitted by this second split.

What can the middle formula say without any shared atom?

Find atom-free interpolants for \(\{\bot\}\vdash_{\mathrm I}q\) and \(\{p\}\vdash_{\mathrm I}\top\). What does the theorem require when the endpoint atom sets are disjoint?

For the first deduction choose \(I=\bot\): the first factor is falsum identity and the second is falsum-left. This is also the prescribed output at the corresponding leaf with \(L=\{\bot\}\), \(R=\varnothing\). For the second choose \(I=\top=\bot\to\bot\). Its first factor is the implication-right proof of \(\top\), and its second is its expanded identity. Both chosen formulas have empty atom support.

In general, disjoint endpoint supports make the theorem's intersection empty, so the returned formula contains no atom names. It may still use the logical constant and connectives, as these two examples show. The theorem supplies its two intuitionistic factors as well as this syntactic restriction.

Does choosing the left side on overlap make the interpolant unique?
