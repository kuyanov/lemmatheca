Separate cut-free trees from composition

Fix an atom set \(P\), the native formulas with \(\bot,\neg,\land,\lor,\to\), and finite antecedents \(\Gamma\). A cut-free certificate for \(\Gamma\Rightarrow C\) is a finite tree generated by the rules in this definition. Write \(\mathcal P(\Gamma,C)\) for the collection of these trees and \(\Gamma\vdash_0 C\) for its nonemptiness.

There are two leaf rules: \(\Gamma\Rightarrow p\) when the atom \(p\) belongs to \(\Gamma\), and \(\Gamma\Rightarrow C\) when \(\bot\in\Gamma\). The right rules introduce conjunction, either disjunct, implication or native negation:

\[\frac{\Gamma\Rightarrow A\quad\Gamma\Rightarrow B}{\Gamma\Rightarrow A\land B},\qquad \frac{\Gamma\Rightarrow A}{\Gamma\Rightarrow A\lor B},\qquad \frac{\Gamma\Rightarrow B}{\Gamma\Rightarrow A\lor B}.\]
\[\frac{\Gamma,A\Rightarrow B}{\Gamma\Rightarrow A\to B},\qquad \frac{\Gamma,A\Rightarrow\bot}{\Gamma\Rightarrow\neg A}.\]

The left rules retain their principal assumption in their premises. Their side conditions and premises are:

\[\frac{A\land B\in\Gamma\quad\Gamma,A,B\Rightarrow C}{\Gamma\Rightarrow C},\qquad \frac{A\lor B\in\Gamma\quad\Gamma,A\Rightarrow C\quad\Gamma,B\Rightarrow C}{\Gamma\Rightarrow C}.\]
\[\frac{A\to B\in\Gamma\quad\Gamma\Rightarrow A\quad\Gamma,B\Rightarrow C}{\Gamma\Rightarrow C},\qquad \frac{\neg A\in\Gamma\quad\Gamma\Rightarrow A}{\Gamma\Rightarrow C}.\]

These are the entire rule list. There is no cut or weakening constructor. Set antecedents identify repeated assumptions, but the separate uses of an assumption in different branches remain separate parts of the tree. This retained-principal presentation permits reuse of a compound assumption without an additional contraction rule.

The equality-free first-order extension keeps these constructive rules and adds protected universal and existential steps.

Weaken a tree and expand a compound identity

If \(\Gamma\subseteq\Delta\), a certificate in \(\mathcal P(\Gamma,C)\) gives one in \(\mathcal P(\Delta,C)\). For every formula \(A\in\Gamma\), there is a certificate in \(\mathcal P(\Gamma,A)\).

For weakening, recurse on the given tree. Membership leaves remain valid in the larger context. Right introduction recurses into the larger context with the same temporary assumption. For a left rule, its principal formula remains available, while the premise context is enlarged by exactly the same one or two component formulas. Reapply that rule to the recursively changed premises. This preserves the tree's logical rule arrangement.

For identity, induct on \(A\). An atom is an initial leaf; falsum uses falsum-left. For \(A=B\land D\), use conjunction-left, identities of \(B,D\) in the augmented context, then conjunction-right. For a disjunction, use disjunction-left and introduce the corresponding disjunct in each branch. For \(B\to D\), introduce the implication; under the additional \(B\), implication-left uses identities of \(B\) and \(D\). For \(\neg B\), introduce negation; under \(B\), negation-left applied to its identity derives falsum. Every recursive identity concerns a strict component.

Change the conclusion of a falsum certificate

A certificate in \(\mathcal P(\Gamma,\bot)\) gives one in \(\mathcal P(\Gamma,C)\) for every \(C\).

Induct on the supplied tree. Its last rule cannot be atomic identity or any right introduction, because their conclusions have a different outer symbol. A falsum-left leaf already permits \(C\). For conjunction-left, recurse on its premise; for disjunction-left, recurse on both branches. At implication-left retain the first premise proving its antecedent and recurse on the continuation proving falsum. Negation-left directly permits \(C\). These are all possible last rules, so this operation adds no cut.

Expose the components of a right conclusion

There are certificate transformations

\[\mathcal P(\Gamma,A\land B)\longrightarrow \mathcal P(\Gamma,A)\times\mathcal P(\Gamma,B),\]
\[\mathcal P(\Gamma,A\to B)\longrightarrow\mathcal P(\Gamma,A,B),\qquad \mathcal P(\Gamma,\neg A)\longrightarrow\mathcal P(\Gamma,A,\bot).\]

Here \(\mathcal P(\Gamma,A,B)\) abbreviates \(\mathcal P(\Gamma\cup\{A\},B)\).

Induct on each input tree. If the last rule introduces the displayed outer connective on the right, take the relevant premise or premises. At falsum-left use the same falsum assumption, weakening its context when a temporary assumption is required. At every left rule commute the inversion upwards: recursively invert each premise with that same right conclusion, keep an implication-left antecedent premise unchanged, and reapply the left rule. For implication and negation, weaken the unchanged antecedent premise to allow the new temporary assumption. Negation-left can directly give the required new conclusion. No other right introduction can have the displayed outer symbol, and atomic identity cannot do so. Thus all cases either expose a premise or recurse on a smaller tree.

State a replacement invariant with all its contexts

For a formula \(A\), the cut-replacement operation \(K_A\) has the following input and output:

\[p\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,A),\quad q\in\mathcal P(\Delta,C),\quad \Delta\subseteq\Gamma\cup\{A\}\quad\longmapsto\quad K_A(p,q)\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,C).\]

The inclusion is part of the input: every allowed assumption of the consumer \(q\) is either already available in \(\Gamma\), or is the cut formula \(A\). This stronger invariant permits recursion below a right introduction or a left rule. If a premise introduces an additional \(D\), replace the producer by its weakening to \(\Gamma,D\), so the premise inclusion becomes \(\Delta,D\subseteq\Gamma,D,A\).

We construct \(K_A\) by induction on \(A\). At that stage only replacement operations for strict components may be used. The auxiliary recursion on the consumer tree treats every nonprincipal rule; a further recursion on a producer tree treats a principal disjunction. These structural recursions, followed by strict descent in the cut formula, give the termination argument.

Move replacement through a consumer tree

Suppose that the principal reductions in the four following blocks are available for \(A\), together with the falsum case. Then recursion on \(q\) defines \(K_A\) with the invariant in .

At an atomic identity leaf, its concluding atom belongs to \(\Delta\). If it equals \(A\), return \(p\); otherwise it belongs to \(\Gamma\), so use the same identity leaf. At falsum-left, falsum is either already in \(\Gamma\), giving a falsum-left leaf, or is \(A\), in which case use on \(p\).

A right rule first transforms its smaller premises, augmenting \(\Gamma\) and weakening \(p\) under a temporary assumption, and then reapplies that rule. Consider a left rule with principal formula \(D\in\Delta\). If \(D\in\Gamma\), transform its smaller premises in their corresponding augmented contexts and reapply the same left rule using \(D\). If \(D\notin\Gamma\), the input inclusion forces \(D=A\). The recursively transformed premises now have only the component assumptions of \(A\) beyond \(\Gamma\); apply the appropriate principal reduction below.

For example, implication-left has premises proving its antecedent and its continuation. Recursing gives a proof of the antecedent from \(\Gamma\), and a proof of \(C\) from \(\Gamma\) plus the implication's consequent. The retained original implication has been removed from both premises by the same consumer recursion. This is why simply cutting on a component before removing that retained assumption would not suffice. Each recursive consumer call uses a strict subtree, regardless of how much the produced tree later grows.

Replace a conjunction by its two components

Assume that \(K_B,K_D\) have been constructed. If \(p\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B\land D)\) and \(r\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B,D,C)\), then a certificate of \(\Gamma\Rightarrow C\) can be constructed without cutting on \(B\land D\).

Invert \(p\) to obtain \(p_B\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B)\) and \(p_D\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,D)\). Weaken \(p_B\) to \(\Gamma,D\) and apply \(K_B\) to \(r\), yielding \(\Gamma,D\Rightarrow C\). Apply \(K_D\) with \(p_D\) to remove the remaining \(D\). These replacements concern strict components, so their output heights do not affect the outer formula induction.

First supply an implication's input, then replace its output

Assume \(K_B,K_D\). Given \(p\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B\to D)\), \(b\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B)\), and \(r\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,D,C)\), there is a certificate of \(\Gamma\Rightarrow C\).

Right inversion changes \(p\) into \(\Gamma,B\Rightarrow D\). Replacement \(K_B\), using \(b\), gives \(d\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,D)\). Replacement \(K_D\), using \(d\) and \(r\), gives \(C\). In the consumer recursion the original \(B\to D\) has already been removed from the antecedent premise and continuation. Thus this two-step reduction does not rely on an occurrence of the implication surviving above the cut.

The order of the two smaller replacements matters: the first proves the consequent needed by the second. This is the asymmetric case that prevents importing the classical multiple-conclusion argument without modification.

Reduce native negation without changing its meaning

Assume \(K_B\). Given \(p\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,\neg B)\) and \(b\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B)\), there is a certificate of \(\Gamma\Rightarrow C\) for any \(C\).

Right inversion of \(p\) gives \(\Gamma,B\Rightarrow\bot\). Apply \(K_B\) using \(b\), then the syntactic explosion transformation. The replacement formula has become the strict component \(B\). No identification of the two parse trees \(\neg B\) and \(B\to\bot\) is needed.

Follow a disjunction producer to its introducing branch

Assume \(K_B,K_D\). From \(p\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B\lor D)\), \(r_B\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,B,C)\), and \(r_D\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,D,C)\), a certificate of \(\Gamma\Rightarrow C\) can be constructed.

Recurse on the producer \(p\), carrying both continuations. If its last rule introduces the left disjunct, use \(K_B\) to put its premise into \(r_B\); for the right disjunct use \(K_D\) and \(r_D\). At falsum-left reuse the same falsum assumption. At negation-left reuse its contradictory antecedent proof and choose conclusion \(C\).

If the last rule is a different left rule, move the operation into its smaller premises with conclusion \(B\lor D\), weaken both continuations to the premise context, and reapply that left rule. For conjunction-left there is one such premise, and for disjunction-left there are two. For implication-left keep its first premise proving the principal implication's antecedent and recurse on its continuation. Atomic identity and all other right introductions cannot conclude this disjunction. These exhaust the constructors. The recursion therefore reaches an introducing branch or a rule already permitting \(C\); it does not require a choice of disjunct from an arbitrary open proof.

Compose two cut-free certificates

For every formula \(A\), the operation \(K_A\) in exists. In particular there is an operation

\[\mathcal P(\Gamma,A)\times\mathcal P(\Delta,A,C) \longrightarrow\mathcal P(\Gamma\cup\Delta,C).\]

Induct on the finite formula \(A\). For an atom there is no matching compound principal left rule, so consumer recursion needs only its identity-leaf replacement. For falsum use syntactic explosion when the removed assumption is falsum. For conjunction, implication, native negation and disjunction, the induction hypotheses supply the strict-component replacement operations used in their respective principal reductions. Feed those reductions to the consumer recursion. Every recursive descent is either inside a fixed finite tree or into a strict component of the cut formula, as stipulated in the invariant.

For the displayed split-context version, weaken the first input to \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\), and use \(\Delta,A\subseteq\Gamma\cup\Delta,A\) as the consumer inclusion. The construction returns an actual tree in the original cut-free rules.

Remove every primitive cut

A certificate with cut uses the rules of and the additional rule \(\Gamma\Rightarrow A,\ \Delta,A\Rightarrow C\,/\,\Gamma\cup\Delta\Rightarrow C\). Every such certificate can be transformed into a cut-free certificate with the same antecedent and succedent.

Recurse on the finite certificate with cut. Transform each premise first. At a logical inference reapply its primitive cut-free rule to the transformed premises. At a cut, apply to the two transformed premise trees. The recursive calls are made on the original strict subtrees; the potentially larger tree returned by cut replacement is not fed back into this outer recursion. Thus neither a proof-height-decrease assertion nor a strong-normalization theorem is required.

In the equality-free first-order extension, the same outer recursion uses quantified replacements that preserve protected parameters and the consumer's actual witness terms.

Recover exactly the established intuitionistic calculus

For every finite \(\Gamma\) and native formula \(C\),

\[\Gamma\vdash_0 C\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm{LJ}}C\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma\vdash_{\mathrm I}C.\]

A cut-free retained-principal rule translates to the established split-context LJ calculus. For a conjunction-left premise, apply its old left rule with the entire retained context; its conclusion inserts a conjunction already present, hence is the same context. For disjunction-left the same observation applies. For implication-left, both ambient contexts may be chosen as \(\Gamma\); their union is \(\Gamma\), and the principal implication is already a member. The other rules translate immediately, while compound identity is already available in old LJ.

Conversely, induct on an old LJ derivation. Translate right rules directly. For an old left rule, weaken the translated premises to retain the displayed principal assumption, then apply the corresponding new rule. For old implication-left use the union of the two premise antecedents, weakening each appropriate premise to it; set membership supplies the principal implication. Old weakening uses the admissible weakening operation, and old compound identity uses identity expansion. Old cut uses the certificate replacement theorem. Thus this direction is a syntactic rule-by-rule construction, including the original primitive cuts.

The established LJ/natural-deduction equivalence gives the last equivalence. Native negation has been retained in every rule and every translation.

A supplied cut-free tree can be turned into a normal natural-deduction verification by interpreting its assumptions as neutral uses.

The same actual trees support intuitionistic interpolant extraction: splitting the antecedent produces a middle formula, two cut-free factor proofs and a bound by the shared atom names. Its implication-left case reverses the antecedent-premise split when that implication belongs to the first side.

Relate native negation to its implication expansion

Let \(e\) be the recursive expansion replacing \(\neg A\) by \(e(A)\to\bot\). Then

\[\Gamma\vdash_0 C\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e[\Gamma]\vdash_0 e(C).\]

The presentation theorem translates either side into native intuitionistic deduction. The already proved expansion equivalence transfers the derivation, including all its assumptions; translate back with the same presentation theorem. Its underlying constructive proof replaces the two native negation rules by implication introduction or elimination with falsum, and uses the proved equivalence of each formula with its expansion in the reverse direction. Thus the bridge relates these particular syntaxes and premise contexts, rather than only their Boolean meanings.

Track formulas through native negation

The analytic closure \(S(A)\) consists of the ordinary native subformulas of \(A\), together with \(\bot\). Explicitly, \(S(p)=\{p,\bot\}\), \(S(\bot)=\{\bot\}\), \(S(\neg A)=\{\neg A\}\cup S(A)\), and \(S(A\mathbin\circ B)=\{A\mathbin\circ B\}\cup S(A)\cup S(B)\) for each binary connective. For a sequent put

\[S(\Gamma,C)=S(C)\cup\bigcup_{A\in\Gamma}S(A).\]

Including falsum matters because native negation-right has falsum as its premise conclusion, even though the separate symbol \(\neg\) has only \(A\) as its native child. Equivalently, one may first expand negations and track the ordinary subformulas of the expanded formulas. We use the explicit native closure here.

Every formula of a cut-free tree belongs to its end closure

If \(p\in\mathcal P(\Gamma,C)\), every formula displayed in any antecedent or succedent of \(p\) belongs to \(S(\Gamma,C)\). Consequently, every derivable finite sequent has a proof with this analytic property.

For each primitive rule, the analytic closure of a premise sequent is contained in that of its conclusion. A conjunction or disjunction rule adds only components of its principal formula. Implication-right adds its antecedent to the context and concludes its consequent; both are components of the concluding implication. Implication-left uses its antecedent as a premise conclusion and adds its consequent to the continuation context; both lie in the closure of the principal implication already in the end antecedent. The negation cases add its child and possibly falsum, which the closure explicitly contains.

Starting at the root, compose these inclusions along the finite path to any node. Every formula at that node belongs to its own sequent closure and therefore to the root closure. The presentation theorem supplies a cut-free certificate for any derivable finite sequent, proving the consequence.

There is no closed proof of falsum

There is no certificate in \(\mathcal P(\varnothing,\bot)\), and neither old LJ nor intuitionistic natural deduction proves falsum without assumptions.

Inspect the last rule of a proposed cut-free certificate. There is no right introduction with conclusion falsum. Atomic identity has an atomic conclusion. Every remaining rule requires a principal formula or falsum in the empty antecedent, which is impossible. The presentation equivalences transfer this nonexistence to the other two calculi.

A principal implication cut becomes two smaller replacements

For all formulas \(A,B\), the context \(\Gamma=\{A,A\to B\}\) has a cut-free certificate of \(B\), and the principal replacement construction gives the same conclusion when composing the identity of \(A\to B\) with its use.

Identity expansion gives \(\Gamma\Rightarrow A\) and \(\Gamma,B\Rightarrow B\). Implication-left, using \(A\to B\in\Gamma\), concludes \(\Gamma\Rightarrow B\). If we instead regard that displayed implication as the cut formula, right inversion of its identity gives \(\Gamma,A\Rightarrow B\). The principal reduction first replaces \(A\) by its identity proof, producing \(B\), then replaces the temporary \(B\) in its continuation by that proof. This is a concrete instance of the two strict-component operations, with no claim that their resulting trees are uniquely determined.

Why does a larger replacement tree not break termination?

Suppose an implication's principal reduction produces a much taller proof. Why is it still legitimate to use that proof in the second smaller cut?

Show answerHide answer

For cut formula \(A\to B\), both component operations \(K_A\) and \(K_B\) have already been constructed by the outer induction on formulas. They apply to every pair of finite input trees, with no height bound. The second replacement therefore uses the already available \(K_B\), even if the proof of \(B\) returned by the first replacement is tall. At a fixed cut formula, the auxiliary recursions inspect strict subtrees of their original consumer or producer. These are different induction parameters, so arbitrary height growth at the smaller formula level does not create a loop.
