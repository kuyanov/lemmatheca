A cofinal subset reaches past every earlier position

A subset \(C\) of an ordinal \(\alpha\) is cofinal if for every \(\beta<\alpha\) there is \(\gamma\in C\) with \(\beta\le\gamma\). This concerns positions in the order, rather than the size of all its points. We assume the axiom of choice throughout this entry and identify cardinals with their initial ordinals.

The cofinality \(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\) is the least cardinality of a cofinal subset of \(\alpha\). It exists: \(\alpha\) itself is cofinal, so the candidate sizes form a nonempty set of cardinals at most \(|\alpha|\). Equivalently, it is the least cardinal \(\mu\) admitting a function \(\mu\to\alpha\) with cofinal range, since a cofinal subset can be enumerated and the range of such a function has size at most \(\mu\).

Thus \(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\le|\alpha|\). The empty subset is cofinal in zero, so \(\operatorname{cf}(0)=0\). A successor \(\beta+1\) has cofinal singleton \(\{\beta\}\), so \(\operatorname{cf}(\beta+1)=1\).

At a nonzero limit, cofinal means having the whole limit as supremum

For a nonzero limit ordinal \(\alpha\), a subset \(C\subseteq\alpha\) is cofinal exactly when \(\sup C=\alpha\). Every such cofinal subset is infinite.

Proof

If \(C\) is cofinal, any \(\beta<\alpha\) has \(\beta+1<\alpha\), and some \(\gamma\in C\) satisfies \(\beta+1\le\gamma\). Hence no \(\beta<\alpha\) bounds \(C\), and its supremum is \(\alpha\). Conversely, a failure of cofinality would give a \(\beta<\alpha\) larger than every member of \(C\), forcing \(\sup C\le\beta\). A nonempty finite subset has a greatest element, which cannot be cofinal in a limit; the empty subset cannot be cofinal either. □

In particular, \(\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\ge\aleph_0\) for nonzero limits. The supremum characterization fails for successors: the cofinal singleton \(\{\beta\}\) in \(\beta+1\) has supremum \(\beta\), not \(\beta+1\).

Very different limits can have the same cofinality

Every nonzero countable limit ordinal has cofinality \(\aleph_0\). In particular, \(\operatorname{cf}(\omega)=\operatorname{cf}(\omega^2)=\operatorname{cf}(\omega^\omega)=\aleph_0\).

Proof

The lower bound is , and the ordinal itself is a countable cofinal subset, giving the upper bound. For explicit examples, the natural positions are cofinal in \(\omega\), the starts \(\omega\cdot n\) of successive blocks are cofinal in \(\omega^2\), and the powers \(\omega^n\) are cofinal in \(\omega^\omega\). Each sequence has the asserted supremum by the corresponding limit rule. □

A limit has a strictly increasing cofinal sequence of minimal size

If \(\alpha\) is a nonzero limit and \(\mu=\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\), there is a strictly increasing function \(g:\mu\to\alpha\) with cofinal range. Every subset of \(\alpha\) of size less than \(\mu\) is bounded below \(\alpha\).

Proof

A smaller subset is not cofinal by minimality of \(\mu\). A failure of cofinality supplies a \(\beta<\alpha\) strictly above all its members. Start with any cofinal map \(f:\mu\to\alpha\). Recursively put

\[ g(i)=\max\left(f(i),\sup_{j<i}\bigl(g(j)+1\bigr)\right). \]

Every \(g(j)+1\) is below \(\alpha\), since \(\alpha\) is a limit. The set of earlier values has size at most \(|i|<\mu\), because \(\mu\) is an infinite initial ordinal. The boundedness just proved ensures that its supremum is also below \(\alpha\). Thus the recursion always produces a value in \(\alpha\), including at zero where the empty supremum is zero. It gives \(g(j)<g(i)\) for \(j<i\), and \(g(i)\ge f(i)\) makes its range cofinal. □

Cofinality itself cannot be approached with fewer stages

If \(\alpha\) is a nonzero limit and \(\mu=\operatorname{cf}(\alpha)\), then \(\operatorname{cf}(\mu)=\mu\).

Proof

Take the increasing cofinal map \(g:\mu\to\alpha\) from . If a cofinal set \(D\subseteq\mu\) had size less than \(\mu\), then \(g[D]\) would be cofinal in \(\alpha\): given \(\beta<\alpha\), choose \(i\) with \(\beta\le g(i)\), then \(d\in D\) with \(i\le d\), and use \(g(i)\le g(d)\). This contradicts the definition of \(\mu\). Therefore \(\operatorname{cf}(\mu)\ge\mu\); the reverse bound follows because \(\mu\) itself is cofinal. □

The increasing condition matters here: an arbitrary function with cofinal range need not remain cofinal when restricted to a cofinal subset of its domain.

Regular cardinals resist shorter approaches

An infinite cardinal \(\kappa\) is regular if \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)=\kappa\), and singular if \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)<\kappa\). Every infinite initial ordinal is a limit ordinal, so its cofinality is infinite. For a regular cardinal, every subset of size less than \(\kappa\) is bounded below \(\kappa\); this boundedness also implies regularity.

The cardinal \(\aleph_0\) is regular: a finite subset of \(\omega\) is bounded, whereas \(\omega\) itself is cofinal. By , the cofinality of every nonzero limit is a regular cardinal.

A cardinal successor differs from an ordinal successor

The successor cardinal \(\kappa^+\) is the least cardinal strictly greater than \(\kappa\). It exists: Cantor’s theorem supplies a larger cardinal, and we can take the least among the larger cardinals up to that bound. Every ordinal \(\beta<\kappa^+\) has \(|\beta|\le\kappa\), since its cardinality is a cardinal below \(\kappa^+\).

Starting with \(\aleph_0\), put \(\aleph_{n+1}=\aleph_n^+\) for natural \(n\), and define \(\aleph_\omega=\sup_{n<\omega}\aleph_n\). This supremum is a cardinal. More generally, if \(K\) is a nonempty set of cardinals and \(\rho=\sup K\) were not initial, its cardinality \(\nu<\rho\) would lie below some \(\kappa\in K\). But inclusion \(\kappa\subseteq\rho\) would give \(\kappa\le|\rho|=\nu\), a contradiction.

For infinite \(\kappa\), the ordinal successor \(\kappa+1\) has the same cardinality as \(\kappa\), whereas \(\kappa^+\) is strictly larger. In particular, \(\kappa^+\) is a limit ordinal, despite being a successor among cardinals.

Every successor of an infinite cardinal is regular

If \(\kappa\) is an infinite cardinal, then \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa^+)=\kappa^+\).

Proof

If \(C\subseteq\kappa^+\) were cofinal and \(|C|<\kappa^+\), then \(|C|\le\kappa\). Each \(\gamma\in C\) has size at most \(\kappa\); choice lets us select injections \(e_\gamma:\gamma\to\kappa\). Since \(\kappa^+\) is a limit, cofinality gives \(\bigcup C=\kappa^+\). Send each \(x<\kappa^+\) to \((\gamma,e_\gamma(x))\), where \(\gamma\) is the least member of \(C\) containing \(x\). This injects \(\kappa^+\) into \(C\times\kappa\), whose size is at most \(\kappa\cdot\kappa=\kappa\) by the square theorem. This contradicts \(\kappa^+>\kappa\). □

The first limit of the aleph sequence is singular

\(\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\omega)=\aleph_0<\aleph_\omega\). Among infinite cardinals, \(\aleph_\omega\) is the first singular one.

Proof

The increasing sequence \(\aleph_0,\aleph_1,\ldots\) has supremum \(\aleph_\omega\), so it is cofinal there. No finite subset is cofinal in this limit ordinal, giving cofinality exactly \(\aleph_0\). The supremum is strictly above every \(\aleph_n\), in particular above \(\aleph_0\). Any infinite cardinal below it lies below some \(\aleph_n\), and the definition of consecutive cardinal successors then places it among the earlier finite-index alephs. These are regular by and . □

Regularity means smaller unions stay smaller

An infinite cardinal \(\kappa\) is regular exactly when every family \((A_i)_{i\in I}\) with \(|I|<\kappa\) and \(|A_i|<\kappa\) for all \(i\) satisfies \(\left|\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i\right|<\kappa\).

Proof

Suppose \(\kappa\) is regular. For \(\kappa=\aleph_0\), this is a finite union of finite sets. For uncountable \(\kappa\), the set of cardinals \(\{|A_i|:i\in I\}\) has size less than \(\kappa\), so it is bounded by some ordinal \(\eta<\kappa\). The infinite cardinal \(\nu=\max(\aleph_0,|I|,|\eta|)\) is below \(\kappa\) and bounds every \(|A_i|\). Choice supplies injections of the sets into \(\nu\), and a well-order on \(I\) chooses the least index containing each point of the union. This injects the union into \(I\times\nu\), of size at most \(\nu^2=\nu<\kappa\). An empty family has empty union.

Conversely, if \(\kappa\) is singular, take a cofinal \(C\subseteq\kappa\) of size \(\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)<\kappa\). Regard its members as sets of earlier ordinals. Each has cardinality less than \(\kappa\), but \(\bigcup_{\gamma\in C}\gamma=\kappa\), because \(\kappa\) is a limit. This family violates the union property. □

Does a common size bound change the union problem?

Can countably many sets of size less than \(\aleph_\omega\) have a union of size \(\aleph_\omega\)? What if they all have size at most one fixed \(\aleph_m\)?

Yes: take the sets \(A_n=\aleph_n\). Their union is \(\aleph_\omega\). But with a fixed bound \(\aleph_m\), choice and least-index coding inject the union into \(\omega\times\aleph_m\), which has size \(\aleph_m<\aleph_\omega\). The singular example succeeds because the sizes of the sets have no common cardinal bound below \(\aleph_\omega\).
