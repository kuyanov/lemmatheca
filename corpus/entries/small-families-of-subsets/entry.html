Three ways a family can resist a new choice

Work in ZFC. Write \(A\subseteq^*B\) when \(A\setminus B\) is finite, and \(\mathfrak c=2^{\aleph_0}\). Recall strong finite intersections and pseudointersections: a family \(\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal P(\omega)\) has the strong finite intersection property if every finite intersection is infinite; an infinite \(P\subseteq\omega\) is a pseudointersection if \(P\subseteq^*F\) for every \(F\in\mathcal F\). The empty intersection is \(\omega\).

The pseudointersection number \(\mathfrak p\) is the least size of such a family with no infinite pseudointersection. The almost disjointness number \(\mathfrak a\) is the least size of an infinite maximal almost disjoint family of infinite subsets of \(\omega\). Maximality means that no further infinite subset can be adjoined while retaining pairwise finite intersections.

A base for an ultrafilter \(U\) is a subfamily \(\mathcal B\subseteq U\) such that every \(X\in U\) contains a member of \(\mathcal B\). The ultrafilter number \(\mathfrak u\) is the least size of a base for a nonprincipal ultrafilter on \(\omega\). Such an ultrafilter contains every cofinite set and no finite set, by the principal-ultrafilter characterization. Both restrictions in italics are part of the definitions.

A free ultrafilter base has no infinite pseudointersection

If \(U\) is nonprincipal and \(\mathcal B\) is a base for \(U\), then \(\mathcal B\) has the strong finite intersection property and has no infinite pseudointersection. The same holds for the family \(U\) itself.

Proof

A finite intersection of members of \(\mathcal B\) belongs to \(U\), so it is infinite. Suppose an infinite \(P\) were almost contained in every base member. It would then be almost contained in every \(X\in U\), because \(X\) contains a base member.

Enumerate \(P\) increasingly and divide its even and odd indexed points into disjoint infinite sets \(C,D\). The ultrafilter contains either \(C\) or \(\omega\setminus C\). In the first case \(P\setminus C\) contains \(D\); in the second, \(P\setminus(\omega\setminus C)\) contains \(C\). Either case contradicts almost inclusion.

□

The defining families exist and the minima are attained

Each of \(\mathfrak p,\mathfrak a,\mathfrak u\) is a well-defined cardinal, attained by a witnessing family, and each is at most \(\mathfrak c\).

Proof

A nonprincipal ultrafilter exists. It is its own base; by it also witnesses that the definition of \(\mathfrak p\) has candidates. Both families are subsets of \(\mathcal P(\omega)\), so their sizes are at most \(\mathfrak c\). The continuum-sized maximal almost disjoint family supplies an infinite candidate for \(\mathfrak a\).

The attained sizes form nonempty sets of cardinals. A nonempty set of cardinals has a least member, so each minimum is attained. Consequently a strong-finite-intersection family of size less than \(\mathfrak p\) has an infinite pseudointersection.

□

All three numbers are uncountable

\(\aleph_1\le\mathfrak p,\mathfrak a,\mathfrak u\le\mathfrak c\).

Proof

Every countable family with strong finite intersections has a countable diagonal pseudointersection, so \(\mathfrak p\ge\aleph_1\). A countably infinite almost disjoint family can be extended, so it cannot attain \(\mathfrak a\). Finally a free ultrafilter has no countable base. The upper bounds were proved in .

□

Pseudointersections give bounds for small families of functions

\(\mathfrak p\le\mathfrak b\), where \(\mathfrak b\) is the bounding number.

Proof

Let \(\mathcal H\subseteq\omega^\omega\) have size less than \(\mathfrak p\). Replace \(f\in\mathcal H\) by its nondecreasing majorant \(\widehat f(n)=\max_{i\le n}f(i)\). On the countable set \(\omega\times\omega\), form

\[X_f=\{(n,m):m\ge\widehat f(n)\},\qquad V_k=\{(n,m):n\ge k\}.\]

These sets have strong finite intersections: above any fixed column threshold, choose a point above the finitely many required graphs. The family has size at most \(\max(|\mathcal H|,\aleph_0)<\mathfrak p\). Transfer it to \(\omega\) using a bijection with the countable product, and take an infinite pseudointersection \(P\).

For each \(k\), only finitely many points of \(P\) lie below column \(k\). Its column indices are therefore unbounded. Choose points \((n_j,m_j)\in P\) with strictly increasing \(n_j\). For each \(n\), let \(j(n)\) be the first index with \(n\le n_{j(n)}\), and set \(g(n)=m_{j(n)}\). Fix \(f\in\mathcal H\). Almost inclusion in \(X_f\) gives \(m_j\ge\widehat f(n_j)\) for all sufficiently large \(j\). Since \(j(n)\) tends to infinity, eventually

\[f(n)\le\widehat f(n)\le\widehat f(n_{j(n)})\le m_{j(n)}=g(n).\]

Thus every family of size less than \(\mathfrak p\) is bounded, which gives the asserted comparison.

□

A family smaller than the bounding number cannot be infinite and maximal

\(\mathfrak b\le\mathfrak a\).

Proof

Let \(\mathcal A\) be an infinite almost disjoint family of size less than \(\mathfrak b\). Choose distinct \(C_n\in\mathcal A\), and put \(D_n=C_n\setminus\bigcup_{i<n}C_i\). Each \(D_n\) is infinite, because finitely many finite intersections were removed; the \(D_n\)'s are pairwise disjoint. Enumerate \(D_n\) increasingly as \(d_n(k)\).

For every \(A\in\mathcal A\setminus\{C_n:n<\omega\}\), choose \(f_A(n)\) so that \(d_n(k)\notin A\) for \(k\ge f_A(n)\). This is possible since \(A\cap D_n\) is finite.

There are fewer than \(\mathfrak b\) functions \(f_A\). Take a common eventual bound \(g\), and set \(x_n=d_n(g(n)+1)\). The infinite set \(X=\{x_n:n<\omega\}\) has at most finitely many points in each remaining \(A\), by the bound on \(f_A\). For each \(i\), the definition of \(D_n\) excludes \(C_i\) whenever \(n>i\), so \(X\cap C_i\subseteq\{x_0,\ldots,x_i\}\). Hence \(X\) can be adjoined to \(\mathcal A\). The family was not maximal.

□

Ultrafilter bases bound the pseudointersection number

\(\mathfrak p\le\mathfrak u\).

Proof

Every nonprincipal ultrafilter base is a strong-finite-intersection family without a pseudointersection by . Apply the definition of \(\mathfrak p\) to a base attaining \(\mathfrak u\).

□

Together the proved comparisons give \(\aleph_1\le\mathfrak p\le\mathfrak b\le\mathfrak a\le\mathfrak c\) and \(\mathfrak p\le\mathfrak u\le\mathfrak c\).

What happens if the restrictions are removed?

Why insist that the maximal almost disjoint family be infinite and that the ultrafilter be nonprincipal?

Show answerHide answer

The family \(\{\omega\}\) is maximal almost disjoint and has size one: every infinite subset meets its sole member infinitely. A principal ultrafilter at \(n\) has the one-element base \(\{\{n\}\}\). Removing either restriction would replace the corresponding invariant by one, losing the uncountable obstruction.

What do the comparisons give under CH?

If \(\mathfrak c=\aleph_1\), what are \(\mathfrak p,\mathfrak a,\mathfrak u\)?

Show answerHide answer

All three equal \(\aleph_1\) by their lower and upper bounds. The inequalities alone do not construct different values when CH fails or prove any independence assertion.
