An ideal specifies which subsets count as small

Fix a set \(X\). An ideal of subsets is a family \(I\subseteq\mathcal P(X)\) such that

\[ \varnothing\in I,\qquad A\subseteq B\in I\Longrightarrow A\in I,\qquad A,B\in I\Longrightarrow A\cup B\in I. \]

Thus a subset of a small set is small, and combining finitely many small sets still gives a small set. This is an ideal in the power-set Boolean algebra. It is proper when \(X\notin I\). If \(X\in I\), downward closure gives \(I=\mathcal P(X)\).

Examples are \(\{\varnothing\}\), the ideal \(\mathcal P(S)\) of subsets of a fixed \(S\subseteq X\), and

\[ \operatorname{Fin}(X)=\{A\subseteq X:A\text{ is finite}\}. \]

The last family is an ideal because subsets and finite unions of finite sets are finite. It is proper exactly when \(X\) is infinite. An ideal need not be closed under countable unions. The quotient construction developed here uses no axiom of choice.

Sets are equivalent when their difference is small

The symmetric difference of \(A,B\subseteq X\) is

\[ A\mathbin\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A). \]

Define \(A\sim_I B\) to mean \(A\mathbin\triangle B\in I\). This is an equivalence relation. Its equivalence classes are

\[ [A]_I=\{B\subseteq X:A\mathbin\triangle B\in I\},\qquad \mathcal P(X)/I=\{[A]_I:A\subseteq X\}. \]

Proof

Reflexivity uses \(A\mathbin\triangle A=\varnothing\in I\), and symmetry uses \(A\mathbin\triangle B=B\mathbin\triangle A\). For transitivity,

\[ A\mathbin\triangle C\subseteq (A\mathbin\triangle B)\cup(B\mathbin\triangle C). \]

Indeed, a point at which \(A\) and \(C\) disagree must disagree between at least one of the two intervening pairs. Union closure and downward closure put the left side in \(I\) whenever both differences on the right belong to \(I\). □

Can an arbitrary collection of small sets replace an ideal?

On \(X=\{0,1\}\), let \(\mathcal S=\{\varnothing,\{0\},\{1\}\}\). Does the rule \(A\mathbin\triangle B\in\mathcal S\) define an equivalence relation?

Show answerHide answer

No. The sets \(\{0\}\) and \(\varnothing\) are related, as are \(\varnothing\) and \(\{1\}\). But \(\{0\}\mathbin\triangle\{1\}=X\notin\mathcal S\), so transitivity fails. The family contains the empty set and is downward closed, but it lacks the union closure needed in the proof.

Set operations give candidate operations on classes

We want to define meet, join, complement, and the two bounds by

\[ \begin{aligned} [A]_I\wedge[B]_I&=[A\cap B]_I,\\ [A]_I\vee[B]_I&=[A\cup B]_I,\\ [A]_I^c&=[X\setminus A]_I,\\ 0&=[\varnothing]_I,\\ 1&=[X]_I. \end{aligned} \]

A class can have many representatives. These formulas define operations only if replacing representatives by equivalent sets leaves the resulting classes unchanged, the same issue addressed by maps from quotient sets.

Changing representatives changes the answer only on a small set

All the operations in are well-defined.

Proof

Suppose \(A\sim_I A'\) and \(B\sim_I B'\), and put \(E=(A\mathbin\triangle A')\cup(B\mathbin\triangle B')\in I\). Outside \(E\), membership in each input agrees with membership in its replacement. Membership in the union or intersection therefore also agrees. Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} (A\cap B)\mathbin\triangle(A'\cap B')&\subseteq E,\\ (A\cup B)\mathbin\triangle(A'\cup B')&\subseteq E. \end{aligned} \]

Downward closure puts both differences in \(I\). For complements, the exact identity

\[ (X\setminus A)\mathbin\triangle(X\setminus A')=A\mathbin\triangle A' \]

gives the result. The fixed classes \(0,1\) need no representative choices. □

Containment of classes means that the excess is small

Define the order on classes by \(u\le v\) when \(u\wedge v=u\). For representatives,

\[ [A]_I\le[B]_I\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\setminus B\in I. \]

Proof

The definition says \([A\cap B]_I=[A]_I\), and \((A\cap B)\mathbin\triangle A=A\setminus B\). It is independent of representatives because meet is well-defined.

Reflexivity follows from the empty difference. Transitivity follows from \(A\setminus C\subseteq(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus C)\). If both \([A]_I\le[B]_I\) and \([B]_I\le[A]_I\), then the union of their two small differences is \(A\mathbin\triangle B\), so the classes are equal. Thus this is a partial order.

The class \([A\cap B]_I\) is a lower bound of \([A]_I,[B]_I\). Any other lower bound \([C]_I\) satisfies

\[ C\setminus(A\cap B)=(C\setminus A)\cup(C\setminus B)\in I, \]

so it lies below \([A\cap B]_I\). Dually, \([A\cup B]_I\) is the least upper bound, using \((A\cup B)\setminus C=(A\setminus C)\cup(B\setminus C)\). Finally, \(0\le[A]_I\le1\) because both relevant differences are empty. □

Ignoring an ideal of errors preserves the Boolean algebra

For every ideal \(I\subseteq\mathcal P(X)\), the quotient \(\mathcal P(X)/I\) is a Boolean algebra with the operations in and the order in . The projection \(A\mapsto[A]_I\) is a surjective Boolean homomorphism.

Proof

The quotient order and pairwise bounds give a bounded lattice. The distributive laws follow by taking classes of the usual set identities. Likewise, \(A\cap(X\setminus A)=\varnothing\) and \(A\cup(X\setminus A)=X\) give the complement laws. This is precisely the Boolean-algebra definition. The projection preserves the two bounds, meet, join, and complement by their definitions, so it is a Boolean homomorphism. It is surjective because every quotient element is a class of some subset. □

The zero class consists exactly of the small sets

For every \(A\subseteq X\),

\[ [A]_I=0\Longleftrightarrow A\in I,\qquad [A]_I=1\Longleftrightarrow X\setminus A\in I. \]

Consequently the quotient is nontrivial exactly when \(I\) is proper.

Proof

Use \(A\mathbin\triangle\varnothing=A\) and \(A\mathbin\triangle X=X\setminus A\). In particular, \(0=1\) exactly when \(X\in I\). In that case \(I=\mathcal P(X)\), so every set represents zero and the quotient has one element. If \(I\) is proper, the two bound classes are distinct. When \(X=\varnothing\), every ideal is improper and this one-element case is unavoidable. □

What remains when all errors lie inside one fixed region?

Let \(S\subseteq X\) and \(I=\mathcal P(S)\). What information does \([A]_I\) retain?

Show answerHide answer

It retains exactly \(A\setminus S\), because \(A\mathbin\triangle B\subseteq S\) if and only if \(A\setminus S=B\setminus S\). Thus \([A]_I\mapsto A\setminus S\) is a well-defined bijection onto \(\mathcal P(X\setminus S)\); its inverse sends \(T\subseteq X\setminus S\) to \([T]_I\). It preserves intersections, unions, both bounds, and complements in \(X\setminus S\), so it is a Boolean isomorphism. If \(S=X\), nothing remains and the quotient has one element. If \(S=\varnothing\), no two different subsets are identified.

Modulo finite sets, the quotient order is almost containment

Take \(X=\mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\) and \(I=\operatorname{Fin}(\mathbb N)\), abbreviated \(\operatorname{Fin}\). Write \([A]\) for \([A]_{\operatorname{Fin}}\). The familiar finite-error equality and almost containment now become equality and order of classes:

\[ [A]=[B]\Longleftrightarrow A=^*B,\qquad [A]\le[B]\Longleftrightarrow A\subseteq^*B. \]

The zero class consists of all finite sets. A class is nonzero exactly when each, equivalently any, representative is infinite. The class \(1\) consists of the cofinite sets. In particular this quotient is nontrivial. The even numbers represent a class different from both \(0\) and \(1\), since both they and their complement are infinite.

Removing finite differences supplies the antisymmetry missing from almost containment on actual subsets: two classes below each other are equal even if their representatives are different sets.

Almost disjoint sets have disjoint nonzero classes

For infinite \(A,B\subseteq\mathbb N\),

\[ A\cap B\text{ is finite}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[A]\wedge[B]=0. \]

This translates almost disjointness into disjointness of nonzero Boolean elements, where disjoint means that their meet is zero.

More generally, an almost disjoint family gives distinct nonzero classes with pairwise zero meets. Distinctness is essential: if \([A]=[B]\) and their meet is zero, their common class is zero, contradicting infinitude. Conversely, choosing one representative of each member of a family of distinct nonzero classes with pairwise zero meets gives an almost disjoint family. Each chosen representative is infinite and each pairwise intersection is finite; choosing representatives for an arbitrary family here may use choice.

Must zero meet mean that the displayed representatives are disjoint?

Let \(E\) and \(O\) be the even and odd numbers, and put \(E'=E\cup\{1\}\). Compare their classes and intersections.

Show answerHide answer

The sets \(E,E'\) differ at one point, so \([E]=[E']\). Although \(E\cap O=\varnothing\) and \(E'\cap O=\{1\}\), both intersections represent zero. Thus the meet is unchanged. Zero meet means that the intersection is small; it does not require the particular representatives to have empty intersection.

Every nonzero finite-error class splits into smaller nonzero pieces

An atom of a Boolean algebra is a nonzero element \(q\) whose only elements below it are \(0\) and \(q\). An algebra is atomless when it has no atoms.

For every nonzero \(q\in\mathcal P(\mathbb N)/\operatorname{Fin}\), there are nonzero \(u,v\) with \(u\wedge v=0\) and \(u\vee v=q\). In particular, this Boolean algebra is atomless.

Proof

Take an infinite representative \(A\) of \(q\) and enumerate it increasingly as \(a_0,a_1,\ldots\). Partition it into

\[ C=\{a_{2n}:n\in\mathbb N\},\qquad D=\{a_{2n+1}:n\in\mathbb N\}. \]

Both sets are infinite, they are disjoint, and their union is \(A\). Therefore \(u=[C]\) and \(v=[D]\) have the stated properties. Also \(0<[C]<[A]\): containment gives the non-strict inequality, and \(A\setminus C=D\) is infinite, so their classes differ. Hence \(q\) is not an atom. Increasing enumeration and the split require no choice axiom. □

A descending countable sequence retains a nonzero lower bound

Assuming the axiom of choice, if \(q_0\ge q_1\ge q_2\ge\cdots\) are nonzero elements of \(\mathcal P(\mathbb N)/\operatorname{Fin}\), there is a nonzero \(r\) with \(r\le q_n\) for every \(n\).

Proof

Choose representatives \(q_n=[A_n]\). Each \(A_n\) is infinite, and the descending order gives \(A_n\subseteq^*A_i\) whenever \(i\le n\). Put \(C_n=\bigcap_{i\le n}A_i\). Then

\[ A_n\setminus C_n=\bigcup_{i\le n}(A_n\setminus A_i) \]

is finite, so \(C_n\) is infinite. The sets \(C_n\) are decreasing. Every nonempty finite intersection of them equals the member with largest index and is infinite; the empty intersection is \(\mathbb N\). They have the strong finite intersection property.

The countable pseudointersection theorem gives an infinite \(B\subseteq\mathbb N\) with \(B\subseteq^*C_n\) for every \(n\). Since \(C_n\subseteq A_n\), also \(B\subseteq^*A_n\). Thus \(r=[B]\ne0\) lies below every \(q_n\). □

Choice was used to select the representative sequence. If representatives \(A_n\) are supplied as part of the data, this proof uses no choice axiom. The nonzero condition matters: zero is always a lower bound, while an infinite pseudointersection supplies a nonzero one.

Why not take the class of the exact countable intersection?

For \(T_n=\{m\in\mathbb N:m\ge n\}\), determine \([T_n]\), their exact intersection, and a nonzero common lower bound. Could taking intersections of representatives define countable meets?

Show answerHide answer

Each tail is cofinite, so \([T_n]=1\). Their exact intersection is empty, but \(1=[\mathbb N]\) is a nonzero lower bound of all these classes, and is their greatest lower bound.

Replacing every \(T_n\) by the equivalent representative \(\mathbb N\) changes the exact intersection from \(\varnothing\) to \(\mathbb N\), whose classes are different. Thus countable intersection of representatives is not a well-defined operation on this quotient. The finite-operation proof cannot be extended by replacing finite unions of errors with countable unions. The lower-bound theorem supplies an approximate intersection and does not assert that its chosen lower bound is greatest.
