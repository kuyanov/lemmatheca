Comparing sets through maps

For finite sets, counting gives a natural number. For arbitrary sets, we compare size by using the injective and bijective maps already introduced. Write

\[ |A|=|B| \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{there is a bijection }A\longrightarrow B, \]
\[ |A|\le |B| \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{there is an injection }A\longrightarrow B. \]

These are statements about sets and maps; we do not yet need to assign a particular object called a cardinal number to each size. For finite sets the notation agrees with ordinary cardinality. Define \(|A|<|B|\) to mean \(|A|\le|B|\) and \(|A|\ne|B|\).

An injection identifies \(A\) with its image inside \(B\). Inclusion therefore gives \(|A|\le|B|\) whenever \(A\subseteq B\), but a proper inclusion need not give a strict inequality of sizes.

Equality and comparison survive a change of names

Equality of cardinality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. The comparison \(|A|\le|B|\) is reflexive and transitive. Both relations are unchanged if either set is replaced by a set in bijection with it.

Proof

The identity map supplies reflexivity. The inverse of a bijection supplies symmetry, and composing bijections supplies transitivity. Composing injections also gives an injection. Finally, if \(u:A'\to A\) and \(v:B\to B'\) are bijections and \(f:A\to B\) is an injection, then \(v\circ f\circ u:A'\to B'\) is an injection. Apply the inverse bijections to reverse this implication. The same argument works for bijections. □

Thus a relabeling preserves size. It need not preserve an order placed on the set: order isomorphisms must also preserve and reflect comparison of elements.

Can a proper subset have the same size?

Compare \(\mathbb N\) with its positive elements and with its even elements. Why does the corresponding phenomenon fail for finite sets?

Show answerHide answer

The maps \(n\mapsto n+1\) and \(n\mapsto2n\) are bijections onto the positive and even natural numbers, respectively. Their inverses, on these restricted codomains, are \(k\mapsto k-1\) and \(k\mapsto k/2\). Both subsets are proper, yet both have the size of \(\mathbb N\).

If \(S\subsetneq A\) and \(A\) is finite, a bijection \(A\to S\), followed by inclusion into \(A\), would be an injective self-map of \(A\) missing an element. This contradicts the finite self-map theorem. One injection that misses a point does not, by itself, prove a strict size inequality for infinite sets.

Injections in both directions give a bijection

If \(f:A\to B\) and \(g:B\to A\) are injective, then there is a bijection \(h:A\to B\). This is the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem.

Proof

The inverse \(g^{-1}:g(B)\to B\) is defined on the image of \(g\), not necessarily on all of \(A\). We will use \(f\) on one part of \(A\) and this inverse on the rest. Starting with the elements outside the image of \(g\), define

\[ C_0=A\setminus g(B),\qquad C_{n+1}=g(f(C_n)),\qquad C=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}C_n. \]

The set \(C\) contains \(C_0\), and \(g(f(C))\subseteq C\): applying \(g\circ f\) to a member of \(C_n\) puts it in \(C_{n+1}\). Every \(a\notin C\) belongs to \(g(B)\), since it is not in \(C_0\). Consequently the following rule defines a map:

\[ h(a)= \begin{cases} f(a),&a\in C,\\ g^{-1}(a),&a\in A\setminus C. \end{cases} \label{eq:bernstein-map} \]

Injectivity. Within \(C\), equality of outputs implies equality of inputs by injectivity of \(f\). Outside \(C\), apply \(g\) to equal outputs. A collision between the two parts is impossible: if \(a\in C\), \(a'\notin C\), and \(f(a)=g^{-1}(a')\), then \(a'=g(f(a))\in C\), a contradiction.

Surjectivity. Fix \(b\in B\). If \(g(b)\notin C\), then \(h(g(b))=b\). If \(g(b)\in C\), it lies in some \(C_n\). It cannot lie in \(C_0=A\setminus g(B)\), so \(n=k+1\) for some \(k\). There is an \(a\in C_k\) with \(g(b)=g(f(a))\). Injectivity of \(g\) gives \(b=f(a)=h(a)\). Thus every \(b\) has a preimage under the same map \(h\). □

The construction also covers empty sets. If \(A=\varnothing\), the injection \(g:B\to A\) forces \(B=\varnothing\), and \(h\) is the empty bijection.

What does the constructed bijection do?

Take \(A=B=\mathbb N\), \(f(n)=n+1\), and \(g(n)=n+2\). Find the sets \(C_n\) and the first six values of the map in \eqref{eq:bernstein-map}.

Show answerHide answer

Here \(C_0=\{0,1\}\), and \(g(f(n))=n+3\). Hence \(C_k=\{3k,3k+1\}\) and \(C=\{n:n\equiv0\text{ or }1\pmod3\}\).

On \(C\), the rule is \(h(n)=n+1\); on its complement, the inverse of \(g\) gives \(h(n)=n-2\). Thus the values at inputs \(0,1,2,3,4,5\) are \(1,2,0,4,5,3\). Each block of three consecutive numbers is cyclically rearranged. Neither given injection was surjective, but the combined map is.

What strict comparison rules out

We have \(|A|=|B|\) if and only if \(|A|\le|B|\) and \(|B|\le|A|\). In particular,

\[ |A|<|B| \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |A|\le|B|\ \text{and there is no injection }B\longrightarrow A. \]

Proof

A bijection and its inverse give the two injections; the converse is . If \(|A|\le|B|\), an injection back would therefore give a bijection, and a bijection would give an injection back. Negating these equivalent possibilities gives the displayed equivalence. □

Strict comparison is transitive. If \(|A|<|B|<|D|\), compose the forward injections to get \(|A|\le|D|\). An injection \(D\to A\), composed with \(B\to D\), would give the prohibited injection \(B\to A\). Thus \(|A|<|D|\). The theorem compares a specified pair once the injections are available; it does not supply an injection in one direction for every arbitrary pair of sets.

To be uncountable is to defeat every list

A set is uncountable if it is not countable. By the encoding characterization, this means there is no injection from it into \(\mathbb N\). For a nonempty set \(A\), the list characterization gives the equivalent assertion that no map \(\mathbb N\to A\) is surjective.

To prove this last assertion, take an arbitrary proposed list \(a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots\) and construct an element outside its range. Repetitions are allowed. A proof must defeat every proposed list, not merely a particular ordering that happens to omit some elements.

Uncountability passes to any set containing an uncountable subset. More generally, if \(U\) is uncountable and \(U\to V\) is injective, then \(V\) is uncountable: an encoding \(V\to\mathbb N\) would compose to an encoding of \(U\).

A power set is strictly larger

For every set \(A\), there is no surjection \(A\to\mathcal P(A)\), and \(|A|<|\mathcal P(A)|\).

Proof

Fix any map \(F:A\to\mathcal P(A)\), and define

\[ D=\{a\in A:a\notin F(a)\}. \label{eq:diagonal-subset} \]

Since \(D\subseteq A\), it is an element of the required codomain. For each \(a\in A\), its membership in \(D\) differs from its membership in \(F(a)\). Therefore \(D\ne F(a)\) for every \(a\), and \(F\) is not surjective. Equivalently, assuming \(F(a)=D\) would give \(a\in D\Longleftrightarrow a\notin D\).

The singleton map \(a\mapsto\{a\}\) is an injection \(A\to\mathcal P(A)\): equality of singleton sets implies equality of their elements. There is no bijection, since every bijection is surjective. Together these facts prove the strict inequality. □

When \(A=\varnothing\), its power set is \(\{\varnothing\}\), so the conclusion is the familiar inequality \(0<1\). For an \(n\)-element set, recording which of its \(n\) elements belong to a subset gives \(2^n\) possibilities. The diagonal proof applies whether or not \(A\) is finite.

Which subset is omitted?

For \(A=\mathbb N\), compute \eqref{eq:diagonal-subset} when \(F(n)=\{n\}\), and when \(F(n)=\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\). What changes if the second rule is instead \(F(n)=\{0,\ldots,n\}\)?

Show answerHide answer

For the singleton map, every \(n\) belongs to \(F(n)\), so \(D=\varnothing\). No singleton is empty. For the initial-segment map, \(n\notin F(n)\) for every \(n\), so \(D=\mathbb N\); every listed initial segment is finite.

If the rule is instead \(F(n)=\{0,\ldots,n\}\), every diagonal membership is true and \(D=\varnothing\), which again is not listed. The omitted subset depends on the proposed list. This does not weaken the conclusion: each map omits the subset constructed from that very map.

Subsets as infinite binary sequences

Let \(2^{\mathbb N}\) denote the set of all functions \(s:\mathbb N\to\{0,1\}\). Here the notation describes a set of sequences; it is different from the single number \(2^n\) for finite \(n\).

A subset \(S\subseteq\mathbb N\) determines its characteristic sequence

\[ \chi_S(n)= \begin{cases}1,&n\in S,\\0,&n\notin S.\end{cases} \]

Conversely, a binary sequence \(s\) determines \(S_s=\{n:s(n)=1\}\). We have \(S_{\chi_S}=S\), and \(\chi_{S_s}=s\), by checking membership or values at each natural number. These two maps are inverses, so \(|2^{\mathbb N}|=|\mathcal P(\mathbb N)|\). In particular, both sets are uncountable: a countable power set would have a surjective list from \(\mathbb N\), contrary to .

Changing the diagonal defeats a sequence list

For any proposed list \(s_0,s_1,\ldots\) of members of \(2^{\mathbb N}\), the sequence \(d(n)=1-s_n(n)\) belongs to \(2^{\mathbb N}\) and is absent from the list.

Proof

Each \(s_n(n)\) is zero or one, so \(d(n)\) is also zero or one and differs from it. Thus \(d\ne s_n\) for every \(n\), with coordinate \(n\) witnessing the difference. No proposed list is surjective. □

Do finite binary words already form an uncountable set?

Compare all finite binary words with all infinite binary sequences. What about infinite sequences with only finitely many ones?

Show answerHide answer

Finite words are countable. Encode a word \((b_0,\ldots,b_{r-1})\) by \(2^r+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}b_j2^j\). Its code lies between \(2^r\) and \(2^{r+1}-1\), so the code determines the length. Within that interval, uniqueness of the binary digits determines the word. The empty word has code one.

Sequences with finitely many ones are also countable: encode such a sequence by \(\sum_{\{j:s(j)=1\}}2^j\), a finite sum. Different finite supports have different sums. At their largest differing index \(k\), the term \(2^k\) outweighs all possible smaller terms together, whose sum is \(2^k-1\). The all-zero sequence has code zero.

For the full set of infinite sequences, there is no last nonzero position in general, and the finite integer code does not apply. The diagonal theorem proves that this full set is uncountable.

The real interval and the real line are uncountable

The interval \((0,1)\), the real line \(\mathbb R\), and the set \(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\) of irrational numbers are uncountable.

Proof

We use the usual decimal-expansion facts: every member of \((0,1)\) has an expansion \(0.a_0a_1a_2\ldots\), and choosing the expansion that is not eventually all nines makes it unique. For example, use \(0.5000\ldots\) rather than \(0.4999\ldots\).

Given any list \(x_0,x_1,\ldots\) in \((0,1)\), write the \(n\)-th entry as \(x_n=0.a_{n,0}a_{n,1}\ldots\) in this convention. Set

\[ b_n=\begin{cases}2,&a_{n,n}=1,\\1,&a_{n,n}\ne1,\end{cases} \qquad y=0.b_0b_1b_2\ldots . \]

The digits of \(y\) are all one or two, so \(1/9\le y\le2/9\) and \(y\in(0,1)\). Its expansion is not eventually nine. It differs from the chosen expansion of \(x_n\) at digit \(n\), so uniqueness gives \(y\ne x_n\) for every \(n\). Thus no list covers \((0,1)\).

Since \((0,1)\subseteq\mathbb R\), the real line is uncountable as well. If the irrationals were countable, their union with the countable set of rationals would be countable by closure under finite unions. That union is \(\mathbb R\), a contradiction. □

Different lengths can have the same cardinality

For real numbers \(a<b\), the intervals \((a,b)\), \((0,1)\), and the whole real line have the same cardinality.

Proof

The affine map \(t\mapsto a+(b-a)t\) bijects \((0,1)\) with \((a,b)\); its inverse is \(x\mapsto(x-a)/(b-a)\). To compare with the whole line, use

\[ u:\mathbb R\longrightarrow(-1,1),\qquad u(x)=\frac{x}{1+|x|}. \]

Its values lie strictly between minus one and one. For \(t\in(-1,1)\), the positive denominator \(1-|t|\) makes \(v(t)=t/(1-|t|)\) well defined. The identities \(|v(t)|=|t|/(1-|t|)\) and \(|u(x)|=|x|/(1+|x|)\) give \(u(v(t))=t\) and \(v(u(x))=x\). Finally \(t\mapsto(t+1)/2\) bijects \((-1,1)\) with \((0,1)\). Compose these bijections. □

Cardinality records the possibility of a bijection. It does not record interval length, nor does it require the bijection to preserve distances.

There is always a larger size

Starting with any set \(A\) and repeatedly taking power sets gives strict comparisons

\[ |A|<|\mathcal P(A)|<|\mathcal P(\mathcal P(A))|<\cdots . \]

Proof

Apply to each set in turn. Thus there cannot be a set whose cardinality is at least that of every set: its own power set would have a strictly larger cardinality. □

Already \(\mathbb N\) and \(\mathcal P(\mathbb N)\) have different infinite sizes. “Uncountable” rules out a natural-number encoding; it does not assert that all uncountable sets have the same size. For example, both \(\mathbb R\) and \(\mathcal P(\mathbb R)\) are uncountable, but their cardinalities are different.
