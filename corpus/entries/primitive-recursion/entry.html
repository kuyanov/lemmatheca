A starting value and a rule for the next value

Repeated addition computes multiplication; repeated multiplication computes powers. Both follow the same pattern. Fix a natural number \(k\ge0\), a function \(g:\mathbb N^k\to\mathbb N\), and a function \(h:\mathbb N^{k+2}\to\mathbb N\). A function \(F:\mathbb N^{k+1}\to\mathbb N\) is defined from \(g,h\) by primitive recursion when

\[F(\bar x,0)=g(\bar x),\qquad F(\bar x,n+1)=h(\bar x,n,F(\bar x,n)).\]

The tuple \(\bar x=(x_0,\ldots,x_{k-1})\) remains fixed throughout the recursion. The step may inspect the parameters, the current index, and the immediately preceding value. The recursion variable is the last argument. When \(k=0\), there are no parameters: \(\mathbb N^0\) has one empty tuple, so \(g\) simply specifies a natural-number constant.

Here and throughout this entry, \(\mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\). We reason about its ordinary arithmetic in the surrounding mathematics. We are not claiming that a particular first-order arithmetic theory proves every assertion made here.

The recursion determines exactly one total function

For every pair of total functions \(g,h\) of the indicated arities, there exists exactly one total function \(F\) satisfying .

Fix \(\bar x\). Apply natural-number recursion with starting value \(g(\bar x)\) and index-dependent step \(u\mapsto h(\bar x,n,u)\). This supplies \(F(\bar x,n)\) for every \(n\). Doing this for every parameter tuple defines the required function. For uniqueness, if \(F,G\) satisfy the equations, their values agree at zero. Agreement at \(n\) gives agreement at \(n+1\) by the common step function. Induction gives agreement for all \(n\) and every \(\bar x\).

Totality of the starting and step functions matters: each step must produce a natural number. The recursion prescribes how many steps to perform, rather than asking us to continue until a possibly unattainable condition is met.

The functions built from the initial functions

The initial functions are the constant-zero functions \(Z_k:\mathbb N^k\to\mathbb N\), for every \(k\ge0\); successor \(S(n)=n+1\); and the projections

\[P_i^k(x_0,\ldots,x_{k-1})=x_i\qquad(i<k).\]

There are no zero-argument projections. Given \(f:\mathbb N^m\to\mathbb N\) and \(g_0,\ldots,g_{m-1}:\mathbb N^k\to\mathbb N\), their composition is

\[\bar x\longmapsto f(g_0(\bar x),\ldots,g_{m-1}(\bar x)).\]

The primitive-recursive functions are the smallest collection of numerical functions of finite arities containing the initial functions and closed under composition and primitive recursion. Equivalently, a function belongs to this collection exactly when it has a finite construction from these rules. Two constructions may give the same function: membership concerns the resulting values, not a chosen expression for them.

Every such function is total. This follows by induction on a construction: the initial functions are total; a composition of total functions is total; and supplies totality at a recursion step. Every natural-number constant is primitive recursive, by applying successor finitely often to zero.

Projections allow inputs to be reordered, repeated or ignored. For example, if \(f\) has three inputs, then \(f(y,x,y)\) is obtained by composing \(f\) with \(P_1^2,P_0^2,P_1^2\). Thus a change in argument order does not change whether a function is primitive recursive. If \(m=0\), the composition has no inner functions and is simply the chosen constant on \(\mathbb N^k\).

Addition, multiplication, powers and factorials have finite constructions

Addition, multiplication, exponentiation and factorial are primitive recursive. Their defining equations are

\[x+0=x,\qquad x+(n+1)=S(x+n),\]
\[x\cdot0=0,\qquad x\cdot(n+1)=x\cdot n+x,\]
\[x^0=1,\qquad x^{n+1}=x^n\cdot x,\qquad 0!=1,\qquad(n+1)!=(n+1)n!.\]

In particular \(0^0=1\), as specified by the recursion.

For addition, the starting function is \(P_0^1(x)=x\), and the step is \(h(x,n,u)=S(u)\), a composition of successor and a projection. These are exactly the usual addition equations, so uniqueness identifies the constructed function with addition.

For multiplication use the zero starting function and \(h(x,n,u)=u+x\), already available by composition with addition. For exponentiation use the constant-one starting function and \(h(x,n,u)=u\cdot x\). For factorial there are no parameters: the initial value is \(1\), and the step is \(h(n,u)=(n+1)u\). Each step uses only functions constructed earlier. The recursion equations and uniqueness identify all four functions.

For instance, computing \(3^4\) successively produces \(1,3,9,27,81\). A recursive definition may be inefficient and still establish primitive recursiveness; no efficiency bound is part of the definition.

Subtraction stays within the natural numbers

The predecessor function, truncated subtraction and absolute difference are primitive recursive. Define them by

\[\operatorname{pred}(0)=0,\qquad\operatorname{pred}(n+1)=n,\qquad x\mathbin{\dot-}0=x,\qquad x\mathbin{\dot-}(n+1)=\operatorname{pred}(x\mathbin{\dot-}n),\]
\[\delta(x,y)=(x\mathbin{\dot-}y)+(y\mathbin{\dot-}x).\]

Here \(x\mathbin{\dot-}y=\max(x-y,0)\), where the difference inside the maximum may be read in the integers, and \(\delta(x,y)\) is the ordinary nonnegative distance between \(x,y\).

Predecessor uses zero as its initial value and the projection \(h(n,u)=n\) as its step. Truncated subtraction starts at \(x\) and applies predecessor to its previous value. Its stated value follows by induction on the second argument: while the value is positive it decreases by one, and after reaching zero it stays zero. Finally \(\delta\) is a composition of truncated subtraction and addition. If \(x\le y\), its first summand is zero and its second is \(y-x\); the opposite order gives \(x-y\). In particular \(\delta(x,y)=0\) exactly when \(x=y\), and \(x\mathbin{\dot-}y=0\) exactly when \(x\le y\).

Finite sums and products preserve primitive recursiveness

If \(f(\bar x,i)\) is primitive recursive, so are

\[A(\bar x,b)=\sum_{i<b}f(\bar x,i),\qquad B(\bar x,b)=\prod_{i<b}f(\bar x,i).\]

The empty sum is \(0\); the empty product is \(1\). A primitive-recursive bound \(b=t(\bar x)\) may replace the separate argument \(b\).

Use the recursions

\[A(\bar x,0)=0,\quad A(\bar x,b+1)=A(\bar x,b)+f(\bar x,b),\]
\[B(\bar x,0)=1,\quad B(\bar x,b+1)=B(\bar x,b)f(\bar x,b).\]

The starting functions are constants, and the steps are compositions of already primitive-recursive functions. Induction verifies the finite-sum and finite-product formulas. Substituting \(t(\bar x)\) into the last input is composition. This proves the variable-bound claim as well.

Relations are represented by zero-one tests

The characteristic function of a relation \(R\subseteq\mathbb N^k\) is

\[\chi_R(\bar x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }R(\bar x),\\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]

Call \(R\) primitive recursive when \(\chi_R\) is primitive recursive. We consistently use \(1\) for true and \(0\) for false. A general numerical function need not have only these two values; a characteristic function does.

Equality, order and Boolean combinations are primitive recursive

The relations \(x=0\), \(x=y\), \(x\le y\) and \(x<y\) are primitive recursive. Primitive-recursive relations are closed under negation, conjunction, disjunction and implication, and under substitution of primitive-recursive functions for their inputs.

The zero test \(z\) is primitive recursive by

\[z(0)=1,\qquad z(n+1)=0.\]

Using , the equality test is \(z(\delta(x,y))\), the test for \(x\le y\) is \(z(x\mathbin{\dot-}y)\), and the test for \(x<y\) is the test for \(x+1\le y\). For zero-one inputs \(a,b\), the functions

\[\neg a:\ 1\mathbin{\dot-}a,\qquad a\wedge b:\ ab,\qquad a\vee b:\ 1\mathbin{\dot-}\bigl((1\mathbin{\dot-}a)(1\mathbin{\dot-}b)\bigr)\]

have exactly the indicated truth tables. Composition therefore gives the characteristic functions of the Boolean combinations. Implication is \(\neg R\vee Q\). Substitution is directly the composition rule.

For example, the graph of a primitive-recursive function \(f\) is a primitive-recursive relation: test \(f(\bar x)=y\) by composing the equality test with \(f\) and a projection.

A primitive-recursive test can choose between two values

If \(R\) is a primitive-recursive relation and \(f,g\) are primitive-recursive functions of the same inputs, then the function equal to \(f(\bar x)\) when \(R(\bar x)\) holds and to \(g(\bar x)\) otherwise is primitive recursive.

The required function is

\[\chi_R(\bar x)f(\bar x)+(1\mathbin{\dot-}\chi_R(\bar x))g(\bar x).\]

When the test is \(1\), the second coefficient is zero; when it is \(0\), the first coefficient is zero and the second is one. The displayed expression is a composition of primitive-recursive functions. Repeating this construction gives finitely many cases, with earlier cases taking precedence if their conditions overlap.

Quantifiers over a specified finite range preserve the class

If \(R(\bar x,i)\) is primitive recursive, so are the relations

\[\forall i<b\ R(\bar x,i),\qquad\exists i<b\ R(\bar x,i).\]

The bound may itself be a primitive-recursive function of the other inputs. At \(b=0\), the universal relation is true and the existential relation is false.

A finite product of zero-one numbers is \(1\) exactly when every factor is \(1\). A finite sum of them is nonzero exactly when at least one summand is \(1\). Consequently the two characteristic functions are

\[\chi_{\forall i<b\,R}(\bar x,b)=\prod_{i<b}\chi_R(\bar x,i),\qquad\chi_{\exists i<b\,R}(\bar x,b)=1\mathbin{\dot-}z\!\left(\sum_{i<b}\chi_R(\bar x,i)\right).\]

They are primitive recursive by and . The empty product \(1\) and empty sum \(0\) give the asserted values at zero. A variable bound is substituted by composition. A bound \(i\le b\) is the same as \(i<b+1\).

This result applies to repeated bounded quantifiers as well: eliminate them from the inside out. It does not cover a quantifier over all natural numbers.

Bounded search returns the first witness or the bound

Let \(R(\bar x,i)\) be primitive recursive. The function

\[m_R(\bar x,b)=\begin{cases}\min\{i<b:R(\bar x,i)\}&\text{if a witness exists},\\b&\text{if no witness exists}\end{cases}\]

is primitive recursive. It always lies between \(0\) and \(b\). A value smaller than \(b\) is the least witness; a value equal to \(b\) means there was no witness in the specified range.

Maintain the first witness found so far, using the current bound itself when none has been found. Define

\[L(\bar x,0)=0,\qquad L(\bar x,n+1)=\begin{cases}L(\bar x,n)&\text{if }L(\bar x,n)<n,\\n&\text{if }L(\bar x,n)\ge n\text{ and }R(\bar x,n),\\n+1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]

This is primitive recursion: replace the old value \(L(\bar x,n)\) in the cases by a separate input \(u\). The resulting step function is primitive recursive by the order tests and .

Induct on \(n\). Initially there is no index below zero, so the value \(0\) is the required sentinel. Suppose the claim holds at \(n\). If a witness already exists below \(n\), the previous value is its least index, is smaller than \(n\), and remains least below \(n+1\). If no witness exists below \(n\), the previous value is \(n\). The new index \(n\) is then the least witness if it satisfies \(R\); otherwise there are still no witnesses, and the correct sentinel is \(n+1\). Thus \(L=m_R\), with the stated specification.

We call this operation bounded minimization. A primitive-recursive bound \(b=t(\bar x)\) is again allowed by composition. The sentinel convention keeps an unsuccessful search distinct from a successful search whose witness is \(0\).

Division with remainder is a bounded computation

There are primitive-recursive functions \(q(x,d),r(x,d)\) such that, for every \(d>0\),

\[x=dq(x,d)+r(x,d),\qquad 0\le r(x,d)<d.\]

These conditions determine \(q,r\) uniquely when \(d>0\). For total functions on all inputs, set \(q(x,0)=0\) and \(r(x,0)=x\); the strict remainder bound is claimed only for positive divisors.

For \(d>0\), search for the first \(j<x+1\) such that \(x<(j+1)d\), and set \(q(x,d)\) equal to that index. The test and the bound are primitive recursive. There is a witness: \(j=x\) works because \(d\ge1\) gives \((x+1)d\ge x+1>x\). Thus returns a genuine witness \(q\le x\), satisfying \(x<(q+1)d\).

If \(q=0\), then \(qd=0\le x\). If \(q>0\), its predecessor is an earlier search index, so minimality says the test fails there: \(x\not<qd\), hence \(qd\le x\). Define \(r(x,d)=x\mathbin{\dot-}dq(x,d)\). Since \(dq\le x\), natural-number subtraction as an additive difference gives \(x=dq+r\). The inequality \(x<dq+d\) gives \(r<d\) by cancellation. Composition and include the specified zero-divisor case.

For uniqueness, suppose \(dq+r=dq'+r'\) with both remainders smaller than \(d\). If \(q<q'\), then \(dq+r<d(q+1)\le dq'\le dq'+r'\), a contradiction. The opposite inequality is equally impossible. Hence \(q=q'\), and additive cancellation gives \(r=r'\).

Recognizing squares and finding their roots

Give a primitive-recursive test for whether a natural number \(x\) is a square. Define a primitive-recursive function which returns its nonnegative square root when one exists, and returns \(x+1\) otherwise. Check the input \(x=0\).

Show answerHide answer

Use the relation \(R(x,i)\) given by \(i^2=x\). It is primitive recursive by multiplication and the equality test. A root always satisfies \(i\le x\): this is immediate for \(i=0\), and for \(i\ge1\) we have \(i\le i^2=x\). Thus \(\exists i<x+1\ R(x,i)\) tests whether \(x\) is a square, and the required root function is \(m_R(x,x+1)\). For \(x=0\), the search includes \(i=0\), so the output is \(0\), not the sentinel \(1\).

A root is unique in \(\mathbb N\). If \(i<j\), then \(i^2<j^2\): for \(i=0\) this follows from \(j>0\); otherwise \(i^2<ij<j^2\). Therefore the least root returned by the search is the only root.

What does a search with bound zero report?

Suppose \(R(\bar x,0)\) holds. What are \(m_R(\bar x,0)\) and \(m_R(\bar x,1)\)? Does an output of \(0\) always certify a witness?

Show answerHide answer

Both values are \(0\), with different meanings. At bound \(0\) the range is empty, so \(0\) is the sentinel and no witness was tested. At bound \(1\), the index \(0\) is tested and is a witness. The successful-search condition is \(m_R(\bar x,b)<b\); the numerical output alone does not distinguish these situations. This is why the bound is part of the search specification.
