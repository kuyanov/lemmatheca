What representation asks arithmetic to prove

Use the arithmetic language with symbols \(0,S,+,\cdot\), its standard structure \(\mathbb N_A\), the numerals \(\overline n\), and the seven axioms of Robinson arithmetic, denoted by \(Q\). The notation \(Q\vdash\sigma\) refers to the existing first-order proof calculus, not merely to truth in the standard natural numbers.

Let \(f:\mathbb N^k\to\mathbb N\) be total. A formula \(F(\bar x,y)\) represents \(f\) in \(Q\) if, for every external tuple \(\bar a\in\mathbb N^k\),

\[Q\vdash\forall y\,\bigl(F(\overline{\bar a},y)\leftrightarrow y=\overline{f(\bar a)}\bigr).\]

The same formula must work for every input: the quantifier order is “there exists \(F\), for every \(\bar a\).” This convention includes zero arguments. It proves the correct numeral instance and rules out every other output, including nonstandard elements of a model of \(Q\). Some texts call this strong numeralwise representation.

A formula \(A(\bar x)\) represents a relation \(R\subseteq\mathbb N^k\) if \(R(\bar a)\) implies \(Q\vdash A(\overline{\bar a})\), and \(\neg R(\bar a)\) implies \(Q\vdash\neg A(\overline{\bar a})\). All displayed substitutions and quantifiers use the existing capture-safe syntax. Variables introduced as witnesses are chosen fresh; no additional function symbols are being added to \(L_A\).

A fixed numeral has only its expected predecessors

Introduce arithmetic abbreviations with the following specific orientation:

\[x\le y\;:\!\Longleftrightarrow\;\exists z\,(z+x=y),\qquad x\lt y\;:\!\Longleftrightarrow\;\exists z\,(z+Sx=y).\]

They have their usual meanings in \(\mathbb N_A\). We use these formulas, rather than assuming that arbitrary models of \(Q\) are ordered semirings. For each external \(n\), \(Q\) proves

\[x\le\overline n\;\leftrightarrow\;\bigvee_{i\le n}x=\overline i,\qquad x\lt\overline n\;\leftrightarrow\;\bigvee_{i\lt n}x=\overline i.\]

It also proves that every \(x\) either equals one of \(\overline0,\ldots,\overline n\), or satisfies \(\overline n\lt x\).

Proof

The proof is an external induction on the fixed numeral. If \(z+x=0\) and \(x\ne0\), the predecessor axiom writes \(x=Su\); then \(z+Su=S(z+u)\ne0\), a contradiction. Hence the initial cut consists only of zero. For a successor bound, either \(x=0\), or \(x=Su\). In the latter case, \(z+Su=S\overline n\) is equivalent, by the addition axiom and successor injectivity, to \(z+u=\overline n\). Apply the preceding cut. Conversely each listed numeral has the required numeral difference as a witness, using numeral computation. The strict cut is the same argument with \(Sx\); in particular the cut below zero is empty.

Finally apply the predecessor axiom successively \(n+1\) times to an arbitrary \(x\). Either a zero appears, giving one of the listed numerals, or \(x=S^{n+1}u\). The addition equations give \(u+S\overline n=S^{n+1}u=x\), the witness for \(\overline n\lt x\). These are finite proof constructions for each \(n\); no induction schema has been used inside \(Q\). Equivalently, the argument establishes the displayed closed sentences in every model of \(Q\), and completeness gives their \(Q\)-derivations. □

Quantifiers with arithmetic bounds

A bounded quantifier is an abbreviation

\[\exists u\le t\,A\;:=\;\exists u\,(u\le t\land A),\qquad \forall u\le t\,A\;:=\;\forall u\,(u\le t\to A),\]

where \(u\) does not occur in the bounding term \(t\); strict bounds are defined similarly. A bounded formula, or \(\Delta_0\) formula, is built from equalities of arithmetic terms by Boolean connectives and bounded quantifiers. The displayed order abbreviations are allowed in this grammar and have the meanings fixed in .

Here “bounded” restricts the arithmetic quantifiers. It does not refer to the number of in-scope variable slots in a formal syntax type. A \(\Sigma_1\) formula in the convention used below has the form \(\exists u_0\cdots\exists u_{r-1}D\), where \(D\) is \(\Delta_0\). An empty existential prefix is allowed. We make no claim that the later representing formulas constructed using arbitrary Boolean operations have this restricted form.

Robinson arithmetic decides each bounded numeral instance

For every \(\Delta_0\) formula \(D(\bar x)\) and external numerical tuple \(\bar a\),

\[\mathbb N_A\models D(\bar a)\ \Longrightarrow\ Q\vdash D(\overline{\bar a}),\]
\[\mathbb N_A\not\models D(\bar a)\ \Longrightarrow\ Q\vdash\neg D(\overline{\bar a}).\]

Proof

Induct externally on the formula, strengthening the claim to all numeral assignments to its free variables. A closed arithmetic term is provably equal to its standard numeral: follow its finite construction, using the addition and multiplication equations. Distinct numerals are provably unequal by successor injectivity and \(Sx\ne0\). This settles equalities and, by , the order abbreviations. The classical Boolean rules combine the proofs or refutations at the next constructor.

At a bounded quantifier, the bounding term under the given numeral assignment is provably some \(\overline b\). The cut lemma replaces a universal quantifier over that bound by the finite conjunction of its numeral instances, and an existential quantifier by their finite disjunction. The induction hypothesis decides each instance. Empty strict bounds give the empty conjunction \(\top\) and empty disjunction \(\bot\). This constructs a proof in either case. Equivalently every model of \(Q\) agrees with \(\mathbb N_A\) on these instances, and completeness converts that agreement into a derivation. □

A true existential bounded assertion has a proof

Every true \(\Sigma_1\) sentence, in the convention of , is provable in \(Q\). The same holds after replacing all free inputs by numerals.

Proof

Choose its finite tuple of standard witnesses. The remaining bounded sentence is true, so supplies its \(Q\)-proof. Introduce the existential quantifiers with those witness numerals. Conversely every \(Q\)-theorem is true in \(\mathbb N_A\), by soundness and the standard model of the axioms. □

This proves the true instances, not refutations of all false existential sentences. An unbounded existential quantifier cannot be replaced by one finite list of possible witnesses.

Coprime moduli allow cancellation

The next three blocks concern ordinary natural numbers and integers, not arbitrary models of \(Q\). Positive integers \(m,n\) are coprime if their only positive common divisor is \(1\). If they are coprime, there are integers \(r,s\) with \(rm+sn=1\). Consequently \(m\mid nt\) implies \(m\mid t\).

Proof

Among the positive integers of the form \(am+bn\), with \(a,b\in\mathbb Z\), choose the least, \(d\). This set is nonempty since it contains \(m\). Divide \(m\) by \(d\), using division with remainder. A positive remainder would be a smaller positive integer combination of \(m,n\); thus the remainder is zero. The same argument gives \(d\mid n\). Coprimality forces \(d=1\), giving the displayed identity. Multiply it by \(t\). Both \(rmt\) and \(snt\) are divisible by \(m\), proving cancellation. □

Coprimality is preserved on taking a product of moduli that are each coprime to a fixed modulus: multiply their Bézout identities, and collect every term containing that fixed modulus. We will use this finite-product consequence.

Choose finitely many remainders simultaneously

Let \(m_0,\ldots,m_{k-1}\) be pairwise coprime positive moduli and let \(a_i\lt m_i\). There is a natural number \(c\) with \(c\equiv a_i\pmod {m_i}\) for every \(i\lt k\).

Proof

For two coprime moduli \(m,n\), take \(rm+sn=1\). The integer \(brm+asn\) is congruent to \(a\) modulo \(m\) and to \(b\) modulo \(n\). Replacing it by its nonnegative remainder modulo \(mn\) gives a natural-number solution. For completeness, division of a possibly negative integer by a positive modulus reduces to natural-number division: if \(u=qM+r\), \(0\le r\lt M\), then \(-u=(-q)M\) when \(r=0\), and \(-u=(-q-1)M+(M-r)\) otherwise.

Induct on the finite list of moduli. Combine a solution for its initial part, modulo the product of those moduli, with the final congruence. That product is coprime to the new modulus by the last observation in . For an empty list, \(c=0\) suffices. □

Two numbers can record any finite sequence

Define the total numerical function

\[\beta(c,d,i):=c\bmod\bigl((i+1)d+1\bigr).\]

For every finite sequence \(a_0,\ldots,a_{k-1}\), there are natural numbers \(c,d\) such that \(\beta(c,d,i)=a_i\) for every \(i\lt k\).

Proof

Choose \(N=\max(1,k)\), let \(A\) be the maximum of zero and the sequence entries, and put \(d=N!(A+1)\). Then \(d\gt0\), every \(a_i\lt d\), and each integer from \(1\) to \(N\) divides \(d\). Set \(m_i=1+(i+1)d\). These moduli exceed both \(1\) and the corresponding \(a_i\).

If a positive integer \(r\) divides both \(m_i\) and \(m_j\), where \(i\lt j\), then it divides \((j-i)d\). It is coprime to \(d\), since a common divisor of \(r,d\) would divide \(m_i-(i+1)d=1\). Cancellation gives \(r\mid j-i\). But \(j-i\mid d\), so \(r\mid d\) and hence \(r=1\). Thus the moduli are pairwise coprime. Apply . The prescribed residues are the actual remainders because \(a_i\lt m_i\). The empty sequence imposes no conditions; the same choice with \(A=0,N=1\) is harmless. □

This is an additional arithmetic coding, used to describe finite histories by formulas. It does not replace the existing effective list code or the syntax and proof codes. We have proved existence of suitable standard numerical codes, not a sequence-coding theorem inside every model of \(Q\).

The decoder has a bounded representing formula

In the formula below, \(m\) abbreviates the arithmetic term \(S((Si)\cdot d)\), whose standard value is \((i+1)d+1\). Define

\[B(c,d,i,z)\;:\!\Longleftrightarrow\;z\lt m\ \land\ \exists q\le c\;(c=mq+z).\]

This is a \(\Delta_0\) formula and strongly represents the total function \(\beta\) in \(Q\).

Proof

In the standard natural numbers, \(m\ge1\); division supplies the quotient and the unique remainder, with quotient at most \(c\). Thus the formula defines the stated decoder. Fix numerical inputs \(c,d,i\). In any model of \(Q\), the bound on \(z\) restricts it to the finitely many standard numerals below the standard value of \(m\). The quotient bound similarly restricts \(q\) to standard numerals at most \(c\). The finite cut lemma and numeral computation therefore establish, uniformly in the remaining output variable \(z\), that \(B(\overline c,\overline d,\overline i,z)\) holds exactly at the standard remainder. By completeness this is the required universal \(Q\)-proof. This also covers \(d=0\), when the modulus is \(1\) and the output is zero. □

Initial functions and composition are represented

Zero, successor and projections are represented by \(y=0\), \(y=Sx\), and \(y=x_j\). Representable total functions are closed under finite-arity composition.

Proof

Numeral computation gives the initial cases. If \(G_j\) represents \(g_j\), and \(F\) represents \(f\), use

\[\exists z_0\cdots\exists z_{r-1}\;\bigl(\bigwedge_{j\lt r}G_j(\bar x,z_j)\land F(z_0,\ldots,z_{r-1},y)\bigr).\]

Fix numerical inputs. Each \(G_j\) forces \(z_j\) to the standard numeral for \(g_j(\bar x)\), and the representing property of \(F\) forces the indicated final answer. The same clauses supply witnesses for existence. This works in every model of \(Q\), hence gives the universal output identity by completeness. Empty tuples give constants and require no witnesses. □

We may consequently use represented auxiliary functions as formula abbreviations: replace their values by fresh variables and adjoining graph formulas. This is a syntactic substitution construction, not an expansion of the arithmetic language.

A search that always finds a zero is represented

Suppose the total function \(f(\bar x,t)\) is represented by \(F(\bar x,t,z)\), and for every standard \(\bar x\) some \(t\) satisfies \(f(\bar x,t)=0\). Then the total function \(g(\bar x)=\min\{t:f(\bar x,t)=0\}\) is represented by

\[G(\bar x,y):=F(\bar x,y,0)\land\forall w\lt y\;\neg F(\bar x,w,0).\]

This use of minimization is called regular: every search has a witness, and its tested function is total.

Proof

Fix standard inputs and let \(b=g(\bar a)\). The representing proofs for the finitely many tests at \(0,\ldots,b\), together with the finite cut below \(\overline b\), prove \(G(\overline{\bar a},\overline b)\). For any possible output \(y\), the cut dichotomy puts it among the numerals at most \(b\), or gives \(\overline b\lt y\). Earlier numeral candidates fail their zero test. A candidate beyond \(b\) fails the bounded universal clause, since its earlier candidates include \(\overline b\), whose zero test has a proof. Thus exactly \(y=\overline b\) is possible in every model of \(Q\). Apply completeness. □

The bound is \(w\lt y\), the candidate being minimized. We do not use this argument to skip an undefined earlier computation in partial sequential search.

Represent a recursion by its finite history

Suppose \(G\) and \(H\) represent total functions \(g(\bar x)\) and \(h(\bar x,i,a)\). The function \(r\) specified by

\[r(\bar x,0)=g(\bar x),\qquad r(\bar x,i+1)=h(\bar x,i,r(\bar x,i))\]

is represented in \(Q\).

Proof

Describe a history up to \(n\) by two existentially quantified code variables \(c,d\). Require the following four conditions:

  1. For every \(i\le n\), some \(a\) satisfies \(B(c,d,i,a)\).
  2. Every \(a\) satisfying \(B(c,d,0,a)\) satisfies \(G(\bar x,a)\).
  3. For every \(i\lt n\) and every \(a,b\), if \(B(c,d,i,a)\) and \(B(c,d,Si,b)\), then \(H(\bar x,i,a,b)\).
  4. The proposed output \(y\) satisfies \(B(c,d,n,y)\).

Their conjunction under \(\exists c\exists d\) is one arithmetic formula \(R(\bar x,n,y)\). The quantifiers over \(a,b\) are unrestricted. We do not assume that \(B\) has a unique decoded value for arbitrary nonstandard codes.

Fix standard \(\bar a,n\). The actual finite list \(r(\bar a,0),\ldots,r(\bar a,n)\) has standard codes by . For those fixed codes, makes all decoded values exactly their expected numerals in every \(Q\)-model. The representing properties of \(G,H\) verify the initial and transition clauses, and the finite cut verifies them for every permitted index. This supplies a history and the correct output.

Conversely consider any codes satisfying the four conditions in a model of \(Q\). Condition 1 supplies a value at each standard index. Condition 2 and the representing property of \(G\) force every initial decoded value to be \(\overline{g(\bar a)}\). Induct externally through the fixed finite list: if every value at index \(i\) is the correct numeral, choose one such value; condition 3 and the representing property of \(H\) force every value at \(i+1\) to be the correct next numeral. Condition 4 therefore forces \(y=\overline{r(\bar a,n)}\). Completeness gives the universal output identity. □

Induction on a primitive-recursive construction, using , now shows that every primitive-recursive function is represented. The argument uses external finite induction, not an induction axiom of \(Q\).

Every total computable function has a representing formula

For every total recursive function \(f:\mathbb N^k\to\mathbb N\), there is one arithmetic formula \(F(\bar x,y)\) such that, for every \(\bar a\in\mathbb N^k\),

\[Q\vdash\forall y\,\bigl(F(\overline{\bar a},y)\leftrightarrow y=\overline{f(\bar a)}\bigr).\]

Proof

Primitive-recursive functions are represented by , and regular minimization preserves representation by . To connect this with the corpus's definition of total recursive functions, use its primitive-recursive bounded evaluation. For a fixed program and input, a positive finite computation has a primitive-recursively checkable certificate; the output is primitive-recursively read from that certificate. Package the certificate and output by the existing effective pairing. Thus there are primitive-recursive functions \(T,U\) such that \(T(\bar x,w)=0\) exactly when \(w\) certifies a terminating run on \(\bar x\), and \(U(w)\) is its output.

The existing normal-form theorem is stated for one numerical input. For a fixed finite input arity, use the existing finite-tuple packing and its coordinate decoders. The unary function obtained by decoding an input before running \(f\) is total recursive; apply the unary theorem and compose its certificate test with the tuple encoder. This gives the stated \(T(\bar x,w)\). Zero input arity gives a constant function.

Because the program computes a total function, every input has such a certificate. Consequently

\[f(\bar x)=U\bigl(\min\{w:T(\bar x,w)=0\}\bigr).\]

The search is regular over a total primitive-recursive test. Represent its two functions, apply regular minimization, and compose. This proves the result for the existing notion of total computability; it does not assume that every intermediate function in an arbitrary partial-recursive construction is total. □

The theorem supplies an arithmetic representing formula. The universal guards used for histories and least witnesses have not been shown to preserve the restricted \(\Sigma_1\) syntax defined above, and that extra syntactic assertion is not part of this theorem.

A total numerical function is recursive if and only if it is represented in \(Q\) in the sense of .

Proof

The forward direction is . Conversely fix a representing formula \(F\) and numerical input \(\bar a\). Search through pairs \((b,p)\), where \(p\) is a proof certificate for \(F(\overline{\bar a},\overline b)\). This is an effective search: numerals and substitutions are effectively coded, the axioms of \(Q\) form a fixed finite set, and proof certificates recognize the actual calculus. Some proof exists at \(b=f(\bar a)\), so the search terminates.

It cannot return a wrong answer. The representing universal theorem proves that any such output equals \(\overline{f(\bar a)}\). Soundness in \(\mathbb N_A\), or consistency together with distinct numeral proofs, excludes a different numeral. Thus proof search computes exactly \(f\). Use the existing enumeration of theorems or, equivalently, search the primitive-recursive certificate predicate over paired candidates. □

Decidable relations have positive and negative numeral proofs

A relation on \(\mathbb N^k\) is recursive exactly when it has a numeralwise representing formula in \(Q\).

Proof

For a recursive relation \(R\), represent its total characteristic function \(\chi_R\), using the convention that true has value \(1\). If \(F\) is its representing formula, put \(A(\bar x)=F(\bar x,\overline1)\). A true instance gives a proof of \(A\). A false instance gives the output identity with zero; since \(Q\) proves \(\overline1\ne\overline0\), it gives a proof of \(\neg A\).

Conversely search in parallel for a \(Q\)-proof of \(A(\overline{\bar a})\) and one of its negation. The representing property ensures one exists; consistency of \(Q\), witnessed by its standard model, ensures they cannot both exist. The successful side decides \(R(\bar a)\), as in two-sided recognition. □

Which totality claim has been proved?

For a representing formula \(F\), compare “for every external \(\bar a\), \(Q\vdash\exists!y\,F(\overline{\bar a},y)\)” with \(Q\vdash\forall\bar x\exists!y\,F(\bar x,y)\). Does the representation theorem establish both?

Show answerHide answer

It establishes the first: use the correct numeral as a witness and the universal output identity for uniqueness. The proof may depend on the input tuple and its actual finite computation. The second asks for one finite proof covering every element of every \(Q\)-model, including nonstandard inputs. Our external construction of a proof for each standard tuple does not supply that universal proof. A failure of this argument to supply uniform totality is not, by itself, a theorem that the particular totality sentence is unprovable.
