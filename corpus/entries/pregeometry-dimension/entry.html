Independence can be checked on finite subsets

Let \(\operatorname{cl}\) be a pregeometry on a set \(X\). A subset \(I\subseteq X\) is independent if and only if every finite subset of \(I\) is independent.

An independent set has independent subsets by monotonicity. Conversely, suppose \(I\) is dependent. Some \(a\in I\) belongs to \(\operatorname{cl}(I\setminus\{a\})\). Finite character supplies a finite \(F\subseteq I\setminus\{a\}\) with \(a\in\operatorname{cl}(F)\). The finite set \(F\cup\{a\}\) is dependent, since removing \(a\) leaves exactly \(F\). This contradicts the finite-subset condition.

A chain of independent sets has independent union

If \(\mathcal C\) is a family of independent subsets of \(X\), linearly ordered by inclusion, then \(\bigcup\mathcal C\) is independent. The assertion includes the empty family.

Every finite subset \(F\) of the union lies in one member of \(\mathcal C\) when \(F\ne\varnothing\). For each point choose a member containing it; among finitely many chosen members, one contains all the others because the family is a chain. That member is independent, so \(F\) is independent. The empty finite set is independent without choosing a member. Apply . If the family is empty, its union is empty and the same conclusion holds.

Every independent set extends to a basis inside the generators

Let \(I\subseteq S\subseteq X\), with \(I\) independent. There is an independent set \(B\) such that

\[I\subseteq B\subseteq S,\qquad \operatorname{cl}(B)=\operatorname{cl}(S).\]

Consequently every closed set has a basis, and every independent subset of a closed set extends to a basis of it.

Order by inclusion the family of independent \(J\) satisfying \(I\subseteq J\subseteq S\). It is nonempty because it contains \(I\). The union of a nonempty chain still contains \(I\), lies in \(S\), and is independent by . The empty chain has \(I\) as an upper bound. Thus Zorn's lemma gives a maximal member \(B\).

If \(s\in S\setminus\operatorname{cl}(B)\), the adjoining lemma makes \(B\cup\{s\}\) an independent member strictly containing \(B\), a contradiction. Hence \(S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(B)\). Monotonicity and idempotence give \(\operatorname{cl}(S)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(B)\), and the reverse inclusion follows from \(B\subseteq S\). For closed \(S=C\), the equality becomes \(\operatorname{cl}(B)=C\), exactly the earlier basis definition.

Maximal independence and spanning describe the same subsets

Suppose \(B\subseteq S\) is independent. Then \(B\) is maximal among independent subsets of \(S\) if and only if \(\operatorname{cl}(B)=\operatorname{cl}(S)\).

Maximality implies the equality by the last paragraph of . Conversely assume equal closures, and let \(B\subseteq J\subseteq S\) with \(J\) independent. If a point \(a\) belongs to \(J\setminus B\), it lies in \(S\), and hence in \(\operatorname{cl}(B)\). But \(B\subseteq J\setminus\{a\}\), so monotonicity places \(a\) in \(\operatorname{cl}(J\setminus\{a\})\), contradicting independence. Thus \(J=B\).

A finite spanning set bounds even an initially arbitrary independent set

If \(F\subseteq X\) is finite, \(I\) is independent, and \(I\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(F)\), then \(I\) is finite and \(|I|\le |F|\).

Every finite \(J\subseteq I\) is independent and lies in \(\operatorname{cl}(F)\). The finite exchange bound gives \(|J|\le |F|\). If \(I\) had more than \(|F|\) points, one could choose \(|F|+1\) distinct points of it, obtaining a finite subset violating this bound. Therefore \(I\) has at most \(|F|\) points. This proves both finiteness and the claimed inequality.

Finite supports cover the other basis

Let \(B,D\) be bases of the same closed set \(C\). For each \(b\in B\), choose a finite \(F_b\subseteq D\) with \(b\in\operatorname{cl}(F_b)\). Such choices exist by finite character. They satisfy

\[D=\bigcup_{b\in B}F_b.\]

Write \(U\) for the union on the right. Clearly \(U\subseteq D\). Every \(b\in B\) belongs to \(\operatorname{cl}(U)\), so \(C=\operatorname{cl}(B)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(U)\). In particular \(D\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(U)\).

If \(d\in D\setminus U\), then \(U\subseteq D\setminus\{d\}\). Monotonicity would put \(d\) in \(\operatorname{cl}(D\setminus\{d\})\), contradicting independence of \(D\). Thus \(D\subseteq U\) as well. When \(B=\varnothing\), the argument forces \(D=\varnothing\); it does not require \(C=\varnothing\).

All bases of a closed set have the same cardinality

For every closed \(C\subseteq X\) and any bases \(B,D\) of \(C\),

\[|B|=|D|.\]

This includes finite bases, infinite bases and the empty basis.

If \(B\) is finite, apply to \(D\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(B)\). It makes \(D\) finite and gives \(|D|\le |B|\). Reverse the roles of the bases to get the other inequality. The same argument settles any case in which either basis is finite.

Now suppose both bases are infinite. Use the finite supports in . A union of \(|B|\) finite sets has cardinality at most \(|B|\cdot\aleph_0=|B|\), by infinite cardinal arithmetic. Explicitly, well-order each finite \(F_b\) using one well-order of \(D\). Its incidence set \(\{(b,d):b\in B,\ d\in F_b\}\) injects into \(B\times\mathbb N\) by the position of \(d\) in that finite order, and projects onto the union. Hence \(|D|\le |B|\). Choosing finite supports in \(B\) for points of \(D\) gives \(|B|\le |D|\). Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein yields equality.

Dimension is a cardinal, including for infinite generating sets

The dimension of a subset \(S\subseteq X\) is

\[\dim(S)=|B|,\]

where \(B\subseteq S\) is independent and \(\operatorname{cl}(B)=\operatorname{cl}(S)\). Such a \(B\) exists by with \(I=\varnothing\), and its cardinality is independent of the choice by . In particular \(\dim(S)=\dim(\operatorname{cl}(S))\). For a finitely generated closed set, this cardinal is the finite cardinal corresponding to the earlier natural-number dimension.

Treat a parameter base as already available

For \(A\subseteq X\), the localization at \(A\) is the closure operation

\[\operatorname{cl}_A(S)=\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S).\]

A set is independent over \(A\) when it is independent for this localized closure. A basis of \(S\) over \(A\) is an independent-over-\(A\) subset \(B\subseteq S\) with \(\operatorname{cl}(A\cup B)=\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)\).

Localization retains all the pregeometry laws

For every \(A\subseteq X\), the operation \(\operatorname{cl}_A\) is a pregeometry.

Extensivity and monotonicity follow from those of \(\operatorname{cl}\). For idempotence, \(A\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)\), so adjoining \(A\) again does not change the closure: \(\operatorname{cl}(A\cup\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S))=\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)\).

If \(x\in\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)\), choose a finite \(F\subseteq A\cup S\) with \(x\in\operatorname{cl}(F)\). The finite set \(F\cap S\subseteq S\) is sufficient in the localized closure, because \(F\subseteq A\cup(F\cap S)\). Conversely any finite localized witness is contained in \(\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)\) by monotonicity. This proves finite character without requiring \(A\) to be finite.

Finally, apply ordinary exchange with base \(A\cup S\): after reassociating unions, its hypothesis and conclusion are exactly localized exchange. Thus every axiom is retained.

Bases over any parameter set exist and have a well-defined size

Every independent-over-\(A\) subset \(I\subseteq S\) extends inside \(S\) to a basis over \(A\). All bases of \(S\) over \(A\) have the same cardinality. Their common cardinal is the relative dimension \(\dim(S/A)\), equivalently the dimension of \(S\) in \(\operatorname{cl}_A\).

Apply and to the localized pregeometry from . Its spanning identity is precisely \(\operatorname{cl}(A\cup B)=\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)\), so this proves the assertions for the stated definition, rather than for a different closure.

Only the closure of the base matters, and a spanned set has dimension zero

For all \(A,S\subseteq X\), localization at \(A\) equals localization at \(\operatorname{cl}(A)\). Moreover

\[\dim(S/A)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(A).\]

Every set independent over \(A\) is disjoint from \(\operatorname{cl}(A)\).

The equality \(\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)=\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(A)\cup S)\) follows by monotonicity in one direction, and by \(\operatorname{cl}(A)\cup S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(A\cup S)\) followed by idempotence in the other.

If the relative dimension is zero, a basis \(B\) has cardinal zero and hence is empty. Its spanning identity gives \(S\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(A)\). Conversely, if this containment holds, the empty set is a relative basis, proving dimension zero. If \(i\) were both in an independent-over-\(A\) set \(I\) and in \(\operatorname{cl}(A)\), then \(i\in\operatorname{cl}(A\cup(I\setminus\{i\}))\) by monotonicity, contradicting independence.

The general dimension applies to the algebraic closure already constructed

For a model \(M\) of a strongly minimal theory, every algebraically independent \(I\subseteq S\subseteq M\) extends to a basis of \(\operatorname{acl}_M(S)\) inside \(S\). All such bases have equal cardinality. The same extension and cardinality assertions hold over every parameter set \(A\subseteq M\), provided the starting set \(I\) is independent over \(A\). In that case the spanning condition is \(\operatorname{acl}_M(A\cup B)=\operatorname{acl}_M(A\cup S)\).

The earlier algebraic pregeometry has exactly \(\operatorname{acl}_M\) as its closure. Apply and to it, and to its localization at \(A\). Thus the new cardinal dimension extends the finite geometry in that same specified model.

For models of one complete strongly minimal theory, a specified bijection between model bases extends to an isomorphism. Thus dimension classifies these models, an additional model-theoretic conclusion beyond equality of basis sizes.

For identity closure, relative dimension counts points outside the base

The identity pregeometry on \(X\) has \(\operatorname{cl}(S)=S\). Every subset is independent, its only basis is itself, and

\[\dim(S)=|S|,\qquad\dim(S/A)=|S\setminus A|.\]

The closure axioms and exchange follow directly from membership; finite character uses the singleton witness \(\{x\}\). Independence says that a point is absent after it is removed. A spanning independent subset of \(S\) must equal \(S\), since closure changes nothing.

After localization, \(\operatorname{cl}_A(S)=A\cup S\). An independent-over-\(A\) subset cannot contain a point of \(A\), and every subset disjoint from \(A\) is independent over it. Thus \(S\setminus A\) is the unique relative basis of \(S\), proving the second equality. This is also the pregeometry of algebraic closure in infinite equality structures.

Can relative dimension be recovered by subtracting infinite cardinals?

In identity closure on \(\mathbb N\), compare the parameter sets \(A=\{2n:n\in\mathbb N\}\) and \(A'=\mathbb N\). Their cardinalities are equal. Must \(\dim(\mathbb N/A)=\dim(\mathbb N/A')\)?

No. The complement of the even numbers is the odd numbers, which are countably infinite, so gives \(\dim(\mathbb N/A)=\aleph_0\). But \(\mathbb N\setminus A'=\varnothing\), giving \(\dim(\mathbb N/A')=0\). Both base cardinalities and the absolute dimension are \(\aleph_0\).

Infinite cardinal addition has \(\aleph_0+\aleph_0=\aleph_0=\aleph_0+0\). There is no subtraction operation recovering a unique missing summand from these equalities. Relative dimension is defined by a localized basis, rather than by cancelling the cardinality of the base.
