Alternate along a finite descending chain

A nested enumerable chain of length \(n\) is a finite family of c.e. sets \(U_0,\ldots,U_{n-1}\subseteq\mathbb N\) with \(U_{i+1}\subseteq U_i\) whenever \(i+1<n\). Extend its notation by \(U_n=\varnothing\). Its alternating difference is

\[D_n(U)=\bigcup_{\substack{i<n\\i\text{ even}}}(U_i\setminus U_{i+1}).\]

For odd \(n\), the last term is \(U_{n-1}\); for positive even \(n\), the last term is \(U_{n-2}\setminus U_{n-1}\). The length-zero family has value \(D_0(U)=\varnothing\). The sets need not be distinct or nonempty. The union is finite, and its notation does not assert that the differences themselves are enumerable.

Count how far an input reaches down the chain

The membership rank of \(x\) in the first \(n\) sets of a family \(U\) is

\[r_n(U,x)=\sum_{i<n}[x\in U_i].\]

A bracket is one when its condition holds and zero otherwise. This defines a numerical function even when no membership algorithm for \(U_i\) is available. For a nested chain, its membership indices form the initial segment \(0,\ldots,r_n(U,x)-1\): membership at index \(i\) forces membership at every smaller index. Thus \(0\le r_n(U,x)\le n\), and \(x\in U_i\) exactly when \(i<r_n(U,x)\), for \(i<n\).

An odd membership rank gives exactly the alternating difference

For every nested chain \(U\) of length \(n\) and every input \(x\),

\[x\in D_n(U)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_n(U,x)\bmod2=1.\]

Write \(r=r_n(U,x)\). If \(r=0\), the input is in none of the sets and in none of the differences. If \(r>0\), its only possible departure from the chain is at index \(r-1\): it belongs to \(U_{r-1}\) and not to \(U_r\), with the sentinel \(U_n=\varnothing\) handling \(r=n\). For smaller indices it belongs to both consecutive sets; for larger ones it belongs to neither. Therefore the union includes \(x\) exactly when \(r-1\) is even, which is equivalent to \(r\) being odd. This proves the equivalence at the two boundaries as well as at an interior departure.

The parity of a revision count is the current answer

If a binary approximation \(a(s,x)\) starts at zero, its prefix change counter satisfies

\[a(s,x)=c_a(s,x)\bmod2\qquad\text{for every }s,x.\]

Induct on \(s\), keeping \(x\) fixed. At stage zero both sides are zero. If the next answer agrees with the old one, the counter is unchanged, so the induction hypothesis remains true. If the answers differ, binary values force a switch from zero to one or from one to zero. The counter increases by exactly one; its parity switches in the same way. These two cases establish the successor step. The initial-bit assumption supplies the base case and cannot be omitted.

Enumerate the inputs which reach each revision threshold

For a jointly computable approximation \(a\), its change-threshold sets are

\[C_i(a)=\{x:\exists s\;i+1\le c_a(s,x)\}\qquad(i\in\mathbb N).\]

These sets are uniformly c.e.: on the supplied pair \((i,x)\), compute the counter at successive stages and accept when it is at least \(i+1\). The counter is jointly computable, so every individual test terminates. This is one recognizer with \(i\) as an input, rather than a separate unconnected existence claim for each threshold. They are nested, since reaching \(i+2\) changes entails reaching at least \(i+1\). If \(a\) has at most \(n\) changes, then \(C_i(a)=\varnothing\) for \(i\ge n\).

A bounded counter has an attained largest value

Suppose \(c_a(s,x)\le n\) for all \(s,x\). For each fixed \(x\), there are \(r\le n\) and a stage \(t\) such that \(c_a(t,x)=r\), every counter value is at most \(r\), and \(c_a(s,x)=r\) for all \(s\ge t\). Moreover,

\[x\in C_i(a)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i<r.\]

The range of the counter on this input is a nonempty subset of the finite set \(\{0,\ldots,n\}\). Choose its largest value \(r\), and a stage \(t\) attaining it. The counter is nondecreasing, so maximality forces every later value to equal \(r\). A witnessed threshold has \(i+1\le r\). Conversely, if \(i<r\), the attaining stage itself witnesses the threshold. The stage \(t\) may depend on \(x\); this finite-range argument does not supply a computable settling function.

The threshold chain reconstructs the limit set

If \(a\) is a computable binary approximation starting at zero, converging to \(\chi_A\), and having at most \(n\) changes, then its first \(n\) threshold sets form a nested enumerable chain and

\[A=D_n(C(a)).\]

Choose a stage after both the counter has attained \(r\) and the approximation has reached its final answer. At that stage gives \(\chi_A(x)=r\bmod2\). By , this bit is one exactly when \(x\in D_n(C(a))\). This proves equality for the specified threshold chain. If \(n=0\), no thresholds occur and the same argument yields the empty set.

Add the discoveries in the supplied enumerations

Choose program-domain indices \(e_0,\ldots,e_{n-1}\), so \(U_i=W_{e_i}\). Let \(W_{e_i,s}\) be their canonical finite stages. Define the stage rank and its parity approximation by

\[R(s,x)=\sum_{i<n}[x\in W_{e_i,s}],\qquad b(s,x)=R(s,x)\bmod2.\]

This is a specified ordinary algorithm. Set an accumulator to zero; for each of the finitely many stored indices, compute its stage-membership bit and add it. Then take the remainder modulo two. Each test is uniformly computable, so \(R\) and \(b\) are jointly computable in \((s,x)\) for the supplied finite list of indices. The starting rank is zero, since every canonical stage at time zero is empty. Each summand is nondecreasing in \(s\), so \(R(s,x)\le R(s+1,x)\) and \(R(s,x)\le n\). The finite snapshots need not be nested; these numerical facts use only the separate increasing enumerations.

Each parity revision spends at least one discovery

Let \(R(s,x)\) be a jointly computable numerical function with \(R(0,x)=0\), nondecreasing in \(s\), and bounded by \(n\). Then \(b(s,x)=R(s,x)\bmod2\) is computable, binary, starts at zero, and satisfies

\[c_b(s,x)\le R(s,x)\le n.\]

Computability follows by numerical composition; a remainder modulo two is zero or one. Prove the counter inequality by induction on \(s\). At stage zero both sides are zero. If the parity stays fixed at the next stage, its counter does not increase, and monotonicity of \(R\) preserves the inequality. If the parity changes, the two rank values differ. Their monotonicity makes the new rank at least the old rank plus one, which pays for the single new revision. This also handles a stage at which several enumeration bits change together: the rank may jump by several units, while its parity still changes at most once. The invariant proves the bound for every finite prefix.

A finite list of stage bits eventually has its final sum

For the indices in and every fixed \(x\), some stage \(t\) satisfies

\[R(s,x)=r_n(U,x)\qquad\text{for every }s\ge t.\]

For each of the finitely many indices, its stage bit converges to \([x\in U_i]\). If \(x\in U_i\), choose a stage at which its accepting computation has been discovered; every later stage retains that discovery. If \(x\notin U_i\), its bit is zero at all stages. The maximum of these finitely many thresholds works for all summands, and therefore for their sum. With an empty index list take \(t=0\). This argument again supplies a threshold for each fixed input, without making that threshold computable.

The stage-rank parity realizes the supplied alternating difference

If \(U_i=W_{e_i}\), for \(i<n\), form a nested enumerable chain, then the specified parity approximation \(b\) starts at zero, has at most \(n\) changes, and converges to \(\chi_{D_n(U)}\). In particular \(D_n(U)\) is \(n\)-c.e.

A finite revision allowance is exactly a finite nested difference

For every \(n\in\mathbb N\) and \(A\subseteq\mathbb N\), \(A\) is \(n\)-c.e. if and only if it is the alternating difference of some nested enumerable chain of length \(n\).

What do three and four nested cancellation levels describe?

Take \(U_0=\mathbb N\), \(U_1=2\mathbb N\), \(U_2=4\mathbb N\), and \(U_3=8\mathbb N\), where \(d\mathbb N\) is the set of multiples of \(d\). Determine the length-three and length-four alternating differences. Must these examples need three or four changes? For each length, use only that initial segment, with its own empty sentinel.

The first is the odd numbers together with the multiples of four: \(D_3=(\mathbb N\setminus2\mathbb N)\cup4\mathbb N\). The second is the odd numbers together with multiples of four that are not multiples of eight: \(D_4=(\mathbb N\setminus2\mathbb N)\cup(4\mathbb N\setminus8\mathbb N)\). Their ranks explain the successive cancellations.

Can the parity formula ignore the nesting hypothesis?

For two arbitrary c.e. sets, does the parity of their membership rank necessarily describe \(U_0\setminus U_1\)? Test \(U_0=\varnothing\) and \(U_1=\mathbb N\).

Show answerHide answer

No. Every input has rank one in this family, so the odd-rank set is all of \(\mathbb N\), whereas \(U_0\setminus U_1=\varnothing\). The family is not nested. The stage-rank algorithm always computes the parity of its membership bits; the nesting hypothesis is what identifies that parity with an alternating chain difference. It is a hypothesis on the final sets, not an assumption that every temporary snapshot already respects all inclusions.
