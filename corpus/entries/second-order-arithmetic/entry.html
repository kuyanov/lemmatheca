One sentence asks every subset to satisfy induction

First-order Peano arithmetic has nonstandard models even though it includes every first-order induction axiom. The reason was precise: induction reaches every subset defined by a formula with parameters, whereas the standard copy need not be definable. Second-order quantification lets us ask for the stronger condition directly.

Keep the arithmetic language with zero, successor, addition and multiplication. Write the full induction sentence as

\[\mathrm{Ind}_2:=\forall X\,\bigl[(X(0)\land\forall x\,(X(x)\to X(Sx)))\to\forall x\,X(x)\bigr].\]

The variable \(X\) is unary. Under full semantics, it ranges over every subset of the individual domain. Thus an arithmetic structure \(M\) satisfies this sentence exactly when every subset \(A\subseteq|M|\) containing \(0^M\) and closed under \(S^M\) equals \(|M|\). A unary relation and its subset of satisfying elements are two descriptions of the same object.

The sentence is finite. It does not contain an infinite disjunction of numeral equalities or an axiom for each subset. The universal second-order quantifier supplies the range of all subsets when the sentence is interpreted. The choice of that range is the difference from the first-order induction schema.

The second-order Peano theory has finitely many axioms

Define second-order Peano arithmetic, written \(\mathrm{PA}_2\), to consist of the seven universally closed axioms of Robinson arithmetic together with \(\mathrm{Ind}_2\). For clarity, its first-order part is

  1. \(\forall x\,Sx\ne0\).
  2. \(\forall x\forall y\,(Sx=Sy\to x=y)\).
  3. \(\forall x\,(x\ne0\to\exists y\,x=Sy)\).
  4. \(\forall x\,x+0=x\).
  5. \(\forall x\forall y\,x+Sy=S(x+y)\).
  6. \(\forall x\,x\cdot0=0\).
  7. \(\forall x\forall y\,x\cdot Sy=x\cdot y+x\).

Each first-order sentence is also a second-order sentence with no relation quantifiers. All quantifiers in this entry are interpreted in a full second-order structure unless we explicitly compare first-order semantics. Domains are nonempty.

The predecessor axiom is redundant: full induction applied to the subset consisting of zero and all successors gives it. Retaining it lets us reuse the same seven-axiom theory \(Q\) throughout. Nothing depends on an irredundant presentation. In particular, \(\mathrm{PA}_2\) is a finite set of at most eight sentences, not the infinite first-order theory \(\mathrm{PA}\).

The standard arithmetic structure is a full model

The natural-number structure \(\mathbb N_A\), with every finite-arity relation available, satisfies \(\mathrm{PA}_2\).

The ordinary operations satisfy the seven \(Q\)-axioms, as already checked. Let \(A\subseteq\mathbb N\) contain zero and be successor-closed. Ordinary induction proves that every natural number belongs to \(A\). Since this works for an arbitrary subset, the semantic condition for \(\mathrm{Ind}_2\) holds. These two verifications establish every axiom.

Successor determines the canonical map from the natural numbers

For any arithmetic structure \(M\), recursion defines a unique map \(j_M:\mathbb N\to|M|\) satisfying

\[j_M(0)=0^M,\qquad j_M(n+1)=S^M(j_M(n)).\]

Its value is the interpretation \(n_M\) of the numeral \(\overline n\). If \(M\models Q\), then \(j_M\) is injective.

The displayed equations are ordinary recursion on the external natural numbers. They are precisely the defining equations for numeral interpretation. Any other map preserving zero and successor agrees at zero and, if it agrees at \(n\), agrees at \(n+1\); ordinary induction proves uniqueness.

For injectivity, suppose \(m<n\) and \(j_M(m)=j_M(n)\). Cancel \(m\) successors using injectivity of \(S^M\). This yields \(0^M=j_M(n-m)\). The right side is a successor since \(n-m>0\), contradicting the first axiom. Thus unequal indices have unequal images. This is the canonical embedding constructed for models of \(Q\); full induction has not yet been used.

Full induction makes the canonical map surjective

If an arithmetic structure \(M\) satisfies \(\mathrm{Ind}_2\), then every element of \(M\) is \(j_M(n)\) for some external natural number \(n\).

Consider the actual subset

\[A:=\{j_M(n):n\in\mathbb N\}\subseteq|M|.\]

It contains zero, witnessed by \(n=0\). If \(a\in A\), choose \(n\) with \(a=j_M(n)\). Then \(S^M(a)=j_M(n+1)\), so \(S^M(a)\in A\). Apply full induction to this subset: \(A=|M|\). Unpacking membership in the image gives surjectivity.

This use of \(A\) needs no formula defining it inside \(M\). Full semantics provides it as a legitimate value for \(X\). In a nonstandard first-order model the same subset is proper and undefinable; consequently that model fails \(\mathrm{Ind}_2\) under full semantics.

The recursion equations determine addition and multiplication

If \(M\models Q\), then for every \(m,n\in\mathbb N\),

\[j_M(m+n)=j_M(m)+^M j_M(n).\]
\[j_M(mn)=j_M(m)\cdot^M j_M(n).\]

If additionally \(M\models\mathrm{Ind}_2\), these identities determine the operations on every pair of elements of \(M\).

Fix \(m\) and induct on the ordinary index \(n\). At zero, the addition equation is axiom 4. For the step, axiom 5 and the induction hypothesis give

\[j_M(m)+^M j_M(n+1)=S^M(j_M(m)+^M j_M(n)).\]

By the induction hypothesis the last expression is \(S^M(j_M(m+n))=j_M(m+n+1)\).

For multiplication, axiom 6 gives the base case. Axiom 7, the multiplication induction hypothesis and the addition identity give

\[j_M(m)\cdot^M j_M(n+1)=j_M(mn)+^M j_M(m)=j_M(mn+m).\]

These are the numeral-computation identities, with the same map now called \(j_M\). Finally, surjectivity expresses arbitrary \(a,b\in M\) as \(j_M(m),j_M(n)\). Hence their sum and product must be the displayed values. Any other operations satisfying the same recursion equations with the same zero and successor give those same values by the same external inductions, so coincide on all pairs.

Every full Peano model is the standard arithmetic structure

For every full model \(M\models\mathrm{PA}_2\), the canonical map \(j_M\) is an arithmetic isomorphism \(\mathbb N_A\cong M\). It is the unique such isomorphism. Consequently any two full models of \(\mathrm{PA}_2\) are isomorphic: \(\mathrm{PA}_2\) is categorical under full semantics.

The first-order axioms give injectivity by . Full induction gives surjectivity by . The map preserves zero and successor by definition, and addition and multiplication by . Thus it is a bijection preserving every symbol of the arithmetic language, which is exactly an arithmetic isomorphism.

An isomorphism \(f:\mathbb N_A\to M\) preserves zero and successor, so the uniqueness part of gives \(f(n)=j_M(n)\) for every \(n\). Thus no second isomorphism is possible. For two models \(M,N\), compose \(j_M^{-1}\) with \(j_N\) to obtain the isomorphism \(M\to N\).

This is an isomorphism theorem, not a decision procedure for all second-order arithmetic statements. Its proof takes place in the surrounding mathematics, where natural-number recursion and all subsets are available.

The fixed finite Peano axioms also turn arithmetic truth into full second-order validity: an arithmetic sentence is true in the standard model exactly when its implication from their conjunction is fully valid.

Does a full second-order Peano model satisfy first-order induction?

Let \(M\models\mathrm{PA}_2\). Does it satisfy every first-order induction axiom, including instances with parameters? Why is there no contradiction with the existence of nonstandard first-order models?

Yes. Fix a first-order formula \(\varphi(x,\bar y)\) and a parameter tuple \(\bar b\) from \(M\). Its satisfying set \(A=\{a\in M:M\models\varphi(a,\bar b)\}\) is one of the subsets available under full semantics. If the base case and induction step hold, then \(0^M\in A\) and \(A\) is successor-closed. Full induction gives \(A=M\), which is exactly the conclusion of that first-order instance. The parameters stay fixed throughout.

The converse fails. A nonstandard model of first-order PA satisfies all such definable-subset instances, but its proper standard copy is a subset witnessing failure of full induction. The two theories impose different semantic conditions.

A fresh constant can avoid any finite set of numerals

Add a fresh individual constant \(c\) to the arithmetic language, and form the second-order theory

\[T^*:=\mathrm{PA}_2\cup\{c\ne\overline n:n\in\mathbb N\}.\]

Every finite subset \(\Delta\subseteq T^*\) has a full model, even one whose arithmetic reduct is \(\mathbb N_A\).

Only finitely many constraints \(c\ne\overline n\) occur in \(\Delta\). Let \(F\subseteq\mathbb N\) collect their indices and choose \(r\notin F\), for example one larger than every listed index; take \(r=0\) if none occur. Expand \(\mathbb N_A\) by interpreting \(c\) as \(r\). Keep full relation ranges on the unchanged domain.

The expansion satisfies every \(\mathrm{PA}_2\)-axiom, since none mentions \(c\), and \(r\ne n\) for every constraint in \(\Delta\). Therefore it satisfies \(\Delta\). Different finite subsets may choose different values for \(c\); a single value avoiding every numeral has not been produced.

Full second-order semantics is not compact

The theory \(T^*\) has no full model, although every finite subset has a full model. Thus the compactness theorem for first-order logic fails for full second-order semantics.

Suppose a full structure satisfies \(T^*\), and let \(M\) be its arithmetic reduct. Its relation ranges remain all relations on the same individual domain, and it satisfies \(\mathrm{PA}_2\). By , the interpretation of \(c\) equals \(j_M(n)=n_M\) for some natural number \(n\). But the sentence \(c\ne\overline n\) belongs to \(T^*\), a contradiction. Finite satisfiability was proved in .

For first-order PA the same construction produced a nonstandard model. Full induction blocks exactly the extra element that compactness would need. The contradiction uses the interpretation of one finite second-order induction sentence, not an infinite formula.

A theory with an infinite full model need not have larger models

The finite theory \(\mathrm{PA}_2\) has an infinite full model, but every full model has cardinality \(\aleph_0\). In particular it has no uncountable full model and no full models of arbitrarily large cardinality.

The standard structure is an infinite full model. For any other full model, supplies the bijection \(j_M:\mathbb N\to M\). Hence its domain has the same cardinality as \(\mathbb N\). This contradicts the conclusion that upward Löwenheim–Skolem would give for a first-order theory with an infinite model.

This example establishes failure of the upward conclusion for full second-order logic. It does not establish failure of a downward Löwenheim–Skolem conclusion: all these models already have countable domains.
