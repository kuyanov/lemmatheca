Ask which vocabulary carries a consequence

Let \(\varphi,\psi\) be sentences of a first-order language \(L\). Write \(\operatorname{sym}(\varphi)\) for the finite set of function and relation symbols actually occurring in \(\varphi\), retaining their kinds and arities. Equality is logical and is not in this set. All structures have nonempty domains, and \(\varphi\models\psi\) means that every \(L\)-structure satisfying \(\varphi\) satisfies \(\psi\).

An interpolant for this consequence is a sentence \(\theta\) such that

\[\varphi\models\theta,\qquad\theta\models\psi,\qquad \operatorname{sym}(\theta)\subseteq\operatorname{sym}(\varphi)\cap\operatorname{sym}(\psi).\]

The last condition is essential: taking \(\theta=\varphi\) always meets the first two conditions, but can retain symbols absent from \(\psi\). The symbol-support definition applies to the actual syntax, not just to symbols on which a particular structure's truth value happens to depend.

A shared atom can carry the whole implication

For distinct propositional atoms \(p,q,r\), the consequence \(p\land q\models q\lor r\) has interpolant \(q\). More generally, for a finite propositional formula \(A\), eliminating an atom \(p\) by the formula \(A[p:=\bot]\lor A[p:=\top]\) gives exactly the assignments to the remaining atoms that extend to an assignment satisfying \(A\).

If \(p\land q\) is true, then \(q\) is true; if \(q\) is true, then \(q\lor r\) is true. The only common atom is \(q\). For the general assertion, any truth value assigned to \(p\) is either false or true. Thus one of the two substituted formulas is true exactly when such an extension exists. This is the semantic operation implemented by resolution elimination. First-order quantifiers and functions prevent this two-case argument from being a general first-order proof.

Move each sentence into its exact finite language

Put \(S=\operatorname{sym}(\varphi)\), \(T=\operatorname{sym}(\psi)\), \(L_1=L\upharpoonright S\), \(L_2=L\upharpoonright T\), and \(L_0=L\upharpoonright(S\cap T)\). There are sentences \(\varphi_1\) of \(L_1\) and \(\psi_2\) of \(L_2\) whose translations along the inclusions are exactly \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\). If \(\eta\) is an \(L_0\)-sentence, its translation \(\theta\) into \(L\) satisfies \(\operatorname{sym}(\theta)\subseteq S\cap T\).

Use restriction to symbol support: replace each symbol by itself together with the certificate that it belongs to the chosen finite set. Translating back removes those certificates, so recovers the original syntax. An induction proves the last assertion. Variables have no nonlogical symbols; a function or relation application introduces only its own certified symbol and those in its arguments; a connective takes the union of its children's supports; a quantifier introduces none.

Restriction applies equally to \(\neg\psi\), because negation adds no nonlogical symbol. The ambient language \(L\) may be uncountable: only the two active sublanguages are finite.

Every first-order consequence has a common-language interpolant

Craig interpolation. If \(\varphi\models\psi\), then there is an \(L_0\)-sentence \(\eta\) whose translation \(\theta\) to \(L\) satisfies \(\varphi\models\theta\models\psi\). In particular, \(\theta\) is an interpolant in the sense above. No countability assumption is made on \(L\).

Consider the two singleton theories \(\{\varphi_1\}\) and \(\{\neg\psi_2\}\). Their translated union has no model, since such a model would satisfy both \(\varphi\) and \(\neg\psi\), contrary to the assumed consequence. By the joint-model theorem, these two theories are not inseparable. Hence there is an actual \(L_0\)-sentence \(\eta\) such that

\[\{\varphi_1\}\vdash\eta,\qquad \{\neg\psi_2\}\vdash\neg\eta.\]

Here each occurrence of \(\eta\) means its inclusion into that side's language. Translate these proofs into \(L\). Their common conclusions are the same sentence \(\theta\), since both composite language maps are the direct inclusion of \(L_0\) into \(L\). Soundness gives \(\varphi\models\theta\) and \(\neg\psi\models\neg\theta\). If a structure satisfied \(\theta\) and failed \(\psi\), the second consequence would make it satisfy \(\neg\theta\), a contradiction. Thus \(\theta\models\psi\). The support lemma gives the required vocabulary bound.

The joint-model theorem supplies the substantive construction: it makes two compatible term models agree on their common named part. We use its proved conclusion here rather than assuming that two separately consistent theories can always be joined.

The interpolant also factors an actual derivation

If \(\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi\) in the corpus's first-order calculus, there is an interpolant \(\theta\) with \(\{\varphi\}\vdash\theta\) and \(\{\theta\}\vdash\psi\). Conversely, two such derivations imply \(\{\varphi\}\vdash\psi\). The interpolant can be chosen with the same common-vocabulary bound as above.

Soundness turns the given derivation into a consequence. Apply sentence interpolation, then completeness for arbitrary languages to each of the two resulting consequences. Conversely, soundness gives the two consequences; their composition is \(\varphi\models\psi\), and completeness supplies the requested derivation. Thus the conclusion concerns the existing proof calculus, although the construction of the interpolant is semantic.

Turn a finite tuple of free variables into fresh constants

Fix distinct free names \(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}\). Let \(L[\bar c]\) add distinct new constants \(c_0,\ldots,c_{n-1}\), none belonging to \(L\). For an \(L\)-formula \(A(\bar x)\) with no other free names, let \(A^{\bar c}\) replace each free \(x_i\) by \(c_i\). Every \(L[\bar c]\)-sentence \(B\) conversely determines an \(L\)-formula \(B^{\bar x}\) by replacing those constants by the corresponding free names. These operations are inverse and satisfy

\[(M,\bar a)\models A^{\bar c}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ M\models A[\bar a],\qquad M\models B^{\bar x}[\bar a]\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (M,\bar a)\models B.\]

Here \((M,\bar a)\) interprets \(c_i\) as \(a_i\). In passing to \(B^{\bar x}\), an original \(L\)-symbol occurs exactly when that same symbol occurs in \(B\); the added constants become free names and contribute no \(L\)-symbol.

For terms, replace a variable leaf by its new constant leaf, or the new constant leaf by its variable. Original functions retain their arguments, so the two term operations are inverse and preserve values under the indicated assignments. Apply the operations recursively to equality and relation atoms and then to connectives and quantifiers. Free names are represented separately from quantified placeholders in the scoped syntax; replacing a constant by a free name therefore does not let an existing quantifier capture that name. Induction on the formula proves both inverse identities, both satisfaction identities, and the original-symbol assertion. This is the finite-name version of the named-sentence representation used earlier.

Interpolate formulas without introducing extra free names

Let \(\varphi(\bar x),\psi(\bar x)\) have their free names among \(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}\). Suppose that in every nonempty \(L\)-structure, every assignment satisfying \(\varphi\) satisfies \(\psi\). There is an \(L\)-formula \(\theta(\bar x)\), with free names among that same tuple, such that \(\varphi\models\theta\models\psi\) under assignments and \(\operatorname{sym}(\theta)\subseteq\operatorname{sym}(\varphi)\cap\operatorname{sym}(\psi)\).

Every expansion by \(\bar c\) comes from the tuple of its constant values. The preceding satisfaction identity therefore turns the hypothesis into the sentence consequence \(\varphi^{\bar c}\models\psi^{\bar c}\). Sentence interpolation in \(L[\bar c]\) gives a sentence \(B\) using only symbols shared by these two translated sentences. Decode it to \(\theta=B^{\bar x}\). The satisfaction identities give both required consequences for every assignment.

If an original \(L\)-symbol occurs in \(\theta\), it occurs in \(B\), hence in both \(\varphi^{\bar c}\) and \(\psi^{\bar c}\), and hence in both \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\). Decoding introduces only names from the fixed tuple, so introduces no other free names. This statement does not require a free name to occur in both original formulas; the common-vocabulary condition concerns nonlogical symbols.

An empty common vocabulary still expresses cardinality

Let \(c,d\) be distinct constant symbols and let \(\alpha=\exists x\exists y\,(x\ne y)\). The consequence \(c\ne d\models\alpha\) has interpolant \(\alpha\), although the common nonlogical vocabulary is empty. Moreover, no sentence true in every nonempty structure can be an interpolant for this consequence.

A structure satisfying \(c\ne d\) supplies two witnesses for \(\alpha\), and \(\alpha\models\alpha\). The sentence \(\alpha\) uses only equality. If a valid sentence \(\theta\) implied \(\alpha\), then a singleton structure would satisfy \(\alpha\), which it does not. Thus an empty common vocabulary does not mean the interpolant must be \(\top\) or \(\bot\).

A private function can disappear behind an existence statement

Let \(P,Q\) be distinct unary relation symbols and \(f\) a unary function symbol. The consequence

\[\forall x\,P(f(x))\ \models\ (\exists x\,P(x))\lor(\forall x\,Q(x))\]

has interpolant \(\exists x\,P(x)\), whose only nonlogical symbol is the common symbol \(P\).

Choose an element \(a\) of the nonempty domain. The premise gives \(P(f(a))\), so \(f(a)\) witnesses \(\exists x\,P(x)\). That existence statement implies the displayed disjunction by its left alternative. The premise contains \(f,P\), the conclusion contains \(P,Q\), and the interpolant contains only \(P\). No interpretation of \(Q\) is needed.

Inconsistent premises and valid conclusions have simple interpolants

If \(\varphi\) has no nonempty model, then \(\bot\) is an interpolant for \(\varphi\models\psi\), for every \(\psi\). If \(\psi\) is valid, then \(\top\) is an interpolant for \(\varphi\models\psi\), for every \(\varphi\).

In the first case \(\varphi\models\bot\), and \(\bot\models\psi\) because no structure satisfies \(\bot\). In the second case \(\varphi\models\top\) and \(\top\models\psi\). Both chosen sentences have empty symbol support, so meet every common-vocabulary bound.

Must an interpolant give an equivalent reformulation of the premise?

For \(\forall x\,P(x)\models\exists x\,P(x)\), is the interpolant \(\exists x\,P(x)\) equivalent to the premise? Can the premise itself also serve as an interpolant?

Show answerHide answer

The two sentences are not equivalent. On a two-element domain, interpret \(P\) as a singleton: the existential sentence is true and the universal sentence false. Nevertheless both are interpolants. Nonemptiness makes \(\forall x\,P(x)\models\exists x\,P(x)\); either endpoint therefore lies between them, and both use just the common symbol \(P\). Craig's theorem asserts the existence of a suitable intermediate sentence, not its uniqueness or equivalence to either endpoint.
