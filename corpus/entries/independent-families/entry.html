Independence permits every finite pattern of membership

Let \(X\) be an infinite set. A family \(\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal P(X)\) is independent if, for every pair of disjoint finite subfamilies \(\mathcal E,\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal A\), the set

\[ C(\mathcal E,\mathcal H) =\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal E}A\;\cap\! \bigcap_{B\in\mathcal H}(X\setminus B) \]

is infinite. The intersection of an empty subfamily is \(X\). Thus the empty pattern leaves all of \(X\), and \(\mathcal E=\{A\}\), \(\mathcal H=\varnothing\) asks that \(A\) be infinite. Reversing these choices asks that \(X\setminus A\) be infinite as well.

The two subfamilies must be disjoint: requiring a point both to belong and not to belong to the same set would be impossible. This notion concerns subsets and their complements; it has no probability assumption. Throughout the existence and counting arguments we work in ZFC.

Two sets have four patterns to satisfy

For distinct subsets \(A,B\subseteq X\), the family \(\{A,B\}\) is independent exactly when all four cells

\[ A\cap B,\qquad A\setminus B,\qquad B\setminus A,\qquad X\setminus(A\cup B) \]

are infinite. Necessity follows by choosing both sides for each of the two sets. Conversely, any pattern specifying fewer choices contains one of these four infinite cells.

For example, take \(X=\mathbb N\), \(A=\{4n,4n+1:n\in\mathbb N\}\) and \(B=\{4n,4n+2:n\in\mathbb N\}\). The four cells are respectively the numbers of the forms \(4n\), \(4n+1\), \(4n+2\), and \(4n+3\). Each is infinite, so this family is independent.

Can an infinite partition be independent?

Suppose \(X\) is partitioned into two infinite sets \(A\) and \(B\). Are \(\{A\}\) and \(\{A,B\}\) independent?

Show answerHide answer

The one-member family is independent because both \(A\) and its complement \(B\) are infinite. The two-member family is not: the pattern requiring membership in both has empty intersection. Individually having infinite sides does not guarantee independence of a family.

Finite records provide a countable testing set

Write \([Y]^{<\omega}\) for the family of finite subsets of \(Y\), and set

\[ D=[\mathbb N]^{<\omega}\times [[\mathbb N]^{<\omega}]^{<\omega}. \]

A record \((F,\mathcal K)\in D\) consists of a finite set of natural numbers and a finite family of finite sets of natural numbers. The family \(\mathcal K\) need not consist of subsets of \(F\).

The finite subsets of a countable set form a countable family. Applying this twice, then using countability of a product, shows that \(D\) is countable. The records \((\{n\},\varnothing)\) are distinct for distinct \(n\), so \(D\) is infinite. It therefore admits a bijection with \(\mathbb N\).

A subset is tested by its finite traces

For each \(A\subseteq\mathbb N\), define a subset of records by

\[ X_A=\{(F,\mathcal K)\in D:F\cap A\in\mathcal K\}. \]

A record tests the trace \(F\cap A\) and accepts it exactly when it belongs to the finite list \(\mathcal K\).

The map \(A\mapsto X_A\) is injective. If \(A\ne B\), choose \(n\) belonging to one of them and not the other. The record \((\{n\},\{\{n\}\})\) lies in \(X_A\) exactly when \(n\in A\), and similarly for \(B\). It distinguishes the two record sets.

A finite coordinate set separates the required choices

Let \(\mathcal E,\mathcal H\) be disjoint finite families of subsets of \(\mathbb N\). There is a finite \(F_0\subseteq\mathbb N\) such that, for every finite \(F\supseteq F_0\),

\[ A\cap F\ne B\cap F \quad(A\in\mathcal E,\ B\in\mathcal H). \]

Proof

For each such pair \((A,B)\), the sets differ because the families are disjoint. Choose one point of their symmetric difference. Let \(F_0\) contain these finitely many points. The chosen point for \((A,B)\) remains in every \(F\supseteq F_0\) and belongs to exactly one of \(A\cap F\), \(B\cap F\). If either family is empty, take \(F_0=\varnothing\); there are no pairwise requirements to check. □

Every finite pattern accepts infinitely many records

The family \(\{X_A:A\subseteq\mathbb N\}\) is independent on \(D\).

Proof

By injectivity, disjoint finite subfamilies of the record sets correspond to disjoint finite families \(\mathcal E,\mathcal H\) of their indices. Choose \(F_0\) as in . For any finite \(F\supseteq F_0\), let

\[ \mathcal K_F=\{A\cap F:A\in\mathcal E\}. \]

Then \((F,\mathcal K_F)\) belongs to every \(X_A\) with \(A\in\mathcal E\). It belongs to none of the \(X_B\) with \(B\in\mathcal H\), because no negative trace equals a positive trace.

There are infinitely many possible \(F\): use \(F_0\cup\{n\}\) for \(n\notin F_0\). Different such \(n\) give different first coordinates and hence different records. This proves that the cell for the pattern is infinite. The argument includes empty positive or negative families; when both are empty the cell is all of \(D\). □

An independent family can have continuum many members

There exists an independent family \(\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal P(\mathbb N)\) of cardinality \(\mathfrak c=2^{\aleph_0}\).

Proof

Fix a bijection \(e:\mathbb N\to D\) and put \(I_A=e^{-1}[X_A]\) for every \(A\subseteq\mathbb N\). Preimages preserve intersections and complements, and a bijection preserves infinitude. Thus makes \(\mathcal A=\{I_A:A\subseteq\mathbb N\}\) independent on \(\mathbb N\).

The map \(A\mapsto I_A\) is injective because both \(e\) and \(A\mapsto X_A\) distinguish the relevant sets. It is a bijection from \(\mathcal P(\mathbb N)\) onto \(\mathcal A\), so \(|\mathcal A|=2^{\aleph_0}\). This is the size of the real numbers. No family of subsets of \(\mathbb N\) can have more members. □

A complete assignment of sides generates a proper filter

Let \(\mathcal A\) be independent on \(\mathbb N\). For an arbitrary function \(\varepsilon:\mathcal A\to\{0,1\}\), take the family

\[ \mathcal G_\varepsilon= \{A\in\mathcal A:\varepsilon(A)=1\} \cup\{\mathbb N\setminus A:A\in\mathcal A,\ \varepsilon(A)=0\} \cup\{C\subseteq\mathbb N:C\text{ is cofinite}\}. \]

Every finite intersection of this family is nonempty. Indeed, the finitely many assigned sides have infinite intersection by independence. Intersecting with finitely many cofinite sets removes only finitely many points, leaving an infinite set. Repeated occurrences of the same side do not change the intersection.

The generated filter is therefore proper. By ultrafilter extension, assuming choice, it extends to an ultrafilter \(U_\varepsilon\). This ultrafilter contains the cofinite filter and hence is nonprincipal.

This construction specifies sides only for the members of \(\mathcal A\). The ultrafilter decides all other subsets too; its extension need not be unique.

Different assignments cannot have the same extension

If \(\varepsilon\ne\delta\), no ultrafilter can extend both \(\mathcal G_\varepsilon\) and \(\mathcal G_\delta\).

Proof

Choose \(A\in\mathcal A\) with \(\varepsilon(A)\ne\delta(A)\). One family contains \(A\), while the other contains \(\mathbb N\setminus A\). A proper filter cannot contain both, because their intersection is empty. □

Apply choice to select one extension for each assignment. The map \(\varepsilon\mapsto U_\varepsilon\) is injective, whatever extensions are selected.

There are as many nonprincipal ultrafilters as families of subsets

Assuming choice, the set of nonprincipal ultrafilters on \(\mathbb N\) has cardinality \(2^{\mathfrak c}=2^{2^{\aleph_0}}\).

Proof

Use the continuum-sized independent family in . There are \(2^{\mathfrak c}\) binary-valued assignments to its members, by coding subsets as binary choices. The preceding construction provides that many distinct nonprincipal ultrafilters.

For the reverse bound, every ultrafilter on \(\mathbb N\) is a family of subsets of \(\mathbb N\), hence an element of \(\mathcal P(\mathcal P(\mathbb N))\). This set has cardinality \(2^{2^{\aleph_0}}\). The two bounds give equality. □

Principal ultrafilters contribute only one for each natural number. The count above already attains the maximal possible size using nonprincipal ultrafilters alone.

Can a large almost disjoint family also be independent?

The almost disjoint construction gives continuum many infinite subsets of \(\mathbb N\). Could the same family be independent?

Show answerHide answer

No. Any two distinct members of an almost disjoint family have finite intersection, while independence requires their intersection to be infinite. Both kinds of family can be large, but they require different behaviour of finite intersections. A one-member family has no pairwise almost-disjointness restriction; it is independent exactly when its member and complement are both infinite.

Why is the testing set infinite?

Could a finite set \(X\) support an independent family under the definition used here? How many ultrafilters does a nonempty finite \(X\) have?

Show answerHide answer

No independent family is possible on finite \(X\), since even the empty pattern would require \(X\) to be infinite. Every ultrafilter on finite \(X\) contains the finite set \(X\), so it is concentrated on one point. Different points give different ultrafilters, and therefore there are exactly \(|X|\) ultrafilters. None is nonprincipal.
