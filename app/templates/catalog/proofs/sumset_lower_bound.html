The definition

Let \(G\) be an additive abelian group, and let \(A,B\subseteq G\). The sumset of \(A\) and \(B\) is the set of all sums you can make by choosing one element from each:

\[ A+B=\{a+b\mid a\in A,\ b\in B\}. \]

We count distinct sums, not the pairs that produce them. For instance, \(\{0,1\}+\{0,1\}=\{0,1,2\}\): four pairs, but only three sums.

The statement

If \(A\) and \(B\) are finite, nonempty subsets of an additive abelian group, then

\[ |A+B|\geq\max\{|A|,\,|B|\}. \]

A sumset is at least as large as either of its summands.

The proof

Find a copy inside

Choose any element \(b_0\in B\). Adding this fixed element to every member of \(A\) gives the map

\[ \begin{aligned} f:A&\longrightarrow A+B,\\ a&\longmapsto a+b_0. \end{aligned} \]

Every image belongs to \(A+B\), by the definition of a sumset. And no two elements merge: if \(a+b_0=a'+b_0\), cancellation gives \(a=a'\). So \(f\) is injective, and hence

\[ |A|\leq |A+B|. \]

Now choose \(a_0\in A\) and repeat the argument with \(b\mapsto a_0+b\). This gives \(|B|\leq |A+B|\). Since the sumset is at least as large as each summand, it is at least as large as their maximum. □

Why nonempty?

The proof needs an element to translate by. If \(A=\varnothing\) and \(B=\{0\}\), then \(A+B=\varnothing\), so

\[ |A+B|=0<1=\max\{|A|,|B|\}. \]

A small hypothesis can do essential work.
